# Attention Built the Modern Language Model

The Transformer's central idea is that a sequence can be modeled entirely by letting each token compute a softmax-weighted mix of other tokens' representations, softmax(QK^T / sqrt(d_k)) V, with no recurrence or convolution at all.[1] Introduced in 2017 for machine translation, the design mattered most for what it unlocked: a constant-length path between any two tokens and training that parallelizes across a whole sequence.[1] OpenAI's GPT line took the decoder half, trained it to predict the next token on ever larger corpora, and showed with GPT-3 that a 175-billion-parameter model could perform new tasks from text prompts alone, without gradient updates.[2] Nine years on, the residual skeleton of the original block survives, though nearly every component around attention has been replaced and attention itself has been made cheaper through IO-aware kernels, shared key-value heads, sliding windows, and mixture-of-experts routing.[3][4][5][6][7] The most serious challengers, state-space and linear-recurrent models, have largely been absorbed into hybrids that still keep some full-attention layers, and at least one lab reverted to full attention after a hybrid underperformed on multi-hop reasoning at scale.[8][9] Attention remains the load-bearing mechanism of large language models in 2026, even as its share of layers shrinks.

## Attention began as a patch for a fixed-length bottleneck

Before the Transformer, neural machine translation relied on recurrent encoder-decoder networks that compressed an entire source sentence into one fixed-length vector.[10] Bahdanau, Cho, and Bengio conjectured that this vector was "a bottleneck in improving the performance" of the architecture, noting that quality deteriorated rapidly as input sentences grew longer.[10] Their remedy, presented at ICLR 2015, let the decoder compute a fresh context vector at every output step as a weighted sum of all encoder states, with weights given by a softmax over alignment scores from a small feedforward network, a(s_{i-1}, h_j) = v_a^T tanh(W_a s_{i-1} + U_a h_j).[10] The payoff was concrete: on WMT'14 English-French, their RNNsearch-50 model scored **26.75 BLEU against 17.82** for the equivalent model without attention and showed no deterioration on sentences of 50 or more words.[10]

Luong, Pham, and Manning simplified the recipe later in 2015 with multiplicative scores such as the plain dot product h_t^T h_s, reporting a gain of 5.0 BLEU over a non-attentional baseline and an ensemble state of the art of 25.9 BLEU on WMT'15 English-German.[11] Yet attention was still bolted onto a recurrent network, leaving two deeper problems untouched. Gradient-based learning becomes increasingly difficult as the span of the dependencies to be captured grows, a difficulty formalized in 1994, and vanishing and exploding gradients were still "two widely known issues" in training recurrent networks in 2012.[12][13] Recurrence is also "inherently sequential," precluding parallelization within training examples.[1] Attention had solved the information bottleneck; the Transformer would solve the computational one.

## Scaled dot-product attention buys constant path length at quadratic cost

Vaswani and seven equally credited co-authors generalized attention into a function that maps a query and a set of key-value pairs to a weighted sum of the values, where each weight reflects the query's compatibility with the corresponding key.[1][14] In matrix form, **Attention(Q, K, V) = softmax(QK^T / sqrt(d_k)) V**.[1] The dot product is Luong's, but the scaling factor is new, and the paper's justification is statistical: if query and key components are independent with mean 0 and variance 1, their dot product has variance d_k, so large dimensions push the softmax into saturated regions with extremely small gradients.[1] Dividing by sqrt(d_k) restores unit variance; at the base model's d_k of 64, it shrinks the logits' standard deviation from 8 to 1.[1] Dot-product scoring was preferred to additive scoring because it is "much faster and more space-efficient in practice."[1]

Instead of one full-width attention, the Transformer projects queries, keys, and values eight times into 64-dimensional subspaces, runs attention in each head in parallel, concatenates the results, and projects them back, so that MultiHead(Q, K, V) = Concat(head_1, ..., head_h) W^O.[1] Multiple heads let the model "jointly attend to information from different representation subspaces," counteracting the loss of resolution that comes from averaging over attention-weighted positions, and because h times d_k equals d_model, the total cost is similar to that of a single full-width head.[1][15]

The original model used attention in three distinct ways. Encoder self-attention lets every source position attend to every other.[1] Decoder self-attention is masked so that each position sees only itself and earlier positions, implemented by setting illegal softmax inputs to negative infinity; the Annotated Transformer's reference code uses -1e9 as a practical stand-in.[1][15] Encoder-decoder, or cross, attention takes its queries from the decoder and its keys and values from the encoder output, mimicking the alignment of earlier sequence-to-sequence models.[1] The causal mask proved the most consequential, because it lets an entire target sequence be trained in parallel while preserving left-to-right generation, the property decoder-only language models exploit.

The complexity analysis lays out the trade. A self-attention layer costs **O(n²·d)** for sequence length n and dimension d, but it requires only O(1) sequential operations and offers an O(1) maximum path length between any two positions; a recurrent layer costs O(n·d²) with O(n) sequential steps and an O(n) path.[1] Self-attention is therefore cheaper whenever n is smaller than d, which held for 2017-era sentence-level translation.[1] At modern context lengths of hundreds of thousands of tokens, the n² term produced by the n-by-n score matrix QK^T dominates, motivating most of the efficiency research described below.

## The 2017 block wrapped attention in residuals, feed-forward layers, and sinusoids

Each of the six encoder layers pairs multi-head self-attention with a position-wise feed-forward network, FFN(x) = max(0, xW1 + b1)W2 + b2, whose inner dimension of 2,048 is four times the model dimension of 512; decoder layers insert cross-attention between the two.[1] Every sub-layer is wrapped as LayerNorm(x + Sublayer(x)), the arrangement now called post-LN, and the input embeddings share a weight matrix with the pre-softmax output projection.[1] Simple arithmetic on those dimensions shows that each feed-forward block holds about 2.1 million weights against about 1.05 million for the four attention projections, so most of each layer's parameters sit outside attention.[1] Because attention by itself ignores token order, the authors added fixed sinusoidal positional encodings to the input embeddings, hoping they would let the model attend by relative position and extrapolate beyond training lengths, and they found that learned position embeddings performed nearly identically.[1]

The big model reached **28.4 BLEU on WMT 2014 English-German**, more than 2 BLEU above the previous best results including ensembles, and a single-model state of the art of **41.8 BLEU on English-French** after 3.5 days of training on eight GPUs, "a small fraction of the training costs" of competing models.[1] The base model trained in 12 hours on eight NVIDIA P100 GPUs.[1] According to Wikipedia, the paper had been cited more than 250,000 times as of 2026, placing it among the ten most-cited papers of the 21st century.[14]

## Attention masks split one architecture into three families

Within about two years, the Transformer had split into three lineages defined largely by which stack they kept and how they masked attention. BERT kept only the encoder, with fully bidirectional attention, and trained it by masked language modeling on 15% of tokens plus a next-sentence objective; it raised the GLUE score to 80.5%, a 7.7-point absolute improvement.[16] OpenAI's GPT-1 kept only a 12-layer decoder with masked self-attention, 768-dimensional states, and learned position embeddings, pre-trained it with left-to-right language modeling on BooksCorpus, and then fine-tuned it to improve the state of the art on 9 of the 12 tasks studied.[17][18] BERT's authors sized their base model to match GPT and named the critical difference: GPT's tokens can attend only to context on their left.[16] T5 and BART retained the full encoder-decoder. T5 recast every task as text-to-text, pre-trained with span corruption on the 750 GB C4 corpus, and found in a controlled comparison that an encoder-decoder trained with a denoising objective outperformed decoder-only and prefix language-model variants.[19] BART paired a bidirectional encoder with an autoregressive decoder, explicitly generalizing BERT and GPT, gaining up to 6 ROUGE in abstractive summarization.[20] Objectives tracked the masks: bidirectional models need corruption-based training, while causal models learn from plain next-token prediction. T5's result makes the later convergence on decoder-only designs for the largest generative models notable, and the evidence gathered here does not settle why it happened.

## Modern blocks keep the skeleton but replace nearly every part

Meta's LLaMA (2023) shows how much changed while the residual skeleton stayed in place. Its paper lists three borrowed refinements: pre-normalization using RMSNorm, SwiGLU feed-forward layers, and rotary position embeddings applied at every layer in place of absolute positions.[3] Xiong and colleagues used mean field theory to show that the original post-LN placement produces large expected gradients near the output layer at initialization, which is why learning-rate warmup had been necessary; moving normalization inside the residual branch, known as pre-LN, yields well-behaved gradients and matched post-LN baselines without warmup.[21] GPT-3 had already inherited pre-normalization from GPT-2.[2] RMSNorm drops LayerNorm's mean subtraction and rescales by root mean square alone; Zhang and Sennrich reported comparable quality with running-time reductions of 7% to 64%.[22] SwiGLU replaces the ReLU feed-forward with a gated product, (Swish(xW) ⊗ xV)W2, and Shazeer shrank the hidden dimension by a factor of two-thirds to hold the parameter count constant; on T5-style pre-training it lowered held-out log-perplexity from 1.677 to 1.636, a success he attributed, with no further explanation, "to divine benevolence."[23] LLaMA adopts the same 2/3 · 4d hidden size, and it still used 2,000 warmup steps despite pre-normalization.[3]

Position handling moved inside attention. RoPE rotates query and key vectors by position-dependent angles so that their dot product depends on relative distance.[24] ALiBi skips position embeddings entirely and adds a fixed, head-specific penalty proportional to query-key distance to the attention scores; a 1.3-billion-parameter model trained on 1,024-token inputs matched the perplexity of a sinusoidal model trained on 2,048 tokens when both were evaluated at 2,048, while training 11% faster.[25] The same study found that sinusoidal encodings fail to extrapolate more than a few dozen tokens beyond their training length, an empirical rebuttal of the original paper's hope.[25] The combined recipe paid off: **LLaMA-13B outperformed the 175-billion-parameter GPT-3 on most benchmarks** while training exclusively on publicly available data.[3]

## Scale, not novelty, made the decoder the LLM template

The decisive move toward LLMs was empirical rather than architectural. GPT-2, announced in February 2019, had 1.5 billion parameters trained simply to predict the next word on 40 GB of text from about 8 million web pages, and OpenAI initially withheld the full model over misuse concerns.[26] GPT-3 kept the GPT-2 design, adding alternating dense and locally banded sparse attention, and scaled it to **175 billion parameters**, ten times more than any previous non-sparse language model, across 96 layers with a 2,048-token context.[2] Its headline finding was that scale "greatly improves task-agnostic, few-shot performance," with tasks and demonstrations "specified purely via text interaction with the model."[2]

Scaling laws explained why bigger kept working. Kaplan and colleagues found in 2020 that loss follows smooth power laws in parameters, data, and compute, with weak dependence on width or depth, and recommended putting most added compute into model size, with optimal parameter count growing roughly as compute to the power 0.73.[27] DeepMind's Chinchilla study revised that advice in 2022 after training more than 400 models, concluding that parameters and training tokens should be scaled equally and that existing large models were "significantly undertrained."[28] The 70-billion-parameter Chinchilla, trained with the same compute as the 280-billion-parameter Gopher but on four times more data, outperformed it uniformly and reached 67.5% on MMLU.[28] The often-quoted ratio of about 20 training tokens per parameter is a commonly cited rule of thumb derived from these results rather than a figure stated in the paper's abstract.[28] DeepSeek-V3's 14.8-trillion-token pre-training run reflects the shift.[7]

The final step made these models usable as assistants: InstructGPT combined supervised fine-tuning on human-written demonstrations, a reward model trained on human rankings of outputs, and reinforcement learning with PPO, and labelers preferred outputs from the **1.3-billion-parameter InstructGPT to those of the 175-billion-parameter GPT-3**.[29] By 2022 the template was set: a decoder-only Transformer, next-token prediction on vast unlabeled text, scale, and alignment post-training.

## Induction heads tie attention to in-context learning

Interpretability research has since offered a mechanistic account of how this template learns from prompts. A 2019 debate asked whether attention weights explain predictions at all; Jain and Wallace showed that learned attention weights often fail to correlate with gradient-based measures of importance and that very different attention distributions can yield equivalent predictions.[30] Wiegreffe and Pinter replied that attention offers plausible but not necessarily faithful explanations.[31] Anthropic's 2022 circuits work instead examined what heads compute. Olsson and colleagues identified **induction heads**, a two-head circuit in which a previous-token head enables a later head to find an earlier occurrence of the current token and copy what followed it, completing patterns of the form [A][B] … [A] → [B].[32] These heads form in a sharp phase change early in training in multi-layer models of every size, coinciding with a jump in in-context learning and a visible bump in the training loss.[32] From six lines of evidence, they argued that induction heads "might constitute the mechanism for the actual majority of all in-context learning" in large Transformers, while acknowledging that their evidence for large models is indirect.[32] The finding acquired practical weight in 2025, when MiniMax attributed long-context degradation in an experimental sliding-window hybrid to retrieval and induction heads that form early in pre-training and resist later change.[9]

## Efficiency work cheapened attention instead of abandoning it

Two costs dominate deployment: quadratic attention over long sequences, and decoding constrained by memory bandwidth. FlashAttention, from Dao and colleagues in 2022, keeps attention mathematically exact but tiles the computation to reduce reads and writes between GPU high-bandwidth memory and on-chip SRAM, yielding a **3x speedup on GPT-2** at a 1K sequence length.[4] During generation, a KV cache stores the keys and values of every processed token so that each step computes them only for the new token, but the cache grows with layers, key-value heads, head dimension, and sequence length; one vendor explainer calculates 20 GiB for a single 131,072-token sequence in a 24-billion-parameter model.[33] Shazeer's multi-query attention attacked this cost in 2019 by sharing one set of keys and values across all query heads, decoding much faster with only minor quality loss.[34] Grouped-query attention generalized the idea with an intermediate number of key-value heads, achieving quality close to full multi-head attention at speed comparable to multi-query attention, and existing checkpoints can be uptrained to it with about 5% of the original pre-training compute.[5] DeepSeek-V3 adopts yet another variant, multi-head latent attention.[7]

A second family limits what each layer attends to. Longformer combined local windowed attention with task-specific global attention so that cost scales linearly with sequence length.[35] Mistral 7B paired grouped-query attention with a 4,096-token sliding window whose stacked layers give a theoretical span of about 131K tokens and used a rolling buffer that cut cache memory eightfold at 32K tokens.[6] A 2026 blog survey reports newer models alternating full and windowed layers, among them gpt-oss-20b with 128-token windows in half its layers and Gemma 3 with five windowed layers for each full one.[8] Mixture-of-experts instead cuts per-token compute in the feed-forward layers. Switch Transformers achieved up to a 7x pre-training speedup over T5 at equal compute, Mixtral 8x7B routes each token to two of eight experts for about 13 billion active out of roughly 47 billion total parameters, and DeepSeek-V3 activates **37 billion of its 671 billion parameters** per token and required 2.788 million H800 GPU hours for full training.[36][37][7]

## Challengers ended up as hybrids that keep some attention

Mamba, from Gu and Dao in late 2023, made state-space parameters input-dependent to recover content-based reasoning, dropped attention and MLP blocks entirely, and reported about 5x higher inference throughput than Transformers, with a 3-billion-parameter model matching Transformers twice its size.[38] RWKV combined parallelizable training with the constant per-token memory of recurrent inference and scaled to 14 billion parameters, performing on par with similarly sized Transformers.[39] Neither displaced the Transformer in the sources reviewed; the field converged on hybrids instead. AI21's Jamba interleaved one attention layer for every seven Mamba layers with mixture-of-experts, fitting a 52-billion-parameter model on one 80 GB GPU with strong results up to 256K tokens.[40] NVIDIA's Nemotron-H replaced most self-attention layers with Mamba layers and reported accuracy on par with or better than similarly sized Qwen-2.5 and Llama-3.1 models at up to 3x faster inference.[41] IBM claims its hybrid Granite 4.0 models cut production RAM use by more than 70%.[42] A single 2026 blog survey, not independently verified here, reports full attention in a quarter or fewer of the layers of models such as Qwen3-Next, Kimi Linear, and MiniMax-Text-01, with similar patterns in 2026 releases including Kimi K3 and Gemma 4.[8]

The counter-trend is instructive: MiniMax reported in October 2025 that a hybrid of linear and full attention looked as good as pure full attention at small scale but showed "clear deficits in complex, multi-hop reasoning tasks" at larger scale, and that infrastructure for linear and sparse attention remained much less mature.[9] Its M2 model kept full attention in all 62 layers, and in MiniMax's own comparison full attention scored 90 against 72 on a 128K-token extraction task on which the two versions tied at 32K.[43] As of late 2026, documented open frontier models such as DeepSeek-V3 and MiniMax M2 remain decoder-only Transformers with mixture-of-experts layers, while closed labs do not publish their architectures, so their status cannot be confirmed.[7][43]

## Conclusion

Every successful refinement since 2017 has either left the attention computation mathematically intact, reduced redundancy in its keys and values, or confined it to fewer layers, and the most ambitious replacements have succeeded only when a minority of full-attention layers stayed in place. The induction-head research and MiniMax's reversal point toward a plausible explanation: exact retrieval and copying across an entire context, the operation that compressed-state layers approximate least well, appears to be what in-context learning depends on. That remains a hypothesis, but it reframes the next phase. The contest is no longer Transformer versus alternative; it is how few full-attention layers a model can keep, and at what context lengths, before reasoning degrades, a question that evaluation, which MiniMax called "the real bottleneck," is not yet equipped to answer cleanly.[9]

A second lesson concerns which of the original bets aged well. The explicit hopes of 2017 fared poorly: sinusoidal encodings did not extrapolate, post-LN needed the warmup that pre-LN later made optional, and T5 found encoder-decoder models stronger than decoder-only ones in a controlled comparison.[25][21][19] The incidental choices proved durable instead, from the causal mask and the residual block to the fourfold feed-forward width that SwiGLU designs still size themselves against.[1][23][3] The architecture won less through foresight than because its simplest parts scaled.
