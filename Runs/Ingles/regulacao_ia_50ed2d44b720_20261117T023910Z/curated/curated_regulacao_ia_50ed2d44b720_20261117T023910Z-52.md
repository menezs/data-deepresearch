# The EU AI Act timeline

Follow the regulation from publication through its phased application. Dates reflect the current framework, including the AI Omnibus changes in force from July 2026.

1. 12 July 2024## Published in the Official JournalRegulation (EU) 2024/1689 was published, beginning the countdown to entry into force.
2. 1 August 2024## The AI Act entered into forceThe regulation became EU law, with its obligations scheduled to apply in phases.
3. 2 February 2025## First rules began to applyThe AI-system definition and prohibited-practice framework entered into application.
4. 2 August 2025## Governance and GPAI obligationsEU and national governance provisions and obligations for general-purpose AI models began to apply.
5. 27 July 2026## AI Omnibus entered into forceThe amending regulation extended the high-risk implementation schedule and introduced targeted simplifications.
6. 2 August 2026## General applicationMost remaining provisions, including the Article 50 transparency framework, apply from this date.
7. 2 December 2026## Two new prohibitions and the legacy marking deadlineArticle 5(1)(ba) and (bb), inserted by the AI Omnibus, prohibit specified non-consensual intimate imagery and child sexual abuse material: taking the Article 5 list to ten. Generative systems already on the market must also carry machine-readable marking of synthetic output by this date.
8. 2 August 2027## Existing GPAI model transition endsProviders of GPAI models placed on the market before 2 August 2025 must meet the applicable obligations.
9. 2 December 2027## Annex III high-risk systemsHigh-risk requirements begin applying to listed sensitive uses such as employment, education, essential services and biometrics.
10. 2 August 2028## AI embedded in regulated productsHigh-risk requirements apply to AI systems integrated into products such as machinery, toys and lifts.
11. 2 August 2030## Legacy large-scale IT systems must complyAI components of Union large-scale IT systems in the area of freedom, security and justice, placed on the market before 2 August 2027, must be brought into compliance.

Source: Regulation (EU) 2024/1689 and European Commission AI Act implementation updates. Always verify dates against the current official text before legal reliance.

This code of practice supports compliance with the AI Act transparency obligations related to marking and labelling of AI-generated content.

The obligations under Article 50 of the AI Act (transparency obligations for providers and deployers of generative AI systems) address risks of deception and manipulation, fostering the integrity of the information ecosystem. These transparency obligations, applicable from 2 August 2026, complement other rules like those for high-risk AI systems or general-purpose AI models. They pertain to marking and detection of AI-generated content and labelling of deepfakes and certain AI-generated publications.

The Code of Practice on Transparency of AI-generated Content was drawn up by independent experts in a multi-stakeholder process facilitated by the AI Office. It helps providers and deployers of generative AI systems to comply with the AI Act’s obligations for labelling and marking of AI-generated content – Article 50(2), (4) and (5) of the AI Act. Even though adherence to the code is voluntary, the transparency requirements under article 50 of the AI Act are legal obligations.

The Commission and the AI Board have confirmed that the code is an adequate voluntary tool to demonstrate compliance with the AI Act transparency obligations.

Below you can find further information about the code and its drawing up process.


The Code of Practice on transparency of AI-generated content (PDF) has **2 sections**:

- **Section 1: Providers** - Rules for marking and detection of AI-generated and manipulated content
- **Section 2: Deployers** - Rules for labelling of deepfakes and AI-generated and manipulated text

The EU has also created a **set of icons** that deployers of generative AI systems may use to label their AI-generated content.

The code is complemented by the guidelines on the scope of the transparency obligations laid down in Article 50 of the AI Act.

Following a positive assessment of the code, all providers and deployers who sign it can rely on its measures to demonstrate compliance with the AI Act’s rules for labeling and detection of AI-generated content, deepfakes and certain text publications. This will reduce their administrative burden and give them predictability, legal certainty and trust across all Member States.

## Working groups

The drafting of the code was centered around 2 working groups, following the structure of the transparency obligations for AI generated content in Article 50. Each working group was led by independent chairs and vice-chairs. You can download the full list of participants of working groups 1&2(PDF).

### Working group 1: Providers

Focused on obligations, requiring providers of generative AI systems to ensure:

- Outputs of AI systems (audio, image, video, text) are marked in a machine-readable format and detectable as artificially generated or manipulated.
- The employed technical solutions are effective, interoperable, robust, and reliable as far as technically feasible. These take into account the specificities and limitations of various types of content, the costs of implementation and the generally acknowledged state of the art, as may be reflected in relevant technical standards.

### Working group 2: Deployers

Focused on obligations, requiring deployers of generative AI systems to disclose:

- Content that is artificially generated or manipulated, constituting a deepfake (image, audio, or video which resembles existing persons, objects, places, entities or events and would falsely appear to a person to be authentic or truthful).
- AI generated/manipulated text publications informing the public on matters of public interest, unless the publication has undergone a process of human review and is subject to editorial responsibility.

Both groups also considered cross-cutting issues, including horizontal requirements for the information provided to the natural persons under Article 50(5) and cooperated with relevant actors across the value chain.

**The “Digital Omnibus on AI” Regulation introduces crucial AI Act amendments by postponing application of the high-risk AI rules and more broadly simplifying its administrative framework.**

Regulation (EU) 2026/1744 of the European Parliament and of the Council, adopted on 8 July 2026 (“Digital Omnibus on AI” or “Regulation”), was published in the Official Journal of the European Union on 24 July 2026.

The Digital Omnibus on AI forms part of the EU’s Omnibus legislative packages to reduce administrative burdens and enhance competitiveness. It accompanies the forthcoming “Digital Omnibus” Regulation amending the GDPR, ePrivacy, the Data Act and other Acts.

The Digital Omnibus on AI introduces targeted amendments and simplifications to the AI Act, without, however, changing its structure and risk-based approach. Given the imminence of the 2 August 2026 deadline for the application of high-risk AI systems’ provisions, the Digital Omnibus on AI entered into force only three days after publication, on 27 July 2026, rather than after the standard twenty-day period.

The Digital Omnibus on AI amends the AI Act’s implementation timeline, principally by postponing the rules governing high-risk AI systems. Article 6 sets the classification rules for systems qualifying as high-risk. The original dates were 2 August 2026 for stand-alone high-risk AI systems under Article 6(2) and Annex III, and 2 August 2027 for high-risk systems embedded as safety components under Article 6(1) and Annex I. Delayed standards, common specifications, alternative guidance and the establishment of national competent authorities would have made those dates disproportionately costly. Article 50 of the AI Act lays down transparency obligations for providers and deployers of certain AI systems. The Digital Omnibus on AI introduces no new transparency obligations and does not substantively alter the existing requirements, which continue to include informing users when they interact with an AI system, labelling AI-generated or AI-manipulated content, and disclosing the use of emotion-recognition systems or synthetic content (deepfakes).

The key change concerns the application date of the Article 50(2) provider duty to apply machine-readable marking to AI-generated or manipulated content, including synthetic and manipulated audiovisual material (deepfakes).

## Drafting process

The code was drafted by eligible stakeholders who replied to a public call. Within this group were providers of specific generative AI systems, developers of marking and detection techniques, associations of deployers of generative AI systems, civil society organisations, academic experts, and specialised organisation with expertise in transparency and very large online platforms.

In its role as a facilitator, the AI Office also invited international and European observers to join the drawing up of code of practice. These organisations did not meet the eligibility criteria of the call, but contributed with their expertise. All participants and observers were invited to take part in plenary sessions, working group meetings, and thematic workshops dedicated to discussing technical aspects of the code.

The drafting process considered:

- The feedback obtained through the consultation on transparency requirements for certain AI systems
- Expert studies commissioned by the AI Office

The key change concerns the application date of the Article 50(2) provider duty to apply machine-readable marking to AI-generated or manipulated content, including synthetic and manipulated audiovisual material (deepfakes).

New Article 111(4) of the AI Act, inserted by the Digital Omnibus on AI, requires providers of systems placed on the market before 2 August 2026 to comply with Article 50(2) by 2 December 2026 (the four-month transition under Recital 38). This grace period applies only to those existing systems. Systems placed on the market after 2 August 2026 must comply upon placement because Article 50’s general application date remains 2 August 2026 under Article 113, as amended.

Under the AI Act’s risk-based classification, Article 5 designates certain practices as prohibited because they are considered dangerous to fundamental rights, democracy or human dignity that they are banned outright in the EU.

The Digital Omnibus on AI amends Article 5 by adding the following two categories to the prohibited practices already in place:

The Regulation differentiates the prohibition for providers from that for deployers. Providers may not place systems on the market where designed for such purposes, or where reasonably and foreseeably usable for them without adequate technical safeguards. Deployers may not use systems to create or manipulate such material.

Both new prohibitions take effect for providers and deployers from 2 December 2026. The existing prohibited practices under Article 5 have been in force since 2 February 2025.

The Digital Omnibus on AI narrows the definition of “safety component” in Article 3(14), which informs high-risk classification. Under new Article 6(1a), AI systems used solely for non-safety-related user assistance, performance optimisation, service efficiency, automation, convenience or quality control are not safety components and therefore are not high-risk on that basis. Under new Article 6(1b), however, systems whose failure or malfunctioning would endanger health and safety remain safety components. This keeps classification aligned with the AI Act’s risk-based approach.

Article 4 required providers and deployers to take measures, to the greatest extent possible, to ensure adequate “AI literacy” among staff and others operating or using AI systems on their behalf. The duty covered appropriate knowledge, skills and understanding of AI capabilities, risks and potential effects, taking account of the technical background, experience and role of the persons concerned.
