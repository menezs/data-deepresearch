## Abstract

### Importance

Concerns about adolescent smartphone use and mental health have prompted many schools to implement mobile phone bans, but evidence of their effects remains mixed.

### Objective

To examine whether schoolwide mobile phone bans were associated with changes in mental health and associated social and behavioral outcomes among adolescents in Iceland.

### Design, Setting, and Participants

This repeated cross-sectional study used difference-in-differences analyses of data from 70 schools across 3 waves of the nationwide Youth in Iceland school survey. The surveys were administered to adolescents aged 13 to 15 years in February 2016, February 2018, and November 2023. Analyses were conducted from February 1, 2025, to January 30, 2026.

### Exposure

Schoolwide mobile phone bans prohibiting student phone use during school hours. Between 2018 and 2023, 22 of 70 schools implemented a phone ban.

### Main Outcomes and Measures

Primary outcomes were depressive symptoms, anxiety, hostility, and mental well-being. Secondary outcomes were perceived peer support, screen use (social media use, gaming alone, gaming with friends, and video watching), and bullying (in person and cyberbullying).

### Results

Among 23 938 adolescent observations across the 3 study years, 11 964 (50.0%) were boys; the mean (SD) age was 13.98 (0.82) years. In the postpolicy period from 2018 to 2023, schoolwide mobile phone bans were not associated with meaningful changes in depressive symptoms (β, 0.65 [95% CI, 0.01-1.30]), anxiety (β, 0.21 [95% CI, −0.08 to 0.50]), hostility (β, 0.23 [95% CI, −0.12 to 0.59]), and mental well-being (β, −0.52 [95% CI, −1.08 to 0.03]). Results were similar in gender-stratified analyses and for the secondary outcomes. Sensitivity analyses examining timing of implementation and more stringent phone restriction criteria were consistent with the main findings.

### Conclusions and Relevance

In this repeated cross-sectional study of adolescents in Iceland, schoolwide mobile phone bans were not associated with meaningful changes in adolescent mental health or related social and behavioral outcomes. These findings suggest that school phone bans implemented in isolation may be insufficient to improve adolescent mental health at the population level.

## Discussion

With school phone restrictions gaining popularity worldwide, this repeated cross-sectional population-based study of Icelandic adolescents using DiD analyses adds to the much-needed information on their potential effect on adolescent mental health. We found that schoolwide phone bans were not associated with meaningful changes in mental health or related social and behavioral outcomes. Findings were broadly consistent across the full sample, gender-stratified analyses, sensitivity analyses examining time since implementation, and analyses using a more stringent definition of phone bans.

Prior large-scale studies have yielded mixed results regarding the association between school phone bans and adolescent mental health.<sup>16,17,18,19</sup> Studies relying on population-based self-reported symptoms and well-being have generally found small or null associations,<sup>16,17</sup> whereas studies using other outcomes or policy contexts have reported more favorable findings.<sup>18,19</sup> One proposed mechanism is that restricting phones during the school day may improve the school social environment by reducing digital distraction and creating more opportunities for face-to-face interaction, peer engagement, and school connectedness. However, perceived peer social support was unchanged in our study, providing little evidence that phone bans meaningfully altered this aspect of the school social environment. Another possible pathway is reduced phone exposure during school. We found no change in self-reported screen use, although we assessed typical daily use rather than in-school use and did not have device-level data. However, phone use in many schools was already restricted during classroom instruction, potentially limiting the additional reduction produced by a formal ban. Together, the absence of changes in both perceived peer support and the lack of evidence for reductions in overall self-reported screen use may help explain why we did not observe population-level improvements in mental health.

Research on school phone restrictions has focused mainly on academic outcomes, with mixed findings.<sup>13,15</sup> Far fewer population-based or nationally representative studies have examined mental health or related behavioral outcomes. In a UK study, phone restriction policies were not associated with differences in mental health, sleep, physical activity, academic attainment, disruptive classroom behavior, or daily social media use among adolescents aged 12 to 15 years.<sup>16</sup> In contrast, a smaller study from South Australia evaluating a state-mandated phone ban introduced in 2023 reported small but significant reductions in psychological distress, including symptoms of depression and anxiety, and in negative affect.<sup>17</sup> A recent study from Norway reported that smartphone bans were associated with reduced bullying, reduced mental health care use among girls, and some improvements in girls’ academic outcomes, particularly among those from lower socioeconomic backgrounds.<sup>18</sup> A large, multistate US study of lockable phone pouches found null effects on academic outcomes and no improvement in perceived online bullying.<sup>19</sup> Other studies based on smaller or nonrepresentative samples have also reported inconsistent findings.<sup>13</sup>

In this repeated cross-sectional study using difference-in-differences (DiD) analyses, we used population-based survey data to examine whether schoolwide phone bans were associated with changes in adolescent mental health and associated social and behavioral outcomes (perceived peer support, bullying, and daily screen use) among adolescents in Iceland aged 13 to 15 years. In Iceland, school phone use policies have been adopted at the school level rather than under a national mandate, with notable variation in timing and stringency across schools. We analyzed data collected in 2016, 2018, and 2023, allowing comparison of prepolicy and postpolicy outcome trends in schools that did and did not implement phone bans. To our knowledge, this study is the first to use multiple prepolicy waves of nationwide self-reported survey data to estimate school-level DiD associations between phone ban adoption and adolescent mental health, mental well-being, perceived peer support, different types of screen use, and bullying.

We also found no evidence of meaningful differences in the experience of cyberbullying or in-person bullying in schools with and without phone bans. This is likely due to online and offline bullying being closely intertwined and often involving the same individuals,<sup>25,26</sup> and altering the broader relational and social processes underlying peer aggression likely requires more than limiting device access during school hours. Prior studies on phone bans and bullying have produced mixed findings, with some reporting no association, some modest reductions, and others potential increases.<sup>13</sup> This variability highlights that the effects of phone bans on peer relations may be context dependent and influenced by other factors, such as implementation, enforcement, school climate, and broader social dynamics.<sup>27</sup> This heterogeneity suggests that ongoing monitoring of in-person bullying and cyberbullying is warranted when such policies are introduced to identify unintended consequences and ensure that supportive measures are in place.

Overall, these findings suggest that school phone bans implemented alone may be insufficient to meaningfully improve adolescent mental health or related social and behavioral outcomes at the population level. However, the absence of changes in these outcomes does not preclude benefits for more proximal classroom-level outcomes, such as student attention, classroom engagement, instructional time, teacher workload, or classroom climate, which were not assessed in this study. Although such policies may be attractive because they are visible and relatively simple to implement and may offer small economic benefits,<sup>28</sup> adolescent mental health and bullying are multifactorial phenomena and unlikely to be substantially shifted by a single school rule in isolation. If schools choose to restrict phone use, such policies may be more effective when embedded within broader strategies, including evidence-based antibullying programs, digital citizenship education, supports for healthy peer interaction, and attention to implementation quality and student needs.<sup>28,29,30,31</sup>

## Introduction

Among countries in the Organisation for Economic Co-operation and Development, an estimated 98% of adolescents aged 15 years owned a smartphone in 2022, and about half reported digital device use of at least 30 hours per week (Figure 1).<sup>1</sup> Screen use differs by gender, with girls spending more time on social media and boys spending more time gaming.<sup>2,3</sup> During the same period, adolescent mental health problems have increased globally,<sup>4</sup> including in Iceland.<sup>5,6</sup> Although associations between screen use and mental health are generally small and mixed, evidence suggests that some forms of screen use may be linked to poorer mental health, particularly among girls.<sup>7,8,9</sup>

Recent data show that adolescents spend more than 1 hour per day on smartphones during school hours, with social media accounting for the largest use.<sup>10,11</sup> In response, schools in many countries have introduced phone restrictions or bans in the hope of improving adolescent well-being and academic outcomes.<sup>12,13</sup> These policies typically restrict students’ access to or use of phones during school hours. By reducing digital distraction during the school day, such restrictions may improve the school social environment by creating more opportunities for face-to-face interaction, peer engagement, and school connectedness.<sup>14</sup> However, their implementation has outpaced the evidence supporting their effectiveness.<sup>13,15</sup> Clarifying whether school phone bans are associated with improvements in adolescent mental health and related behaviors is therefore important for policy and practice.

### Strengths and Limitations

This study has several strengths and limitations. A key strength is the prepolicy and postpolicy comparison within a population-based, repeated cross-sectional design. The use of adolescent self-report is both a strength and potential limitation. Self-reports provide valuable information on mental health and experiences not captured in administrative or clinical records<sup>32</sup> but do not allow inference about clinical levels of disorder, and self-reported screen use is only weakly correlated with objectively measured use.<sup>33</sup> Similarly, mental health symptoms were assessed during the preceding week, which may have limited sensitivity to longer-term changes, although a short recall period may reduce retrospective recall demands and is commonly used in validated pediatric measures.<sup>34</sup>

Phone ban adoption was not random, and we did not systematically assess the schools’ reasons for implementing these policies. Schools that later adopted bans differed from comparison schools on some baseline characteristics and outcomes, although school fixed effects account for stable between-school differences and models adjusted for grade, household composition, and language spoken at home. However, some differential prepolicy trends were also observed, including for depressive symptoms and mental well-being among girls and respondents who reported gaming alone in the full sample. Although there was no consistent pattern of prepolicy divergence across outcomes, these differential trends warrant cautious interpretation of postpolicy estimates. Unmeasured time-varying differences between adopting and nonadopting schools may also remain, including differential effects of broader changes in adolescent mental health and digital-media use associated with the COVID-19 pandemic. Timing-based sensitivity analyses also produced imprecise estimates with wide 95% CIs at longer leads and lags, reflecting limited statistical power to detect small or delayed associations. Although phone ban status was based on formal school policies and direct confirmation from schools, we did not directly assess implementation fidelity, enforcement, or student compliance, which may have varied across schools and over time. We also lacked objective measures of in-school smartphone use and therefore could not determine whether bans reduced actual phone use. Finally, analyses were restricted to schools participating in all 3 waves, and the sample was not fully nationally representative by geographic residency. However, included and excluded schools were similar on observed sociodemographic characteristics, and the sample comprised approximately 60% of the national population and was broadly representative by age and gender.

### Participants and Study Design

We analyzed 3 Youth in Iceland survey waves administered during regular class time in February 2016, February 2018, and November 2023 to students aged 13 to 15 years in 70 schools. The 2016 and 2018 waves served as prepolicy observations and the 2023 wave served as the postpolicy observation, with this interval selected to allow sufficient time for ban implementation and potential effects to emerge. In 2016 and 2018, students completed paper questionnaires returned in sealed envelopes. In 2023, the survey was completed electronically. Responses were anonymous in all waves.

In this study, the analytic sample was restricted to schools participating in all 3 survey waves. The sample was broadly representative of the national adolescent population by age and gender, although adolescents residing in the capital region were overrepresented relative to national benchmarks (eFigures 1 and 2 in Supplement 1), consistent with the greater retention of capital-region schools in the analytic sample noted above.

There is no national mandate on smartphone use in Icelandic primary or lower secondary schools. Decisions on phone policies are made at the school level. School phone use policies were obtained from school websites and supplemented by structured telephone interviews with staff knowledgeable about policies that apply to students in grades 8 to 10. Information was collected on whether a ban was in place, year of implementation, specific rules, and enforcement; however, schools’ reasons for adopting phone ban policies were not systematically collected. Schools classified as having a phone ban explicitly prohibited student phone use during school hours, with students being required to leave phones at home or store them in lockers or schoolbags. Enforcement typically involved recording infractions in school communication platforms visible to parents, with repeated violations leading to parent meetings. By the 2023 survey, 22 of the 70 schools in the analytic sample had implemented a phone ban, with most bans introduced between 2019 (n = 6) and 2023 (n = 7). Schools without bans had fewer formal restrictions, often prohibiting phone use during lessons but allowing use during breaks and generally permitting students to carry phones during the school day.

This assumption would be supported by the absence of systematic differential prepolicy changes between schools that later adopted bans and those that did not. To examine this, we estimated (1) a prepolicy comparison between 2016 and 2018 and (2) the main postpolicy contrast between 2018 and 2023. For continuous outcomes, estimates are reported as unstandardized mean differences with 95% CIs. For binary outcomes, models were estimated on the log-odds scale, with odds ratios and 95% CIs presented for interpretation.

In the main analyses, we compared all schools with and without a phone ban. In sensitivity analyses, we incorporated the year of ban implementation to examine whether associations differed according to how long the ban had been in place. Most schools implemented bans between 2019 and 2023, resulting in limited long-term follow-up for early adopters. Duration analyses were therefore limited to 3 years. Additionally, we ran sensitivity analyses restricted to schools in which phones needed to be stored at home, in lockers, or in schoolbags, excluding schools in which students were allowed to keep phones on their person.

### Statistical Analysis

Data were analyzed from February 1, 2025, to January 30, 2026. Statistical analyses were conducted using R, version 4.4.2 (R Project for Statistical Computing). We evaluated associations between schoolwide phone bans and adolescent outcomes using a DiD framework with school and survey year as fixed effects.<sup>23</sup> Additional details about the study design and the statistical and sensitivity analyses are provided in the eMethods in Supplement 1. Because we used repeated cross-sectional data rather than longitudinal observations of the same individuals, the DiD estimates compared changes in school-level outcome trends over time, as captured through repeated individual responses within schools rather than within-person change.

For each outcome, we fit models of the form using the following equation:

| *Y<sub>ist</sub>* = β<sub>0</sub> + β<sub>1</sub> (Post*<sub>t</sub>* × Treat*<sub>s</sub>* ) + γ*<sub>s</sub>* + δ*<sub>t</sub>* +*X<sub>ist</sub>* θ + ε*<sub>ist</sub>* | 

where *i* indicates students, *s* indicates schools, and *t* indicates survey years; γ*<sub>s</sub>* denotes school fixed effects capturing time-invariant school characteristics; and δ*<sub>t</sub>* denotes survey year fixed effects capturing national period effects common across schools. Treat*<sub>s</sub>* indicates schools that adopted a phone ban, and Post*<sub>t</sub>* indicates the postpolicy period. Standard errors were clustered at the school level. Covariates *X<sub>ist</sub>* included grade, language spoken at home, and household composition. Models were estimated in the full sample and stratified by gender.

## Methods

This cross-sectional study was approved by the National Bioethics Committee of Iceland, and the protocol was registered with the Personal Data Protection Authority. Study data were obtained from the Youth in Iceland surveys, an established repeated nationwide school-based survey of adolescents. Students were informed that participation was voluntary, responses were anonymous and confidential, and they could decline any question or withdraw at any time. Students provided consent by voluntarily completing the survey. Parents or guardians were informed in advance and given the opportunity to opt their child out by notifying the school. The study followed the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) reporting guideline.

### Study Measures

Survey items are listed in eTable 1 in Supplement 1. Covariates included school grade (8-10), household status (2 caregivers vs other arrangements), language spoken at home (Icelandic only vs another language alone or with Icelandic), and school municipality (of 13 municipalities). Household status, language spoken at home, and school municipality were included as proxies for socioeconomic context, given their established associations with adolescent socioeconomic background in Iceland. Gender was self-reported as binary (girl or boy) in 2016 and 2018 and expanded to include additional nonbinary options in 2023. Because nonbinary options were available only in 2023 and were selected by few participants, gender-stratified analyses were restricted to girls and boys.

The primary outcomes were depressive symptoms, anxiety, and hostility in the preceding week assessed with the Symptom Checklist–90–Revised.<sup>20</sup> The depression subscale comprised 10 items (score range, 10-40), and the anxiety and hostility subscales comprised 5 items each (score range, 5-25). Item responses were summed, with higher scores indicating greater symptom severity. Internal consistency was good to excellent (Cronbach α, 0.91 for depression, 0.80 for anxiety, and 0.83 for hostility). Mental well-being was assessed using the Short Warwick-Edinburgh Mental Well-being Scale.<sup>21</sup> Participants rated 7 statements reflecting positive functioning during the past 2 weeks (range, 7-35; Cronbach α, 0.85), with higher scores indicating better well-being.

The secondary outcomes were perceived peer social support, bullying, and screen use. Perceived peer social support was measured with the 5-item Perceived Peer Support Scale<sup>22</sup> (range, 5-20; Cronbach α, 0.86), which captures perceived ease of obtaining social and emotional support from friends and peers. Items were summed, with higher scores indicating greater perceived support.
