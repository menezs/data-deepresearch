# **Statement from the Initiators** 

Over the past twelve months, the impression emerged that the debate around the four-day workweek in Germany had lost momentum with the federal elections. Discussions about work time reduction gave way to calls for longer hours and a more critical stance toward part-time arrangements. The question emerged: Had the chapter of “New Work” — to which the four-day week undoubtedly belongs — come to an end? 

From our perspective as practice partners of the study, however, what we observed was not an ending but rather a shift: **from public hype to a more focused and serious examination.** Organizations today are less concerned with symbolic models and more focused on the fundamental question of how productivity, efficiency, and employer attractiveness can be designed in a way that remains viable under real economic conditions. 

This is precisely where the findings of the update report resonate with our experience in organizational development: **Companies are no longer searching for an ideological 4-or-5-day solution, but for a sustainable mix of structure, flexibility, and performance orientation.** Working time is increasingly understood as a design variable rather than a dogma — especially in a moment in time where AI-based tools are beginning to redefine how work itself is organized and executed. What matters most is the ability to adapt models, sharpen processes, and actively involve employees in this transformation. 

**Working time reduction is not a golden bullet; it is a change process.** And like any meaningful change process, it requires investment, leadership, and clarity. The report underlines that the real challenge lies less in the specific model and more in the adaptability of people and organizations. The broader questions it raises — about the relationship of work, life, and performance — have also guided our thinking beyond the study in our capacity as Consultants. 



**Thus, the report offers an alternative perspective on the current economic policy debate in Germany.** It suggests that performance does not necessarily result from increasing working hours, but rather from smarter work organization, clearer prioritization, and adaptive structures. With or without a four-day week, the decisive factor is not the number of working days, but an organization’s ability to design work in a way that allows performance, health, and innovation to reinforce one another over the long term.

# **1. Study Context & Introduction** 

At the beginning of 2024, 45 organizations from various industries in Germany began piloting a four-day-week (4DW over a six-month period. Summary results were presented after the trial period in the report "The 4 Day-Week in Germany: First Results of Germany's Trial on Work Time Reduction," published in October 2024. The report examined performance and productivity, employer attractiveness, health, leisure activities, and social impacts (Backmann et al., 2024. 

Overall, a clear tension has emerged: while some politicians and employer representatives call for extended hours and greater flexibility, trade unions and employee representatives emphasize the protective function of existing limits and advocate for relief rather than expansion. Precisely because the debate oscillates between these two poles, the question of what effects specific work time models actually have in practice is becoming increasingly important. 

The relevance of these findings has since grown, as working hours remain a central topic of political debate in Germany. Particularly contentious is a proposal to make the Working Hours Act more flexible by shifting from a daily to a weekly maximum limit, planned for implementation in 2026. Proponents of the reform, including the Confederation of German Employers' Associations (BDA and the German Skilled Crafts Association (ZDH, argue that the current daily limit is outdated and ill-suited to modern, often project-driven and digitalized ways of working (Deutscher Bundestag, 2024. Hence, the shift does not necessarily mean longer hours, but rather a more flexible distribution of working time. Furthermore, the reform would bring German legislation in line with the EU Working Time Directive (2003/88/EC, which already operates on a weekly basis (Legal Tribune Online, 2025. On the other hand, Ver.di, the German Trade Union Confederation (DGB, and the Economic and Social Science Institute (WSI have warned against longer working days, less predictable schedules, and potential risks to health protection, with some announcing their opposition (Tagesschau, 2026.

# **5. Conclusion** 

The results of this update report show that a majority of organizations continue to operate with some form of WTR more than a year after the end of the pilot phase. At the same time, the classic 4DW has become less central. Many organizations have moved towards broader and more flexible forms of WTR that better accommodate their operational realities. This shift was already observable during the trial period and the continued adaptation of WTR models since then reinforces a key finding: the practical viability of WTR appears to depend less on adherence to a fixed format and more on an organization's willingness and capacity to adapt the model over time. 

However, the findings also make clear that these models do not come without costs. Regardless of whether organizations continued or discontinued reduced working time, the implementation process required sustained management attention, coordination effort, and, in some cases, came with productivity losses or delays during the implementation phase. WTR should therefore not be understood as a simple efficiency gain, but as a structural change process that involves trade-offs, transitional friction, and real implementation costs independent of the final outcome. 

At the individual level, the employee data paints a differentiated picture. Employees currently working under reduced hours consistently report more favorable outcomes across stress, burnout, mental health, and several performance dimensions. Yet these advantages are not equally pronounced across all domains. Life satisfaction and creativity at work do not significantly differ. Meanwhile, employees who returned to a conventional schedule occupy a distinct middle position. The finding that this group reports the lowest physical health scores while also reporting the highest financial satisfaction may point to inherent trade-offs that WTR involves at the individual level. 

Importantly, these findings are cross-sectional and self-reported, and the possibility of selection effects, whereby employees and organizations that continued with WTR differ systematically from those that did not, should be kept in mind. 

At the same time, many organizations report substantial benefits, ranging from improved employer attractiveness and employee satisfaction to more sustainable ways of working. Even among those that no longer use WTR, these benefits are often acknowledged, though they may be outweighed by operational or economic constraints in the specific organizational context. Conversely, the 30% of organizations that discontinued the model serve as an important reminder that WTR is not universally viable. Workload peaks, volatile market conditions, and structural constraints can render it unsustainable regardless of employee preferences.

Please note, this report no longer speaks primarily of a "four-day-week," but of "work time reduction". Already during the trial period, it became apparent that the label "four-day-week" did not fully capture the variety of models introduced. While the original trial was widely associated with a fixed day off per week, some organizations started to adopt more flexible arrangements even within the trial period. This trend has continued and become more pronounced since. 

Many organizations have moved towards flexible weekly reductions, monthly hour targets, or rotationbased systems that no longer correspond to a classic four-day format. What these diverse models share is not a particular schedule structure, but a reduction in total working hours. WTR is therefore the more accurate and analytically appropriate umbrella term as it captures the common denominator across all models without implying a specific format. 

Of the 41 organizations that completed the pilot, 40 participated in our follow-up survey, on which this report is based. As of February 2026, 70% of these organizations operate with some form of WTR, while 30% do not. The overall picture since the last report is one of consolidation. Most organizations that were previously in an extended trial phase have now either transitioned into full implementation — in some cases in an adapted form — or have discontinued the model altogether. Only in a small number of cases was an initial decision to continue later reversed. At the same time, the 30% that no longer practice WTR underscore that that sustaining such models is not straightforward for all organizations.

### **3.2.  Organizations Without Work Time Reduction** 

The transition from a reduced working time model back to a standard schedule was associated with several perceived negative effects across the participating organizations. According to their reports, nearly all organizations identified a worsening of both working conditions and work-life balance as a consequence of returning to the original schedule, with all organizations specifically noting a deterioration in work-life balance. In terms of health-related indicators, some organizations reported that sick leave had worsened compared to the period during the reduced schedule. Regarding external positioning, a few organizations reported a decline in employer attractiveness. For all other areas surveyed, no changes were reported. 

Despite these predominantly unfavorable perceptions, most organizations did not encounter major difficulties during the transition itself. Although employees in most organizations did not support the decision to return to a standard schedule, this did not lead to significant operational disruptions such as resignations. In particular, when the reasons for discontinuing the WTR were clearly communicated, management reported that employees were generally understanding, even if not in agreement with the decision. 

Regarding future plans, the organizations without WTR are evenly split into three groups: one third remains undecided, one third has no concrete plans for future changes, and one third has concrete plans to reintroduce some form of work time adjustment. Notably, however, the organizations with concrete plans intend to prioritize work time compression (i.e., redistributing the same number of hours into fewer days) over an actual reduction in total working hours.
