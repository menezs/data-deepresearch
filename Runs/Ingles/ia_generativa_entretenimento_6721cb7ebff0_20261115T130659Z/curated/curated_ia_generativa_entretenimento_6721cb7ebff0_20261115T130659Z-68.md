## What bill L 49 provides

The text amends the Danish Copyright Act with two new provisions. The first, the new section 73a, covers all natural persons, of any nationality. It bans making realistic digitally generated imitations of someone's appearance, voice or movements available to the public without their consent. The second, section 65a, protects performing artists who are citizens or residents of a country in the European Economic Area. It covers digital imitations of their performances. The technical framework comes from the analysis by the law firm Plesner and from the PPC Land reconstruction of the text filed on October 8.

The part that weighs most on publishers is the burden of proof. If a piece of content imitates a real person, the one who spreads it must show they have consent, and the imitated person does not have to show they withheld it. Intent to harm is not required either, since publishing through negligence is also unlawful. Consent can be withdrawn at any time and without formalities, but it applies only to future publications.

The protection lasts for the person's whole life and for 50 years after death (counted from the end of the year of death). Uses in the private sphere stay outside, such as a video shared at a party among friends, which the text does not treat as making something available to the public.

## Copyright in name only

The bill sits inside the Copyright Act but does not create a copyright in the classic sense. The text states that it protects personal characteristics as such, meaning identity. The protection therefore does not require that a work exist. That makes "copyright over your own face" a useful shortcut and a slightly misleading one. Nobody becomes the owner of a photograph or a recording, and what they gain is a right to ban certain imitations.

The choice has already stirred debate among jurists. According to Plesner's analysis, many commentators consider it conceptually improper to place personality rights in a law meant to support the circulation of works. Doubts remain about compatibility with the EU copyright directive.

The text, it should be said, does not implement European obligations and does not touch those of the AI Act, as PPC Land reports. Denmark is thus moving alone, with a purely national rule.

## Frequently asked questions

#### 1. Does the Danish law also apply to people who are not Danish citizens?

**Yes, for the general protection.** Section 73a protects all natural persons regardless of citizenship, while the rule on artists (section 65a) covers only performers who are citizens or residents of the European Economic Area. It remains to be seen how it will apply to content published by platforms based outside Denmark.

#### 2. Is sharing a deepfake in a private group banned?

**Generally no, if it stays in the private sphere.** The text excludes sharing within a small circle, such as a party among friends. The ban applies when the content is made available to the public, so publication on an open profile or on a platform.

#### 3. Can consent be withdrawn after authorizing an imitation?

**Yes, at any time and without formalities.** The withdrawal applies only going forward. Content already published with valid consent does not become unlawful retroactively, but no new content can be published.

#### 4. Who actually risks a conviction?

**No criminal penalty, at least according to the available analyses.** The bill operates on the civil level, with damages and a removal order. Whether and how fines for platforms will be provided will be settled by the committee work and the final text.

The point that no rule solves on its own remains practical. In any given week, who decides whether a video is satire or a well-made scam? In Denmark it would be a civil judge, case by case, after the fact. For a face that has already spread online, the time between publication and ruling is exactly when the damage is done.

## What is happening in the rest of Europe and in Italy

Other countries have already moved, but Denmark picks a different path from the rest. France bans spreading AI-generated images or audio of a person without consent when the artificial nature is not obvious or declared. The United Kingdom has made it an offense, since February, to create or request intimate non-consensual deepfakes. On the British criminal front we also covered the case of the 26 months in prison for sexual deepfakes of women. At European level, directive 2024/1385 on violence against women asks member states to punish the creation and spread of manipulated sexual material by June 14, 2027, although Denmark is excluded thanks to its opt-out clause. The comparative summary again comes from Euronews.

In Italy the route is criminal and already open. Article 612-quater of the penal code, introduced by law 132/2025 and in force since October 10, 2025, punishes with one to five years in prison anyone who spreads without consent images, videos or voices falsified with AI systems, when they can mislead about their genuineness and cause unjust harm to a person. The difference in approach is stark. In Italy it takes a crime, a harm and an investigation, while in Denmark a civil suit would suffice, with proof of consent resting on the publisher.

Anyone in Italy who wants more preventive protection makes do in other ways. The most cited case is the voice registered as a European Union trademark, a move that turns a personal trait into an exclusive right. And on the front of apps that undress people, the EU has already decided to ban nudifier apps from December 2. Meanwhile, with the Digital Omnibus on the AI Act, the European Parliament has postponed some of the AI rules, including those on the transparency of synthetic content.

## The road so far

The idea has a long history. On June 26, 2025, the government of the time and other parties reached a political agreement to protect people from unauthorized digital imitations, when Engel-Schmidt led the ministry. The project went to public consultation from July 7 to August 21, 2025, a period of 45 days, and the groups consulted included Google Denmark, Meta, TikTok and Spotify. A first version lapsed with the Danish general election, so the one of October 8 is in effect a second attempt.

The floor debate is still ahead. The 2025 agreement was signed by the government of the time and six other parties, so the political base is there, but the text still has to be debated and voted on. The bill does carry a date, January 1, 2027. Whether it is met depends on parliamentary timing, and a delay would not be a surprise.

## Satire, parody and false information

The exceptions are the ones you would expect. Satire, parody, caricature and criticism of power or society remain allowed, even when made with artificial intelligence tools. The limit comes when the imitation becomes disinformation capable of seriously endangering the essential rights or interests of other people, and in that case the satirical frame no longer protects it. Both Euronews and the AI Policy Desk analysis report this.

The line between satire and manipulation is thin. Stampe herself acknowledges it, and according to press accounts the minister left the decision to the courts, case by case. This is the most delicate point of the whole text. A political meme generated with AI can be satire today and disinformation tomorrow, depending on the context in which it circulates.

## Platforms, takedowns and penalties

Here the accounts diverge, so it is worth keeping them apart. The ministry explained that one of the law's main purposes is to make large platforms remove manipulated content quickly. On the rest, the versions differ. Part of the international press speaks of fines for those who fail to remove content after a request. In 2025 the Guardian had gathered, as Security Affairs summarizes, the words of Jakob Engel-Schmidt, then head of the culture ministry, about severe penalties for those who do not comply. The analysis drawing on the Plesner study, by contrast, describes a bill with no new filtering obligations and no criminal penalties. The route is civil, with damages, compensation for non-material harm and the possibility for a judge to order removal of the content from the medium where it was published.

I could not read the text of L 49 directly (the Folketing website rejects automated requests), so the part on penalties for intermediaries should be taken with that caveat. The point will be clarified in committee, the parliamentary Culture Committee to which the bill was assigned, along with some inconsistencies between the text and the explanatory notes that the analysts themselves have flagged.

## Why voice is the most urgent case

Video deepfakes make more noise, but the most concrete case remains voice. A few seconds of audio are enough to clone it, as the piece on how to clone a voice from a few seconds of recording shows. The scams that follow reach serious sums, as in the Fideuram case worth 36 million euros. Platforms are also getting equipped, with tools such as YouTube's face and voice detection, which works only for those who turn it on.

A law that shifts the burden of proof onto the publisher changes the incentive of a newsroom, a creator, an ad platform. Under the Danish rule, "I didn't know" is no longer enough, and publishers need to be able to show where a voice or a face comes from. That is traceability work that not everyone does.
