# Generative AI in Entertainment: Digital Actors, Synthetic Voices, and Copyright

Nov 14, 2026 · @Davi

## Scope

This report covers the period from the 2023 Hollywood strikes through mid-November 2026. It focuses on the United States, where most of the litigation, collective bargaining and lawmaking has happened, and adds the European Union and Denmark for comparison. It follows three connected threads. The first is digital actors, meaning both replicas of real performers and fully synthetic "performers." The second is synthetic voices. The third is copyright, which covers two questions: whether AI-generated output can be owned, and whether training AI on copyrighted works is lawful. The argument running through the report is that copyright law has turned out to be a poor tool for protecting a performer's face and voice. Contracts, labor agreements, state publicity laws and proposed federal legislation have taken over much of that job.

## Digital Actors: From Replicas to "Synthetics"

The most visible test of the synthetic-actor idea came in late September 2025. Xicoia, a new AI talent studio started by actress and producer Eline Van der Velden, CEO of the production company Particle6, presented an AI-generated "actress" named Tilly Norwood at the Zurich Summit, the industry event of the Zurich Film Festival \[1\]. Van der Velden answered the angry reaction by calling Norwood a creative work and an art piece rather than a substitute for a person. She also said AI characters should be treated as a genre of their own instead of being compared with human actors \[2\]. Scottish actress Briony Monroe said publicly that she believed Norwood was partly based on her likeness, because one promotional image looked like her \[2\]. SAG-AFTRA's formal response said Norwood was not an actor at all. In the union's view, it was the output of a computer program trained without permission or payment on the work of many professional performers \[3\].

That dispute fed directly into the next round of collective bargaining. SAG-AFTRA members ratified the 2026 TV/Theatrical Agreement with the Alliance of Motion Picture and Television Producers (AMPTP). The agreement took effect on July 1, 2026, and runs until June 30, 2030 \[4\]. About 91 percent of voting members approved it. Trade coverage noted that synthetic performers had become a flashpoint after Tilly Norwood appeared \[5\]. In the union's summary, producers commit to "a principle strongly favoring human performances" and agree not to use synthetic performers in place of humans unless doing so adds significant value \[6\]. The test is relative in two directions. A producer must show that a synthetic performer adds "significant additional value" compared with a union performer and also compared with a digital replica of that performer \[5\].

The weak point is that the parties never agreed on what that phrase means. Insiders said the contract contains no shared definition. Studio sources said that a proposed per-use payment for synthetics, nicknamed the "Tilly Tax," was a nonstarter for the AMPTP \[7\]. Instead, the deal sets out a procedure. A studio that wants to use a synthetic must notify the union and explain the value the synthetic adds. That triggers a mandatory bargaining window of about 26 days, and arbitration may follow \[8\]. Chief negotiator Duncan Crabtree-Ireland has said that in the end an arbitrator will decide whether a synthetic actually delivered significant additional value \[9\]. The union also gave something up: under the deal, members have no right to strike over AI until 2030 \[10\]. The agreement treats a real performer's replica differently from a synthetic. To replicate a performer's likeness or voice, a studio needs that performer's informed consent and must pay fairly, because the replica is treated as an extension of the performer and its work as the performer's work \[8\].

The animation contract that followed applies the same approach to voice actors. SAG-AFTRA's summary of the tentative 2026 Television Animation Agreement says consent will generally be required before a voice actor's digital replica is used to dub a performance into another language. Protections apply even when a replica is made without a traditional scan. Producers that license covered performances for commercial generative-AI training must notify the union and discuss the arrangement if asked \[11\]. That last provision brings collective bargaining into territory that copyright law normally covers: who gets to authorize training on recorded performances.

## Synthetic Voices: The Hardest Asset to Protect

The voice is arguably the most exposed part of a performer's identity. One reason is that voice cloning needs relatively little data. Another is that the law has never treated a voice as property in the way it treats a recording. The case that brought the issue to wide public attention involved Scarlett Johansson in May 2024. She said OpenAI had asked her to voice its ChatGPT assistant and that she declined. After users noticed that the "Sky" voice sounded like her, OpenAI paused it. Sam Altman said the voice came from a different actor who was cast before anyone approached Johansson \[12\]. Johansson used the episode to call for transparency and new legislation to protect individual rights \[13\].

The courts addressed the issue more directly in *Lehrman v. Lovo, Inc.* Voice actors Paul Lehrman and Linnea Sage said that people who turned out to be Lovo employees hired them through Fiverr in 2019 and 2020. Lovo then used their recordings to train its "Genny" generator and sold clones of their voices under the names "Kyle Snow" and "Sally Coleman" \[14\]. According to reporting on the case, Lehrman was paid $1,200 and Sage $800, after being told the recordings were for academic research and test radio scripts \[15\]. In a July 10, 2025 opinion, Judge J. Paul Oetken of the Southern District of New York held that copyright covers expression, not something as abstract as a voice, and dismissed the copyright claims based on the AI's output \[16\]. He also found that the plaintiffs had not explained how the training process itself infringed their copyrights. He let the breach-of-contract claims and the New York right-of-publicity claims go forward, along with Sage's claim over Lovo's use of her actual recording in investor presentations and YouTube videos \[14\]. Lawyers commenting on the decision concluded that people whose voices are cloned are likely to have more success under state law than under federal copyright \[17\].

Video games produced the longest labor fight over voices. SAG-AFTRA struck video game companies starting July 26, 2024 \[18\]. In July 2025, members ratified the 2025 Interactive Media Agreement by a vote of 95.04 to 4.96 percent \[19\]. One negotiator said "AI was the centerpiece of our proposal package," and the agreement added consent and disclosure requirements \[20\]. Coverage noted that nine major studios agreed to the AI guardrails, which brought game companies closer to the standards that apply to traditional Hollywood studios \[21\]. During the strike, a separate dispute showed that an estate's consent does not settle the labor question. In May 2025, SAG-AFTRA filed an unfair labor practice charge against Epic Games' Llama Productions over an AI-generated Darth Vader voice in *Fortnite* based on the late James Earl Jones. Epic said it had worked with Jones's estate on the voice, but the union said it received no notice and had no chance to bargain \[22\]. SAG-AFTRA argued that the AI voice took work from members, including actors who had previously matched Vader's rhythm and tone in games \[23\].

A licensed market for voices has also started to form. ElevenLabs launched its Iconic Voice Marketplace on November 11, 2025, with more than 25 voices. They included living performers such as Michael Caine, Liza Minnelli and Art Garfunkel, and historical figures such as Maya Angelou, Alan Turing and Mark Twain \[24\]. Analysts pointed out that the marketplace does nothing to stop unauthorized imitations, disputed archive rights or replicas made outside a licensed catalog \[24\]. Critics also noted that many of the "iconic" voices on the platform belong to people who are no longer alive to consent \[25\].

OpenAI's Sora 2 showed what happens when a consumer app tries to enforce consent at scale. During the invite-only launch, users were able to generate Bryan Cranston's likeness and voice even though OpenAI's policy required performers to opt in. Cranston took the issue to SAG-AFTRA, and OpenAI then strengthened its guardrails together with the union and the agencies CAA, UTA and the Association of Talent Agents. All of them said they supported the NO FAKES Act \[26\]. OpenAI called the incident "unintentional generations" \[27\]. The episode showed that an opt-in rule is only as strong as the technical filters behind it.

## Copyright, Part One: Who Owns AI Output?

On authorship, U.S. law is now fairly settled. In its January 29, 2025 report on copyrightability, the U.S. Copyright Office repeated that copyright requires human authorship. It drew the key line between AI used to assist a human creator and AI that stands in for one \[28\]. The Office concluded that prompts alone usually do not meet the creativity threshold because they function as instructions. A person who creatively selects or arranges AI output can still protect the resulting compilation, though not the AI-generated material itself \[29\]. The courts have agreed. On March 2, 2026, the Supreme Court denied certiorari in *Thaler v. Perlmutter*. That left in place the D.C. Circuit's ruling that U.S. copyright requires human authorship \[30\]. The D.C. Circuit did not decide how much human involvement is enough, because Thaler had disclaimed any creative input. That question is still being litigated in *Allen v. Perlmutter* \[31\].

For studios, this has a practical consequence. AI tools can reduce production costs, but footage, characters or dialogue generated mostly by AI may not be protectable on their own terms. Human writing, direction, editing and performance therefore carry legal weight as well as creative value. This is one underappreciated reason the industry still has an incentive to keep people involved, alongside union contracts and public opinion.

## Copyright, Part Two: Is Training Lawful?

Training is far more contested. In a pre-publication version of its training report released May 9, 2025, the Copyright Office said that training a model on large and diverse datasets will often be transformative. It warned, however, that this does not make every use fair \[32\]. Following *Warhol v. Goldsmith*, the Office treated market harm as often decisive. The release coincided with the firing of Register of Copyrights Shira Perlmutter \[33\]. The most important court ruling so far came in *Bartz v. Anthropic*. In June 2025, Judge William Alsup found that training Claude on the plaintiffs' books was fair use. He ruled separately on the company's acquisition of pirated copies \[34\]. The case settled for $1.5 billion, roughly $3,000 for each of about 500,000 books \[34\]. The settlement received final approval on July 20, 2026 \[35\], from Judge Martínez-Olguín, who took over the approval stage after Alsup retired \[36\]. The general lesson is that how training data was obtained matters as much as what the model does with it.

The entertainment industry's own litigation focuses more on outputs. In June 2025, Disney and Universal filed a 110-page complaint against Midjourney in Los Angeles. It placed the service's images next to characters such as Shrek, Homer Simpson and Darth Vader \[37\], and called the company a "bottomless pit of plagiarism" \[38\]. Midjourney responded that training on copyrighted works is fair use. Observers noted that the case is unusual because it targets outputs more than the training process \[39\]. In a June 2026 discovery order, a magistrate judge required Disney, Universal and Warner Bros. to produce some data on the studios' own use of AI \[40\]. That turns the studios' own AI adoption into a factor in the case. Disney also sued the Chinese video-AI firm MiniMax \[41\].

At the same time, the studios tried licensing. In December 2025, Disney agreed to invest $1 billion in OpenAI and to license more than 200 characters from Disney, Marvel, Pixar and Star Wars to Sora for three years. The deal explicitly excluded any talent likenesses or voices \[42\]. Keeping performers' faces and voices out of the license reflected how sensitive those rights had become after the strikes. The partnership did not last. On March 24, 2026, OpenAI said it would discontinue the Sora app to narrow its focus and resources \[43\]. Disney then withdrew from the $1 billion investment \[44\]. The Wall Street Journal reported that the app had been losing users \[45\]. The collapse showed that licensing deals depend on the business health of the platforms involved.

The music industry has moved furthest from lawsuits toward licensing. Universal Music Group settled with Udio in October 2025 and announced licensing deals for both recordings and publishing to support a new AI creation platform \[46\]. Warner Music Group then settled with Suno. Under that deal, Suno will introduce licensed models in 2026 to replace its current ones \[47\]. These agreements raise a question that matters for performers too: whether labels can license their whole catalogs for AI use without explicit consent from individual artists. So far the major labels have resisted requirements to get that consent for general model training \[48\].

## Beyond Copyright: Publicity Rights and New Legislation

Because copyright does not protect a voice or a face as such, lawmakers have used the right of publicity instead. On September 17, 2024, Governor Gavin Newsom signed two California laws. AB 2602 protects living performers from contracts that hand over their digital replica rights without real understanding. AB 1836 prohibits using digital replicas of deceased performers without the estate's consent \[49\]. Under AB 2602, a contract clause allowing a replica to replace a performer's actual services is unenforceable unless it includes a reasonably specific description of the intended uses \[50\]. Illinois passed similar requirements a few weeks earlier \[51\]. Tennessee's ELVIS Act, signed in March 2024, was the first state law to establish these protections for voice and likeness \[52\].

The federal NO FAKES Act would create a national right. It was reintroduced on May 20, 2026, as S.4591 and H.R.8915 \[53\]. The 2026 version includes a notice-and-takedown system similar to the DMCA's, along with more explicit exemptions for news, commentary, criticism and parody \[54\]. It would preempt some overlapping state laws. Tennessee's ELVIS Act would probably be covered by a carve-out for state laws that predate the January 2, 2025 cutoff \[55\]. The Senate Judiciary Committee advanced the bill by unanimous voice vote on June 18, 2026 \[56\], although Senators Mike Lee, Ted Cruz and Eric Schmitt raised First Amendment concerns before the vote \[57\]. On September 30, Cruz objected when Senator Marsha Blackburn sought to pass the bill by unanimous consent. Cruz had voted for the bill in committee but reportedly did not help broker a larger package that would have combined it with AI preemption and children's online safety \[58\]. RIAA chief Mitch Glazier said the bill still has a real chance in the lame-duck session. If it fails there, it will have to start over in 2027 \[59\]. Civil-liberties groups including the EFF and FIRE continue to argue that the bill gives too much takedown power and creates free-speech risks \[60\].

Europe offers two other models. In June 2025, Denmark proposed treating likeness as a copyright-style right. People could issue takedown notices and claim compensation even without showing harm to their reputation, and platforms could be liable if they fail to act \[61\]. The bill introduced in October 2026 would protect ordinary people as well as performers, including against digital imitations of their performances \[62\]. Under bill L 49, the rules would apply from 2027 and would shift the burden of proof onto publishers. Questions remain about whether it is compatible with EU copyright directives \[63\]. A European Parliament briefing noted that despite where the rules sit in Danish law, they are not copyright in the strict sense \[64\]. At the EU level, the AI Act's Article 50 transparency obligations, including deepfake labeling, became enforceable on August 2, 2026. The Commission endorsed a voluntary code of practice as an adequate way to comply and gave systems already on the market until December 2, 2026, to meet the marking requirement \[65\].

## Analysis: An Emerging Settlement of Consent, Compensation and Control

Taken together, these developments suggest an emerging arrangement for how the industry treats AI and performers, though it is incomplete. The first part is consent. Union contracts, California and Illinois law, ElevenLabs' licensed marketplace and the Disney–OpenAI deal all treat explicit, specific consent as what makes replica use legitimate. The second part is compensation, which so far comes more from contracts and settlements than from statutes. Examples include the replica payment terms in SAG-AFTRA contracts, the $1.5 billion Anthropic settlement and the music-label licensing deals. The third part is control: takedown systems, platform guardrails and labeling rules designed to keep consent meaningful after content circulates. Each part is still fragile. The "significant additional value" standard has no definition and will be tested in arbitration. Platform opt-in policies have already failed in practice, as the Cranston case showed. Federal publicity legislation is stalled by objections over free speech.

The most persistent gap is between replicas and synthetics. Current law and contracts protect identifiable people well: the actor whose face is scanned, the voice actor whose recordings are cloned, the estate that controls a late star's voice. They do much less for the diffuse group of performers whose work trained a model that produces a "new" face or voice that looks like no one in particular. Tilly Norwood fits that description, and Monroe's claim shows how hard it is to prove that a synthetic face resembles a specific person. Copyright doctrine adds to the difficulty. *Lovo* suggests that a voice's characteristics are not protectable expression, and the fair-use rulings on training have so far favored developers that obtained their data lawfully. The 2026 animation agreement's requirement to notify the union about training licenses is one of the few mechanisms that reaches that diffuse contribution. Even so, it requires discussion, not consent.

The industry's own economic incentives point in competing directions. Studios sue AI companies to protect valuable franchises and license to them to capture new revenue, sometimes in the same month. AI companies support likeness legislation while defending training as fair use. Unions accepted procedural limits instead of outright bans and gave up strike leverage over AI until 2030 in exchange. The human-authorship requirement, now reinforced by the Supreme Court's denial in *Thaler*, may end up protecting human performers in a quiet way: productions built mainly on AI output risk producing material nobody can fully own.

Several things will shape the next phase. One is whether the NO FAKES Act passes in the lame-duck session or has to start over in 2027. Another is how the Midjourney litigation resolves the question of output-focused infringement, and whether *Allen v. Perlmutter* defines how much human involvement is enough for authorship. A third is how the first arbitrations interpret "significant additional value." Further out, Denmark's model and the EU's labeling regime will show whether treating identity as a property-like right, enforced through takedowns, works better than the U.S. approach of publicity statutes and labor contracts. For now, the clearest conclusion is that copyright alone does not protect performers' faces and voices in the generative-AI era. Most of that protection comes from contracts, state laws and union agreements.
