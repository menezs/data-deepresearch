On March 19, at the AD/PD 2026 International Conference on Alzheimer’s and Parkinson’s Diseases and Related Neurological Disorders, during a symposium focused on oral semaglutide in early Alzheimer’s disease, which includes mild cognitive impairment (MCI) and mild Alzheimer’s, Novo Nordisk presented full data from the EVOKE (NCT04777396) and EVOKE+ (NCT04777409) trials for the first time. Top line results for these trials were announced in November 2025. Semaglutide is a glucagon-like peptide-1 receptor agonist (GLP-1RA). Since GLP-1RAs are currently a hot topic in the pharma industry, as they have made waves in the metabolic space, there was high anticipation for their potential to affect a neurodegenerative disease such as Alzheimer’s. Therefore, the failure of oral semaglutide in the two EVOKE trials has been hugely disappointing for the Alzheimer’s and neurodegenerative field.

The results of the EVOKE and EVOKE+ trials were comprehensive in showing oral semaglutide’s lack of clinical efficacy for cognition or function in patients with early Alzheimer’s. In both trials, there was no difference between oral semaglutide and placebo at the primary endpoint of change from baseline in Clinical Dementia Rating – Sum of Boxes (CDR-SB) score at week 104 (two years), or at the secondary endpoint of change in Alzheimer’s Disease Cooperative Study – Activities of Daily Living Scale for MCI (ADCS-ADL-MCI) score from baseline to week 104. The same trend was seen for the other secondary endpoints, such as Mini-Mental State Examination (MMSE) score and Alzheimer’s Disease Assessment Scale–Cognitive Subscale (ADAS-Cog) score. A pooled analysis across both trials also showed that oral semaglutide did not delay progression from MCI to mild Alzheimer’s, as there was no difference between the oral semaglutide and placebo groups. A further exploratory analysis of pre-defined subgroups, including sex, age, race, body mass index (BMI), and type 2 diabetes (T2D) status, also failed to provide any strong signals of efficacy for a particular subgroup of early Alzheimer’s patients.

## EVOKE and EVOKE+

EVOKE and EVOKE+ were built to answer the question at scale. The 2 phase 3, placebo-controlled trials (NCT04777396; NCT04777409) randomly assigned 3808 adults aged 55 to 85 years with amyloid-confirmed mild cognitive impairment (MCI) or mild dementia to once-daily oral semaglutide up to 14 mg or placebo across 566 sites in 40 countries. Topline results were presented at the 2025 Clinical Trials on Alzheimer's Disease (CTAD) conference, held December 1-4 in San Diego, California, and full data followed in *The Lancet* on March 19, 2026.<sup>2,6</sup>

After 2 years, patients in both arms had declined at nearly the same pace. CDR-SB scores rose 2.3 points with semaglutide and 2.3 with placebo in EVOKE (difference, −0.08; 95% CI, −0.35 to 0.20), and 2.2 vs 2.1 points in EVOKE+ (difference, 0.10; 95% CI, −0.17 to 0.38).

The biomarker picture was less uniform. In a CSF substudy of 199 participants, semaglutide lowered markers of tau pathology, glial activation, and synaptic injury, including p-tau217, YKL-40, and neurogranin, by up to 10% relative to placebo, and plasma hsCRP declined in both trials.<sup>2</sup> Plasma p-tau levels, however, did not change.

The investigators offered several explanations. They cited a mean CSF semaglutide concentration of 0.08 nmol/L as evidence of limited central exposure, and noted that only 13.6% of participants had T2D, unlike the real-world cohorts that produced the original signal. They also raised earlier, presymptomatic treatment as a possibility the trials did not test. Discontinuations due to adverse events were about twice as common with semaglutide (roughly 17% vs 8%), and Novo Nordisk ended the planned 1-year extension.<sup>2,6</sup>

Despite the conclusive negative efficacy results, there was a significant impact on biomarkers in the EVOKE trials. In patients who were treated with oral semaglutide, there was a significant reduction in cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) biomarkers of Alzheimer’s pathology, such as p-tau181, at week 78 compared with the placebo group. Despite the significance of this finding, the reduction was only around 10%. Experts agreed that this reduction, in combination with the negative clinical outcomes in the trials, was too small to provide any real benefit for patients. However, experts noted that this finding does raise the need for more investigation to better understand the standard amyloid- and tau-based biomarkers in the context of non-amyloid and non-tau therapies.

Semaglutide was being evaluated in Alzheimer’s following consistent real-world and observational evidence from multiple studies showing that patients receiving GLP-1RAs for obesity and T2D had a reduced risk of all-cause dementia, as well as Alzheimer’s specifically. Furthermore, two small clinical trials (NCT01469351, NCT01843075) had shown the possible benefits of GLP-1RAs in mild-to-moderate Alzheimer’s, although no clinical conclusions could be drawn because of their sample sizes and study designs. It is therefore surprising that the EVOKE trials were so comprehensively negative. However, there were some key differences between the real-world evidence (RWE) and the clinical trials. Notably, the RWE was looking at patients with metabolic pathologies (T2D and obesity), whereas regulators specified that patients had to have confirmed the pathology for an Alzheimer’s trial, creating two very different patient populations. Additionally, the RWE was looking at a time to diagnosis of Alzheimer’s as a kind of endpoint, whereas the endpoint for the EVOKE clinical trials was measuring the slowing of disease progression in patients with already-established Alzheimer’s, creating a different question about the effects of semaglutide.

## A Hypothesis Built on Shared Biology

Much of the interest centers on glucagon-like peptide-1 receptor agonists (GLP-1RAs), the incretin-based class that includes liraglutide and semaglutide. In transgenic mouse models of AD, liraglutide improved memory and prevented synaptic loss while reducing β-amyloid and tau aggregation, neuroinflammation, and oxidative stress.<sup>1</sup> Investigators behind the semaglutide program framed their rationale around 2 early features of AD: glial activation and neurovascular dysfunction that can weaken the blood-brain barrier.<sup>2</sup>

Population data gave that preclinical work clinical weight. A 2022 analysis pooling 3 cardiovascular outcome trials in type 2 diabetes (T2D) found a lower dementia rate with GLP-1RAs than with placebo among 15,820 participants (HR, 0.47; 95% CI, 0.25-0.86).<sup>3</sup>

Later target trial emulations, which structure health record analyses to mimic a randomized trial, pointed the same way. Among more than 1 million patients with T2D, semaglutide was linked to a 40% to 70% lower risk of a first AD diagnosis compared with 7 other antidiabetic drugs.<sup>4</sup> A 2025 study found comparable reductions in AD and related dementias with GLP-1RAs (HR, 0.67) and with sodium-glucose cotransporter-2 (SGLT2) inhibitors, a second glucose-lowering class (HR, 0.57).<sup>5</sup>

## From Observation to Randomized Trials

The first randomized look in established disease came from ELAD (NCT01843075), a UK phase 2b trial of daily injectable liraglutide in 204 people with mild to moderate AD and no diabetes, published in December 2025. Over 52 weeks, liraglutide did not change the primary end point of cerebral glucose metabolism on FDG-PET (*P* = .14). It did show a nominal advantage in executive function (unadjusted *P* = .01), although global and functional measures, including the CDR-SB and ADCS-ADL, did not separate from placebo.<sup>1</sup>

# From Glucose to Cognition: Where Repurposed Diabetes Drugs Stand in Alzheimer Disease

*Marco Meglio*

### Key Takeaways

- Preclinical GLP-1RA data indicate effects on synapses, amyloid/tau aggregation, neuroinflammation, and oxidative stress, aligning with AD mechanisms such as glial activation and neurovascular dysfunction.
- Cardiovascular outcome trials and target-trial emulations in T2D associate GLP-1RAs—and also SGLT2 inhibitors—with materially lower incident dementia/AD risk versus comparators.

As diabetes drugs are tested for repurposing in Alzheimer disease, the negative phase 3 EVOKE and EVOKE+ results for oral semaglutide have moved attention toward prevention and earlier, combination-based approaches.

As World Diabetes Day is observed on November 14, a central question in Alzheimer disease (AD) research is whether drugs designed to control blood glucose can also alter the course of neurodegeneration. The largest test of that idea, Novo Nordisk's phase 3 EVOKE program of oral semaglutide, did not slow clinical decline, and attention has since shifted toward earlier intervention and combination approaches.

This feature traces how diabetes drugs became candidates for repurposing in AD, from the mechanistic and real-world evidence behind the hypothesis to the randomized trials that have tested it. It also looks at the studies still underway and what they are designed to answer.

## What Is Still Being Tested

Other metabolic agents remain in the clinic. The National Institute on Aging-funded MAP trial (NCT04098666), a phase 2/3 study of the biguanide metformin, is comparing an extended-release formulation at up to 2000 mg daily with placebo over 18 months in overweight or obese adults with amnestic MCI and no diabetes. It reached primary completion in April 2026, with final completion expected in April 2027.<sup>7</sup>

Prevention is the other front. At the 2026 Alzheimer's Association International Conference (AAIC), held July 12-15 in London, the Alzheimer's Association launched PROTECT-Cog, a $100 million, 3-year global trial. The study will test whether adding a GLP-1RA or similar metabolism-targeting drug to the U.S. POINTER multidomain lifestyle program further lowers the risk of cognitive decline in at-risk older adults, moving the question from slowing established disease to preventing it.<sup>8</sup>

The world’s largest and longest trial of exenatide in people with Parkinson’s disease was funded by the National Institute for Health & Care Research (NIHR) with support for sub-studies from Cure Parkinson’s and Van Andel Institute.

For their research, published in *The Lancet,* the team designed a randomised controlled phase 3 trial to definitively determine whether exenatide use was associated with any benefit in people with Parkinson’s disease and if it led to a slower rate of progression of the illness, after smaller studies had suggested the drug may be helpful. 

194 people with Parkinson’s disease participated in the trial across six research hospitals in the UK, including in London, Oxford, Plymouth, Salford and Edinburgh. They either used exenatide injections on a weekly basis, or a visually identical placebo.

The trial ran for 96 weeks and was double blind, meaning that neither the participants nor researchers knew who was receiving the real treatment or placebo.

At the end of the study period, the researchers found that there was no advantage at any point over the course of 96 weeks in the group using exenatide. And no benefits were shown in the objective examination of patients’ movements, their own reports of symptom severity, or imaging findings.

Lead author, Professor Thomas Foltynie (UCL Queen Square Institute of Neurology) said: “The results of this trial have been eagerly anticipated and the negative results will be a major disappointment to patients affected by Parkinson’s disease and the Parkinson’s disease research community.”

GLP-1 drugs – such as semaglutide (Wegovy) and Ozempic - have become increasingly popular over recent years and there has been an enormous interest in their potential health benefits.

For example, semaglutide (Wegovy) can be used as a treatment for Type 2 diabetes alongside being a licensed weight loss drug.

Meanwhile, older members of the drug class – such as exenatide and lixisenatide – have also been shown to be capable of penetrating into the brain and previous small trials have suggested potential benefits to people with Parkinson’s disease.
