# FDA Requires Safety Labeling Changes for Glucagon-Like Peptide-1 Receptor Agonists (GLP-1 RAs) Due to Potential Increased Risk of Rare But Serious Eye Condition

FDA Drug Safety Communication – November 9, 2026


The U.S. Food and Drug Administration is informing patients and health care providers of a potential increased risk of non-arteritic anterior ischemic optic neuropathy (NAION) associated with use of glucagon-like peptide-1 receptor agonist (GLP-1 RA) medications. NAION is a rare condition that can result in acute, painless, decreased vision in one eye, including permanent vision loss.



The agency is requiring application holders for all GLP-1 RA medications to add information to the Warnings and Precautions section of labeling about the potential increased risk of NAION associated with use of these medications.



GLP-1 RAs are a class of medications (see list in Table) that mimic the effects of a natural hormone called glucagon-like peptide-1 (GLP-1) that the intestine releases. GLP-1 helps lower blood glucose (sugar) levels after eating and acts in parts of the brain that control appetite and food intake. GLP-1 RAs are widely used to treat people with type 2 diabetes or to reduce excess body weight and maintain weight reduction long term.

There are additional approved uses in adults for several medications in this drug class. These approved uses for certain GLP-1 RA drugs include:

- To reduce the risk of major cardiovascular events, such as death, heart attack, or stroke in adults with known heart disease and with either obesity/overweight or type 2 diabetes
- To reduce the risk of worsening kidney disease, kidney failure, and death due to cardiovascular disease in adults with type 2 diabetes and chronic kidney disease
- To treat moderate to severe obstructive sleep apnea in adults with obesity
- To treat adults with metabolic dysfunction-associated steatohepatitis with moderate-to-advanced liver scarring (fibrosis)

# PRAC concludes eye condition NAION is a very rare side effect of semaglutide medicines Ozempic, Rybelsus and Wegovy

Treatment with semaglutide should be stopped if NAION occurs

      NewsHumanMedicinesPharmacovigilance

EMA’s safety committee (PRAC) has concluded its review of medicines containing semaglutide following concerns regarding a possible increased risk of developing non-arteritic anterior ischemic optic neuropathy (NAION), an eye condition that may cause loss of vision. Semaglutide, a GLP-1 receptor agonist, is the active substance in certain medicines used in the treatment of diabetes and obesity (namely Ozempic, Rybelsus and Wegovy).

After reviewing all available data on NAION with semaglutide, including data from non-clinical studies, clinical trials, post-marketing surveillance and the medical literature, PRAC has concluded that NAION is a very rare side effect of semaglutide (meaning it may affect up to 1 in 10,000 people taking semaglutide).

Results from several large epidemiological studies suggest that exposure to semaglutide in adults with type 2 diabetes is associated with an approximately two-fold increase in the risk of developing NAION compared with people not taking the medicine. This corresponds to approximately one additional case of NAION per 10,000 person-years of treatment; one person-year corresponds to one person taking semaglutide for one year. Data from clinical trials also point to a slightly higher risk of developing the condition in people taking semaglutide, compared with people taking placebo (a dummy treatment).

EMA has therefore recommended that the product information for semaglutide medicines is updated to include NAION as a side effect with a frequency of ‘very rare’. If patients experience a sudden loss of vision or rapidly worsening eyesight during treatment with semaglutide, they should contact their doctor without delay. If NAION is confirmed, treatment with semaglutide should be stopped.

#### **A safety signal against a widening neurology pipeline**

The warning lands as GLP-1 RAs are being tested across a growing list of neurologic diseases for their anti-inflammatory and neuroprotective potential. None of the trials below has reported a NAION signal, but each carries its own safety data that will now be read alongside FDA's warning.

In Alzheimer disease (AD), Novo Nordisk's phase 3 EVOKE and EVOKE+ trials tested oral semaglutide in 3808 adults with mild cognitive impairment or mild dementia due to AD. Neither trial met its primary end point of slowing decline on the Clinical Dementia Rating-Sum of Boxes scale, though semaglutide reduced high-sensitivity C-reactive protein and showed nominal reductions in several cerebrospinal fluid tau markers.

Full results presented at CTAD 2025 showed AEs in 91.2% of semaglutide-treated patients vs 84.8% on placebo, with the most common being decreased appetite, nausea, diarrhea, and vomiting, and no visual or ophthalmologic events reported above the 10% threshold.<sup>3</sup>

In Parkinson disease, 3 trials have tested different GLP-1 RAs with mixed results. A phase 2 trial of lixisenatide in 156 patients found a statistically significant slowing of motor disability progression at 12 months, but nausea occurred in 46% of treated patients vs 12% on placebo, and 1 patient developed treatment-related pancreatitis, prompting outside experts to caution that the drug was “not ready for prime time,” according to the investigators.<sup>4</sup>

A larger phase 3 trial of weekly exenatide in 194 patients found no benefit on motor, cognitive, or imaging measures over 96 weeks, with serious AEs in 9% of exenatide-treated patients vs 11% on placebo.<sup>5</sup> A smaller Swedish trial found exenatide altered motor-network imaging patterns without a measurable clinical benefit. None of the 3 reported eye-related AEs.
