# NO FAKES Act: where the US bill on AI voice and likeness stands

The United States still has no federal law on AI copies of a person's voice or face. The NO FAKES Act would create one. It passed the Senate Judiciary Committee unanimously in June 2026, but a fast-track vote was blocked at the end of September.

## What the bill would do

- Create a federal right in a person's voice and visual likeness.
- Make people and companies liable for producing or distributing an unauthorised digital replica of someone's voice or likeness.
- Set up a notice-and-removal process for platforms.
- Preempt some state laws, while keeping state laws that already existed in early 2025.

## Timeline

- 20 May 2026: the NO FAKES Act of 2026 is introduced in the Senate (S. 4591) and the House (H.R. 8915).
- 18 June 2026: the Senate Judiciary Committee advances it by unanimous voice vote.
- 30 September 2026: a request to pass it by unanimous consent is blocked by an objection from Senator Ted Cruz, who cited free-speech concerns.
- As of late October 2026: neither chamber has held a recorded vote.

## Until then, state law applies

Without a federal rule, protection depends on the state. Tennessee's ELVIS Act, in force since July 2024, already protects a person's voice against unauthorised AI imitation, and several other states have right-of-publicity laws that courts apply to digital replicas.

## What it means for you

Wherever the law ends up, the safe practice is the same: clone only your own voice and likeness, keep proof of consent for anyone else, and label AI-made content when you publish it. That is how voice cloning on TalkMie works: one voice, recorded by its owner, for the owner's own twin.

## Frequently asked questions

### Is the NO FAKES Act law?

No. As of October 28, 2026, it has cleared the Senate Judiciary Committee but has not passed the full Senate or the House, and it has not been signed.

### What does NO FAKES stand for?

Nurture Originals, Foster Art, and Keep Entertainment Safe. The 2026 version is S.4591 in the Senate and H.R. 8915 in the House.

### Who sponsored the NO FAKES Act?

Senator Chris Coons of Delaware introduced S.4591 on May 20, 2026, with Senator Marsha Blackburn of Tennessee among its lead supporters and 15 cosponsors from both parties.

### Why did Ted Cruz block the NO FAKES Act?

He objected to passing it by unanimous consent on September 30, 2026, saying its parody exception is unclear and leaves technology platforms to decide what counts as protected satire.

### When could the NO FAKES Act pass?

The earliest realistic window is the lame-duck session after the November 3, 2026 midterms. If it does not pass by January 3, 2027, it must be reintroduced in the new Congress.

### Does the NO FAKES Act protect people who are not famous?

Yes. It defines an individual as "a human being, living or dead," with no requirement of fame or commercial value.

### How long would the NO FAKES Act protect someone after death?

Ten years, renewable in five-year periods if the right holder shows active, authorized use and files a notice with the Register of Copyrights, up to a maximum of 70 years.

### Would the NO FAKES Act replace state laws like the ELVIS Act?

No. It would preempt future state laws on digital replicas but preserve state laws that existed on January 2, 2025, including Tennessee's ELVIS Act.

### Does a disclaimer make a deepfake legal under the NO FAKES Act?

No. The bill says a disclaimer that a replica is unauthorized or made with AI is not a defense.

### Would the NO FAKES Act ban AI voice cloning?

No. It targets unauthorized replicas and tools built to clone specific people without permission. A replica made with the person's consent, such as a Persona you build of yourself, is exactly what the bill allows.

## **IV. CONCLUSION** 

The Copyright Office agrees with the numerous commenters that have asserted an urgent need for new protection at the federal level.  The widespread availability of generative AI tools that make it easy to create digital replicas of individuals’ images and voices has highlighted gaps in existing laws and raised concerns about the harms that can be inflicted by unauthorized uses. 

We recommend that Congress establish a federal right that protects all individuals during their lifetimes from the knowing distribution of unauthorized digital replicas.  The right should be licensable, subject to guardrails, but not assignable, with effective remedies including monetary damages and injunctive relief.  Traditional rules of secondary liability should apply, but with an appropriately conditioned safe harbor for OSPs.  The law should contain explicit First Amendment accommodations.  Finally, in recognition of well-developed state rights of publicity, we recommend against full preemption of state laws. 

The Office remains available as a resource to Congress, the courts, and the executive branch in considering the recommendations in this Report and future developments.

## Definitions

**NO FAKES Act:** the Nurture Originals, Foster Art, and Keep Entertainment Safe Act of 2026, S.4591 in the Senate and H.R. 8915 in the House, which would create a federal right in every person's voice and visual likeness.

**Digital replica:** in the bill's words, "a newly created, computer-generated, highly realistic electronic representation that is readily identifiable as the voice or visual likeness of an individual," in material where the person did not actually perform, or where a real performance was materially altered.

**Right holder:** the person whose voice or likeness it is, and anyone who later acquires the right "through a license, inheritance, or otherwise."

**Unanimous consent:** a Senate shortcut that passes a bill without a recorded vote, provided no senator objects. A single objection stops it.

**Lame-duck session:** the weeks between the November election and the start of the new Congress in January, when the outgoing Congress often clears bills it left unfinished.

## **EXECUTIVE SUMMARY** 

This first Part of the Copyright Office’s Report on copyright and artificial intelligence (“AI”)<sup>1</sup> addresses the topic of digital replicas.  From AI-generated musical performances to robocall impersonations of political candidates to images in pornographic videos, an era of sophisticated digital replicas has arrived.  Although technologies have long been available to produce fake images or recordings, generative AI<sup>2</sup> technology’s ability to do so easily, quickly, and with uncanny verisimilitude has drawn the attention and concern of creators, legislators, and the general public. 

As part of a broad AI Initiative, the Copyright Office sought comments on these developments.  We asked whether existing laws provide sufficient protection against unauthorized digital replicas or if new protection is needed at the federal level.  In response, numerous commenters called for a new federal law to protect individuals from the appropriation of their persona.  They provided extensive input into the justifications for and the appropriate parameters of such a law. 

In the months since the Office’s inquiry was launched, unauthorized digital replicas have continued to make headlines, and have triggered Congressional activity.  During this time, we analyzed the comments received, performed additional research, and consulted with other agencies on their relevant areas of expertise.  Based on all of this input, we have concluded that a new law is needed.  The speed, precision, and scale of AI-created digital replicas calls for prompt federal action.  Without a robust nationwide remedy, their unauthorized publication and distribution threaten substantial harm not only in the entertainment and political arenas, but also for private individuals. 

Section I summarizes the context and history of the Office’s study of the digital replicas issue.  Section II.A outlines the main existing legal frameworks:  state rights of privacy and publicity, including recent legislation specifically targeting digital replicas, and at the federal level, the Copyright Act, the Federal Trade Commission Act, the Communications Act, and the Lanham Act.

##### **b) NO FAKES Act Discussion Draft** 

The NO FAKES Act discussion draft provides for a right “to authorize the use of the image, voice, or visual likeness of the individual in a digital replica.”<sup>177</sup> The right is a descendible and licensable property right that continues for 70 years after the individual’s death, even if it is not exploited during their lifetime.<sup>178</sup> Licensing of the right is valid only if the individual is represented by counsel; the agreement is in writing; or the license is governed by a collective bargaining agreement.<sup>179</sup> 

The draft bill imposes liability for producing and disseminating a digital replica without consent.<sup>180</sup> It conditions liability on “knowledge that the digital replica was not authorized by the applicable individual or rights holder.”<sup>181</sup> The draft includes a list of categorical exclusions from liability, including the use of digital replicas in news, public affairs, or sports broadcasts; in documentary, historical, or biographical works; for comment, criticism, scholarship, satire, or parody; and where the use is _de minimis_ or incidental.<sup>182</sup> 

Potential remedies include statutory or actual damages, whichever is greater; punitive damages; and attorney’s fees.<sup>183</sup> The bill categorizes the law as an intellectual property law for the purposes of Section 230 of the Communications Decency Act.<sup>184</sup> It expressly does not preempt other state or federal laws.<sup>185</sup>
