# NO FAKES Act: where the US bill on AI voice and likeness stands

The United States still has no federal law on AI copies of a person's voice or face. The NO FAKES Act would create one. It passed the Senate Judiciary Committee unanimously in June 2026, but a fast-track vote was blocked at the end of September.

## What the bill would do

- Create a federal right in a person's voice and visual likeness.
- Make people and companies liable for producing or distributing an unauthorised digital replica of someone's voice or likeness.
- Set up a notice-and-removal process for platforms.
- Preempt some state laws, while keeping state laws that already existed in early 2025.

## Timeline

- 20 May 2026: the NO FAKES Act of 2026 is introduced in the Senate (S. 4591) and the House (H.R. 8915).
- 18 June 2026: the Senate Judiciary Committee advances it by unanimous voice vote.
- 30 September 2026: a request to pass it by unanimous consent is blocked by an objection from Senator Ted Cruz, who cited free-speech concerns.
- As of late October 2026: neither chamber has held a recorded vote.

## Until then, state law applies

Without a federal rule, protection depends on the state. Tennessee's ELVIS Act, in force since July 2024, already protects a person's voice against unauthorised AI imitation, and several other states have right-of-publicity laws that courts apply to digital replicas.

## What it means for you

Wherever the law ends up, the safe practice is the same: clone only your own voice and likeness, keep proof of consent for anyone else, and label AI-made content when you publish it. That is how voice cloning on TalkMie works: one voice, recorded by its owner, for the owner's own twin.

## Why did Ted Cruz block the NO FAKES Act?

Senator Cruz objected because he believes the bill leaves too much power over parody in the hands of technology platforms. "One of my concerns about how this bill is drafted right now is it will apply to websites like the Babylon Bee or The Onion, and it leaves the decision as to whether someone's use of a likeness falls within the exception," he said on the Senate floor, according to reporting on the September 30 exchange. "It creates an exception for what it calls bona fide parody, but it leaves to big tech to make the decision whether it falls in that exception or not." He cited AI-generated political content, including images posted on President Donald Trump's social media accounts and a deepfake from the Los Angeles mayoral race.

Senator Blackburn answered that the protections Cruz wanted were already in the bill. "We have worked with First Amendment legal experts in drafting the bill, and we are confident that it is First Amendment compliant," she said, adding that the sponsors had "doubled down" on the language. She pointed to the counter-notice process: anyone who believes their removed content is protected speech can file a counter-notice, and unless the complainant goes to court within 14 days, "the content can go right back up."

The disagreement is narrower than it sounds. Nobody on the Senate floor argued against a federal right in a person's voice; Cruz voted for the bill in committee and said he wants it to pass. The fight is over who decides, in the first days after a takedown notice, whether a fake is protected satire. Under the bill's design, a platform facing damages of up to $750,000 per work has every reason to remove first and let a court sort it out later, which is the same concern the Electronic Frontier Foundation raised when it opposed the bill in June. A deal on the parody wording would likely clear the way for a vote.

## What happens next?

The bill has three realistic paths, and the calendar decides between them. The first runs through the lame-duck session that follows the November 3 midterms. If the sponsors and Senator Cruz agree on revised parody language, Senator Blackburn can ask for unanimous consent again, and the House would then need to pass the same text before Congress adjourns. The RIAA's Mitch Glazier, whose industry has lobbied hardest for the bill, put the odds plainly on Billboard's On the Record, as summarized by AI Musicpreneur: "We have a shot in the lame duck [period], a real shot."

The second path is a ride on a larger bill. Year-end spending and defense packages often carry smaller measures that could not pass alone, although no one has announced a plan to attach this one. The third path, which Glazier called the more likely outcome, is a fresh start: the bill dies on January 3, 2027, and its sponsors introduce it again in the new Congress, where it has to clear committee a second time in both chambers.

Whatever happens in Congress, the direction of federal policy is set. The White House's AI policy framework, released in March 2026, recommends that Congress create a federal rule against the unauthorized distribution or commercial use of AI replicas of a person's voice or likeness, according to a Forbes analysis. The open questions are the parody wording, the takedown mechanics and how much room states keep, not whether a federal right is coming.

## Definitions

**NO FAKES Act:** the Nurture Originals, Foster Art, and Keep Entertainment Safe Act of 2026, S.4591 in the Senate and H.R. 8915 in the House, which would create a federal right in every person's voice and visual likeness.

**Digital replica:** in the bill's words, "a newly created, computer-generated, highly realistic electronic representation that is readily identifiable as the voice or visual likeness of an individual," in material where the person did not actually perform, or where a real performance was materially altered.

**Right holder:** the person whose voice or likeness it is, and anyone who later acquires the right "through a license, inheritance, or otherwise."

**Unanimous consent:** a Senate shortcut that passes a bill without a recorded vote, provided no senator objects. A single objection stops it.

**Lame-duck session:** the weeks between the November election and the start of the new Congress in January, when the outgoing Congress often clears bills it left unfinished.

### What Would the NO FAKES Act Do for Creators?

**It would make your face and voice something you own nationally, the way you already own the videos you make**. That protection currently exists state by state, and what it covers changes every time you cross a border.

What it would mean in practice:

- **You could not sign it away.** The right stays yours for life and cannot be transferred, whatever a brand deal, production contract, or platform's terms of service might say. After death, it becomes licensable by your estate for up to 70 years.
- **Platforms would have to act on your report,** under a takedown process modelled on the one that already exists for copyright. A service that removes flagged content promptly is shielded from liability, and none would be obliged to go hunting for fakes on its own.
- **The uploader could contest it.** If they dispute your report and you do not sue within 14 days, the video can be restored. A dispute filed in bad faith carries a $25,000 penalty.
- **The penalties are heavy.** A service that ignores valid reports faces**up to $750,000 for a single video** , falling to $25,000 where it can show a good-faith effort to comply.
- **Parody, news reporting, criticism, documentaries, and incidental appearances stay protected.** Nobody would lose the right to make a video about you.

### Which Laws Protect Your Likeness Right Now?

Your state probably does, and creators overlook this while watching Congress.

**Most US states have long had right-of-publicity laws, which stop other people earning money from your name, face, or voice without permission**. Several have now been rewritten to say explicitly that an AI copy counts.

- **Tennessee's ELVIS Act** is the strongest of them for a cloned voice. It has been law since July 1, 2024, and despite the name, it protects every individual rather than only recording artists. Its important move is definitional: a sound the public recognises as you counts as your voice whether or not you ever made it,**which puts AI imitations inside the statute rather than in a grey area beside it** . Liability extends to whoever distributed the cloning tool. Damages triple where the defendant knew the clone was unauthorised.
- **California went after the paperwork.** Since January 1, 2025, a contract clause handing over rights to a digital replica of you is void there unless the agreement specifies how the replica will be used and you were represented by a lawyer or a union when you signed it. That is worth checking against any likeness clause a brand or production company has sent you. A companion law from the same date**bars AI recreations of deceased performers without estate consent, at $10,000 per violation or actual losses, whichever is greater.**

Whether any of it reaches your case depends on where you live, where the uploader lives, and where the harm landed. Establishing that takes a lawyer and several months. A takedown request to YouTube takes an afternoon, which is why it is almost always the first move.

Two details matter more than the headlines suggest. The first is how it treats the past. The right applies to every individual "regardless of whether the individual dies before, on, or after the date of enactment," and for people who died before the bill becomes law it would "vest in the executors, heirs, assignees, or devisees," so the estates of people who have already died, including Dolly Parton's, would gain a federal right. Civil liberties groups have long criticized the same mechanism in copyright law, and our view is that unmasking an anonymous speaker should require a judge.

## What would the NO FAKES Act mean for you and your family?

If it passes in its current form, you would hold a federal property right in your voice and face from the day it takes effect, whether or not you are famous. Anyone who posted a convincing AI copy of you without your permission, or sold an app built to clone you, could be sued, and you could send a platform a takedown notice instead of hiring a lawyer first. Children would be protected by the five-year license limit and the court approval requirement, which would stop a parent from signing away a child's face in an app's terms of service.

The after-death rules are where families need to pay attention. Your heirs would hold the right for ten years. Keeping it beyond that would require two things: someone actively and lawfully using your voice or likeness in commerce, and a sworn filing with the Register of Copyrights during each renewal window. Seventy years is the ceiling for estates that do both, like the estates of famous musicians. For most families, the federal right would quietly lapse a decade after death, which is fine if nobody ever wanted to use your voice, and a problem if your grandchildren later want to stop a stranger from doing so.

That puts the weight on something no statute supplies: a named person who knows they hold the right, knows what you consented to while you were alive, and can prove what your real voice sounds like. ## What Should You Do If You Find a Deepfake of Yourself on YouTube?

Pick the route that matches what the video is. These run as separate processes at YouTube, not one form with different labels, and filing under the wrong one wastes the review.

**A realistic AI video of you saying or doing something you did not**…

…goes through the privacy complaint process. YouTube's privacy guidelines cover synthetic or altered content that depicts a realistic version of you, so you do not need to argue copyright. You must be uniquely identifiable in the video, and the request has to come from you or your legal representative.

**Your own footage, re-uploaded or clipped into an AI edit…**

…is a copyright removal request. This is stronger ground, and the review is faster, because ownership is easier to prove than harm.

**Intimate images shared without your consent, real or AI-made…**

…have a legal deadline behind them. Platforms must take them down, and any copies they know about, within 48 hours of a proper request. That became enforceable on May 19, 2026, and the FTC runs a complaint site at **TakeItDown.ftc.gov** if a platform ignores you.

**A channel pretending to be you…**

…is an impersonation report, filed separately from all of the above. It targets the account rather than a single upload.

If you are enrolled in Likeness Detection, the privacy and copyright routes both start from the Likeness tab instead of a blank form, with the video already attached.
