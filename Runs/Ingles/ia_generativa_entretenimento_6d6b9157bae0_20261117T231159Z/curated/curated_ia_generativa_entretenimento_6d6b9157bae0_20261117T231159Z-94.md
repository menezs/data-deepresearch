### Which Laws Protect Your Likeness Right Now?

Your state probably does, and creators overlook this while watching Congress.

**Most US states have long had right-of-publicity laws, which stop other people earning money from your name, face, or voice without permission**. Several have now been rewritten to say explicitly that an AI copy counts.

- **Tennessee's ELVIS Act** is the strongest of them for a cloned voice. It has been law since July 1, 2024, and despite the name, it protects every individual rather than only recording artists. Its important move is definitional: a sound the public recognises as you counts as your voice whether or not you ever made it,**which puts AI imitations inside the statute rather than in a grey area beside it** . Liability extends to whoever distributed the cloning tool. Damages triple where the defendant knew the clone was unauthorised.
- **California went after the paperwork.** Since January 1, 2025, a contract clause handing over rights to a digital replica of you is void there unless the agreement specifies how the replica will be used and you were represented by a lawyer or a union when you signed it. That is worth checking against any likeness clause a brand or production company has sent you. A companion law from the same date**bars AI recreations of deceased performers without estate consent, at $10,000 per violation or actual losses, whichever is greater.**

Whether any of it reaches your case depends on where you live, where the uploader lives, and where the harm landed. Establishing that takes a lawyer and several months. A takedown request to YouTube takes an afternoon, which is why it is almost always the first move.

## Frequently asked questions

### What is the right of publicity?

It is a state-law right to control commercial use of your identity, typically your name, image, likeness and voice. Using someone's identity to advertise or sell a product without written consent is the classic violation. News, commentary, parody and most expressive works are usually exempt.

### Is there a federal right of publicity?

No. As of November 9, 2026, the right exists only under state statutes and court decisions. The NO FAKES Act (S. 4591) would create a federal right covering digital replicas of voice and visual likeness; it was reported by Senate Judiciary in June 2026 but has not had a floor vote.

### Does the right of publicity cover AI voice clones?

In many states, yes. Tennessee's ELVIS Act expressly covers simulated voices, Illinois and California regulate digital replicas, and a New York federal court let voice actors pursue state publicity claims over AI voice clones in 2025. Whether a particular clone is actionable depends on the state and on commercial use.

### How long does the right of publicity last after death?

It depends on the state. California protects deceased personalities for 70 years (Senate Judiciary analysis), Illinois for 50 and New York for 40. Tennessee grants ten years and then continues the right while it is commercially used, ending it after two years of non-use. Some states recognize no post-mortem right at all.

### New York

New York's living right sits in Civil Rights Law §§ 50 and 51, which cover use of a person's name, portrait, picture or voice for advertising or trade without written consent; voice was added to § 51 in 1995, according to the Digital Media Law Project. ## Likeness rights and AI

AI does two things that strain older publicity law. It can imitate a voice or face convincingly without copying any particular recording, and it can produce that imitation at scale, often through tools run by someone other than the person who publishes the output. Courts adapted to the first problem decades ago: *Midler* held that imitating a famous singer's distinctive voice to sell a product can be actionable in California (849 F.2d 460). The newer statutes target the second problem by reaching distributors and tool makers.

### Voice cloning

A voice clone is covered wherever a state's statute or common law protects voice, which includes all four states in the table. The harder questions are about commercial purpose and who is liable. Tennessee reaches the maker of a tool built mainly for unauthorized replicas (Covington). Illinois reaches anyone who knowingly distributes a recording containing an unauthorized replica, and those who knowingly facilitate it (765 ILCS 1075/30). In *Lehrman v. Lovo*, the court held that New York's statute could reach AI voice clones used in New York for commercial purposes (Skadden).

### Digital replicas

"Digital replica" has become a defined legal term. Illinois defines it as a newly created electronic representation of a real person's voice, image or likeness, fixed in a recording or audiovisual work, that a reasonable person would believe is that individual (765 ILCS 1075/5). California's AB 1836 uses the same definition as AB 2602 with its own exceptions, according to the Daily Journal. Sexual deepfakes are mostly handled by separate criminal and civil laws rather than publicity law; see are deepfakes illegal and the federal TAKE IT DOWN Act.

### The federal NO FAKES Act

The NO FAKES Act of 2026 (S. 4591) would create a federal right to authorize digital replicas of one's voice and visual likeness that survives death, with statutory damages and a notice-and-takedown system for platforms. Senate Judiciary reported it in June 2026, but as of November 9, 2026 it has had no floor vote. As reported, it would preempt state digital replica laws enacted after January 2, 2025 while preserving earlier ones (reported text). Details are on our NO FAKES Act page.

## What is the right of publicity?

The right of publicity is the right to decide whether and how your identity is used for commercial purposes. Illinois states it plainly: its statute recognizes "the right to control and to choose whether and how to use an individual's identity for commercial purposes," and defines identity to include name, signature, photograph, image, likeness and voice (765 ILCS 1075/5 and 1075/10). Other states use similar lists. The right is usually violated by using someone's identity to sell or advertise a product without written consent; news reporting, commentary and most expressive works are generally exempt.

It differs from copyright and trademark. Copyright protects a creative work, such as a photograph or a recording, and belongs to the author; the right of publicity protects the person shown or heard in it. That gap is why AI voice clones fit poorly in federal law. In *Lehrman v. Lovo*, decided July 10, 2025, Judge J. Paul Oetken of the Southern District of New York dismissed most of two voice actors' federal trademark and copyright claims against an AI voice company but let their New York publicity claims proceed, as Skadden reported.

There is no federal right of publicity. Each state decides whether the right exists, what it covers, and whether it survives death. Some states have a statute, some recognize the right only in court decisions, and some have both. New York, for example, has only a statutory right, under Civil Rights Law §§ 50 and 51, and its courts have refused to recognize a separate common-law right, according to the Digital Media Law Project.

## Three Different Questions, Three Different Answers

Most confusion about AI voice cloning comes from collapsing three separate situations into one. They carry completely different legal weight, and separating them is the first thing any lawyer will do.

**Cloning your own voice.** No jurisdiction covered in this guide prohibits a person from making a synthetic model of their own voice. The constraints here are contractual rather than statutory, which is why recording, production, and management agreements matter more than any AI statute.

**Cloning a consenting artist under license.** This is the shape the majors settled into. Universal and Warner both moved from litigation to licensing deals with AI music companies during late 2025, a shift documented in our music industry AI lawsuits tracker. Consent plus a written scope of use is what turns a clone from a liability into a product.

**Cloning a real artist without consent.** This is where the law lives. It is also where the answer changes completely depending on the state, the country, and whether the artist is alive.

## Post mortem right of publicity

Whether the right survives death, and for how long, varies more than any other feature. California protects deceased personalities for 70 years (Senate Judiciary analysis), Illinois for 50 years (765 ILCS 1075/30(c)) and New York for 40 years (Civil Rights Law § 50-f(8)). Tennessee gives heirs an exclusive ten-year term after death, after which the right continues for as long as it is commercially used and ends after two years of non-use; the ELVIS Act counts the commercial availability of a recording as use (Tenn. Code § 47-25-1104, ELVIS Act redline).

Post-mortem rules matter most for AI because estates now license, and police, digital recreations of performers. New York's 2025 amendment replaced a disclaimer-based rule with a consent requirement for deceased performers' digital replicas in expressive works, according to Katten. California's AB 1836 does the same for audiovisual works and sound recordings (Cozen O'Connor). New York's post-mortem right applies only to people domiciled in the state when they died, so the law of a person's last home often decides whether an estate has any claim at all.

## Right of publicity examples

- **Sound-alike advertising.** Ford's agency hired a singer to imitate Bette Midler after she declined; the Ninth Circuit held the deliberate imitation of a distinctive voice to sell a product can be actionable (*Midler v. Ford* ).
- **AI voice clones sold as a service.** Two voice actors alleged an AI company cloned their voices and sold them to customers; their New York publicity and consumer protection claims survived dismissal in July 2025 (Skadden).
- **Synthetic performers in ads.** From June 9, 2026, an advertiser reaching New York audiences must disclose a known AI-generated synthetic performer (Lowenstein Sandler).
- **Recreating a deceased performer.** In California and New York, using a digital replica of a deceased performer in a film or recording now generally requires the estate's consent (Cozen O'Connor; Katten).

Uses that usually fall outside the right include news reporting, documentaries and biographies, commentary, parody and satire. Illinois lists these exemptions in detail, though it removes them for digital replicas that create a false impression the person actually took part (765 ILCS 1075/35).

## Europe: Copyright and Transparency, Not Personality

Europe has not given performers a voice right. It has given them two adjacent tools, and both landed in 2026.

The first is transparency. Article 50 of the EU AI Act applies from August 2, 2026 and requires deployers who create deepfake content to disclose it, with a lighter obligation where the work is evidently artistic, creative, satirical, or fictional, per the European Commission's guidance. We covered what this means for release workflows in our EU AI Act music enforcement guide.

The second is copyright. On July 31, 2026 Munich Regional Court I decided GEMA v Suno, prohibiting reproduction for training, memorization in the model, communication to the public, and reproduction through outputs across six compositions. Read what it does not do: the case turned on compositions, not on anybody's voice, and the judgment is first instance with an appeal signaled.

### What Happens After You File a Removal Request?

YouTube contacts the uploader first and gives them 48 hours to remove or edit the video. Creators read the silence in that window as a rejection and give up on the request, when the clock is simply running.

If the uploader does nothing, YouTube's team reviews the video independently. It can remove the video outright, blur your face and other identifying details, or strip identifying information out of the title, description, and metadata while the video stays up.

YouTube has published what its reviewers weigh.

Parody and satire cut against removal. So does a video where you are not uniquely identifiable. Public-official or well-known-figure status raises the bar rather than lowering it, and that one catches creators off guard — an audience puts you nearer the public-figure end than a private individual, so a request that only says "this is fake" tends to lose.

Specificity is what moves a request.

Give timestamps for every point where you appear, describe what the video claims you said or endorsed, and name the guideline it breaks rather than describing how it feels to watch. **Requests that read like evidence get treated like evidence.**

### What Can You Do About a Cloned Voice?

**File a privacy complaint.** YouTube's synthetic-likeness request has covered voice as well as face since November 2023, and that route still works even though Likeness Detection cannot find these videos for you. You will have to find the video yourself, and once you have it the complaint is the same one you would file over a face.

Musicians get a route of their own.

Labels and distributors representing artists in YouTube's AI music programme **can request removal of tracks that mimic an artist's singing or rapping voice, judged case by case and weighed against news reporting or critique of the synthetic vocals.**

State law reaches further on voice than most creators expect, and the section on state protections below covers what is already enforceable.
