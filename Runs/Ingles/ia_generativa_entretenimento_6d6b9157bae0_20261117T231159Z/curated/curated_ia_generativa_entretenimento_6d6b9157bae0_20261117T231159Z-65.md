Members of the Screen Actors Guild-American Federation of Television and Radio Artists union (SAG-AFTRA) have ratified new television animation contracts that strengthen protections for actors regarding the use of AI voice cloning.

SAG-AFTRA members approved the 2026 Television Animation Agreement and the 2026 Basic Cable Animation Agreement, which run through June 2030 and cover animated series, mini-series, and long-form movies produced for television, basic cable, and new media. In addition to protections from AI voices, the agreements also include gradual pay raises that total 12.6% over the term of the contract.

At the time of writing, the full Memorandum of Agreement (MoA) for the new contracts is not available on the SAG-AFTRA website. However, the announcement includes a summary of the tentative agreement describing the key changes.

What’s New?

A major win for voice actors is the reversal of an AI dubbing clause in the previous contract that had allowed producers to use a digital replica to dub performances into another language without the original actor’s consent. According to the union’s summary of the tentative agreement, the new consent requirement applies only to productions commencing principal photography on or after July 1, 2027.

When producers use an independently created digital replica (ICDR), the actor will now be entitled to at least their minimum session fee. An ICDR is a digital replica not created by the producer for their project, such as a voice clone an actor built independently. The original voice actor will also be entitled to residuals from the use of the ICDR if it is “used to create a performance that would qualify a natural voice actor for residuals.”

The new agreement strengthens actors’ protections against producers who want to use a synthetic voice instead of a human performer. A “synthetic” is defined as a voice created with generative AI that sounds like a performer but is not recognizable as any specific real person. Under the new contract, producers must notify the union and bargain over “appropriate considerations” when opting for a synthetic.

Producers will also commit to a “principle strongly favoring human performances,” and must show that a synthetic brings “significant additional value” compared with a digital replica. A digital replica could generate residuals for the original voice actor, whereas a synthetic is not based on any one person’s likeness. If producers cannot demonstrate that additional value, the union may seek damages in arbitration that are not limited to the fee a human actor would have earned.
