### **Outputs Remain a Separate Infringement Question**

Even if training qualifies as fair use, a particular output may still infringe. Copyright does not protect ideas, facts, general themes, or artistic “style” standing alone. Liability ordinarily requires copying protected expression and substantial similarity between the accused output and the copyrighted work. Exact or near-exact “regurgitation” presents the clearest risk; a derivative work that recasts protected characters, lyrics, images, code, or other expression can also create liability.

Early pleading decisions are illustrative. In *Tremblay v. OpenAI, Inc*., 716 F. Supp. 3d 772 (N.D. Cal. 2024), and an earlier order in *Kadrey*, claims based on allegedly infringing outputs were dismissed where the authors did not identify outputs substantially similar to their books. In *Andersen v. Stability AI Ltd*., 744 F. Supp. 3d 956 (N.D. Cal. 2024), by contrast, the court allowed core claims by visual artists to proceed into discovery and declined to resolve fair use on the pleadings. The technical operation of the models and evidence connecting training data to outputs remain central issues in the litigation.

The distinction between inputs and outputs is essential. A plaintiff may challenge the making or acquisition of training copies without proving that the model reproduces the work for users. A separate output claim generally requires comparison of the resulting work to protectable expression in the original.

#### **Introduction** 

Generally, in the common law, “secondary liability” is imposed on someone who does not commit the legal wrong directly, but is found responsible for encouraging, facilitating, or profiting from it. For there to be secondary liability, there needs to be underlying direct infringement. In copyright law, someone must directly violate the exclusive rights provided by § 106 before secondary liability can be imposed on any third party. 

As you will see, the Copyright Act has no provisions imposing secondary liability. (Compare to § 271 of the Patent Act.) As a result, the secondary liability provisions of copyright law are judicially developed, without even an open-ended statutory basis like that given to fair use jurisprudence under § 107. _Sony v. Universal_ (1984), the case you read in Chapter 13, was the Supreme Court’s landmark decision regarding copyright secondary liability and new technology. Twenty-one years later, in 2005, the Supreme Court revisited secondary liability and technology in _MGM v. Grokster_ . Then, another twenty-one years later, it significantly reinterpreted _Sony_ and _Grokster_ in _Cox Communications, Inc. v. Sony Music Entertainment_ (2026). This latest episode in the progressive rewriting of secondary liability by the courts concludes this chapter. 

As you learned in Chapter 13, _Sony_ established the safe harbor for technologies that are “capable of substantial, commercially significant noninfringing uses” and discussed two forms of secondary liability—contributory infringement and vicarious liability—though it did not clearly delineate between them. _Grokster_ then outlined another basis for secondary liability: inducement. It was unclear about whether inducement was a third form of secondary liability or a subset of contributory infringement, but it did distinguish inducement from the scenario in _Sony_ (“ _Sony_ dealt with a claim of liability based solely on distributing a product with alternative lawful and unlawful uses, with knowledge that some users would follow the unlawful course...MGM’s evidence in this case most obviously addresses a different basis of liability”). 

While Congress has not explicitly spelled out the bases for imposing secondary liability, it did implicitly address secondary liability in the 1998 Digital Millennium Copyright Act (“DMCA”) when it created a safe harbor that shielded qualifying online service providers from both direct and secondary liability. However, this was a statutory _defense_ from liability rather than a Congressional articulation of the underlying standard.

#### **How does copyright apply to AI-generated or AI-aided art?** 

As we noted in the Introduction, humans have long used technology to create – from the paintbrush to the camera to Photoshop or computer-generated imagery. In the latter set of works, the technology obviously plays a central role. In the past, copyright law has struggled with the question of how it should deal with these new, technologically-mediated creations. Initially, for example, courts said that photographs were not eligible for copyright because the work was created by a mere mechanical process. Congress amended the Copyright Act in 1865 to include photographs, but doubt remained. The Congressional authority to pass copyright laws comes from the Constitution and specifies that it is aimed at the “writings” of “authors.” Could a photographer be called an author? A photograph be called a writing? In a case involving a fetching photograph of Oscar Wilde, the Supreme Court found that the definition of writings could expand to fill new modes of artistic fixation and that, because the choices of lighting, framing, costume, and posing were made by the human photographer, photography could be a form of authorship. _Burrow-Giles Lithographic Co. v. Sarony_ (1884). How will we answer the same kinds of questions when the technology is generative AI? 

If a work is generated _entirely_ by AI, it is ineligible for copyright. The D.C. Circuit confirmed this in 2025, holding that the copyright statute required human authorship. _Thaler v. Perlmutter_ (D.C. Cir. 2025), _cert. denied_ (2026). Notably, the court said it did not need to go further and consider the argument, which had been made by the Copyright Office, that the Constitution itself required human authorship. If that argument were to be accepted it would mean that no mere statutory revision could change that conclusion; Congress could _never_ grant copyright to purely AI-generated works. Recent developments further illustrate that copyright protection for AI-assisted works is not necessarily an all-or-nothing proposition. In January 2025, the Copyright Office registered *A Single Piece of American Cheese*, but expressly excluded its AI-generated image components. The registration instead covered the human selection, coordination, and arrangement of those components. The decision demonstrates that a creator may obtain a relatively narrow copyright in the manner in which AI-generated elements are selected and assembled, even though the individual elements remain unprotected. Correspondingly, infringement will depend on whether the defendant copied that protectable human contribution, not merely whether the defendant copied some portion of the finished image.

The developing authorities also distinguish manual authorship from iterative prompting. Repeated prompts, even hundreds of them, may demonstrate substantial effort and creative intention without establishing that the user determined the output’s expressive details. *A stronger claim arises when the creator directly edits, redraws, crops, masks, positions, combines, or otherwise controls identifiable elements of the finished work*. Creators using AI should therefore preserve prompts, drafts, source files, editing layers, intermediate outputs, and other evidence identifying precisely what the human author contributed.

### **What the Copyright Office Says**

The Copyright Office’s May 2025 pre-publication report, Copyright and Artificial Intelligence, Part 3: Generative AI Training, rejected a categorical answer. It concluded that multiple stages of AI development may implicate copyright owners’ exclusive rights and that fair use depends on the works used, their source, the purpose of the use, and controls on outputs. Uses for analysis or research may be less likely to substitute for expressive works. Commercial use of vast quantities of copyrighted expression to produce competing content, especially through illegal access, may exceed established fair-use boundaries.

The Copyright Office recommended allowing voluntary licensing markets to develop before imposing a compulsory federal licensing scheme. That recommendation describes the market already emerging in publishing, news, music, images, and specialized data. It also reinforces a practical point: the absence of a definitive court ruling does not make licensing irrelevant. A negotiated license can allocate rights, warranties, attribution, compensation, data security, output restrictions, and responsibility for infringement more predictably than litigation.
