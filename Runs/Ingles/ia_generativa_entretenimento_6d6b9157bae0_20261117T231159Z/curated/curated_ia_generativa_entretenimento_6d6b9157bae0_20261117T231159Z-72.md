# **A Bajillion Prompts and Counting**

The case that’s going to define the line—the one *Thaler* was always too extreme to draw—is *Allen v. Perlmutter*, sitting right now in the District of Colorado.

Jason Allen created an image called “Théâtre D’opéra Spatial” using Midjourney. He didn’t type one prompt and call it a day. He entered prompts at least 624 times. He specified genre, tone, color palette, composition, and style. He used Midjourney’s variation and upscaling tools. He cleaned up the final image in Photoshop. The image won first place at the Colorado State Fair.

The Copyright Office still said no; their argument was that prompts are “instructions that convey unprotectable ideas.” The AI—not Allen—made the expressive choices. Proof? Run the same prompt twice and you get different images. If you can’t predict or control the specific output, you’re not the author of that output. It doesn’t matter if you prompted 624 times or 6,024 times. Effort isn’t authorship.

Allen fired back: that conflates the idea/expression dichotomy with the authorship question. When he specified the scene, the genre, the tone, the color relationships, the composition, the finish—through hundreds of iterative decisions—he wasn’t just throwing ideas at a wall. He was making specific expressive choices reflected in the final work. The fact that the tool’s output varies doesn’t negate the human direction any more than the unpredictability of watercolors negates a painter’s authorship.

This is the fight that matters. And it’s the fight the Supreme Court left for everyone else to figure out.

## 08Questions people ask

### Is the Copyright Office's AI guidance legally binding?

It binds how the Office examines applications, but courts make the final call on copyright law. So far, in Thaler v. Perlmutter on March 18, 2025, the D.C. Circuit agreed with the Office that a human author is required.

### Has the Copyright Office registered any works made with AI?

Yes. In its December 11, 2023 SURYAST decision, the Review Board noted that the Office had granted about 100 registrations in 2023 for works containing AI material, with that material disclaimed.

### Do I have to name the AI tool I used?

No. In its September 5, 2023 decision on Théâtre D'opéra Spatial, the Review Board said applicants are not required to list the AI tools used; a brief description of the AI-generated content is enough.

### Where can I read the reports myself?

On the Copyright Office's own site. Its Copyright and Artificial Intelligence page at copyright.gov/ai links all three parts of the report, the March 16, 2023 guidance, the Review Board decisions and the court filings in Thaler and Allen.

### Has a court ruled on whether prompting counts as authorship?

Not yet. The test case is Allen v. Perlmutter in federal court in Colorado, about a Midjourney image made with 624 or more prompts. Briefing closed on February 27, 2026, and the docket showed no ruling when we checked it on November 1, 2026. Until a court rules, the Copyright Office's position applies: prompts alone are not authorship.

## 05What the courts have added

# **The Line Nobody’s Drawing**

Here’s what we do have. At least a century of copyrighted works with very basic rules behind how they became copyrighted. You want to throw a baseball? Here’s a 7-step process to go through before it hits home plate within 30 seconds. You want to play guitar? Okay, wait—guitar’s a thing I actually still don’t know how to do.

Here’s what we don’t have. We don’t have a bright-line rule for how much human involvement makes an AI-assisted work copyrightable. We don’t have guidance on whether specifying “syncopated trap beat, 140 BPM, dark minor key” in an AI composition tool constitutes authorship of the resulting drum pattern. We don’t know if generating thirty versions and selecting the best one is a creative act or just a slot machine pull. We don’t know if manually adjusting the velocity and timing of AI-generated notes tips the balance. And we won’t know until either *Allen v. Perlmutter* resolves, another case works its way through the system, or Congress decides to act—which, given the current legislative landscape, is roughly as likely as Thaler getting his copyright after all.

The Copyright Office has tried. They’ve drawn a line between AI as a “tool” and AI as a “substitute for human creativity.” That’s a reasonable principle. But principles without precision are cold comfort when you’re trying to register a song, close a sync deal, or negotiate a publishing agreement. The *Zarya of the Dawn* decision showed the limits: a graphic novel combining human-authored text with Midjourney images got copyright for the text and the compilation, but not for the individual images. Translation for humans: you can copyright the human parts. But the AI-generated elements—the ones you might have spent hours shaping through prompts and iterations—might fall straight into the public domain.

### What This Decision Leaves Unresolved

The D.C. Circuit noted that the human authorship requirement does not prohibit copyrighting work "made by or with the assistance of artificial intelligence" – only that the author must be a human, not the machine itself. *Thaler v. Perlmutter*, 130 F.4th 1039, 1049 (D.C. Cir. 2025). Because Dr. Thaler disclaimed any human creative input, the court did not address how much human involvement is sufficient to support a copyright claim for AI-assisted works.

That question, however, is presented in another case pending in Colorado, *Allen v. Perlmutter*, where an artist challenges the Copyright Office's denial of registration for an image generated using Midjourney with more than 600 iterative prompts. The Copyright Office's 2023 Registration Guidance acknowledges that works incorporating AI-generated material may contain sufficient human authorship to support registration where a human selects or arranges AI-generated elements creatively.

### Practical Recommendations for Businesses

Businesses using GAI should consider the following strategies:

1. **Embed****meaningful human creativity** : The current framework rewards identifiable human contributions to the conception, selection, arrangement, and editing of AI outputs. Simply pressing "generate" is unlikely to produce copyrightable output.

### Conclusion

*Thaler v. Perlmutter* addresses a narrow but foundational question: under current interpretations of the Copyright Act, an AI system cannot be listed as the author of a copyrightable work. But the harder questions – about where human creativity ends and machine autonomy begins – remain open. Congress may revisit these issues as AI evolves, and courts will have opportunities to refine the boundaries of human authorship. Businesses should design AI workflows around human creativity, maintain thorough documentation, and monitor developments in *Allen v. Perlmutter* and future Copyright Office guidance.

## 03Part 2: copyrightability

Part 2, published January 29, 2025, is the one most creators need. It addresses whether AI outputs can be copyrighted. Its main conclusions, as the Office summarized them:

- Questions of AI and copyrightability can be resolved under existing law, without new legislation.
- Using AI to assist, rather than stand in for, human creativity does not affect protection.
- Copyright protects a human's original expression even if the work also includes AI material.
- Copyright does not extend to purely AI-generated material, or material where a person had too little control over the expressive elements.
- Based on current generally available technology, prompts alone do not provide enough control.

### The D.C. Circuit's Decision

On March 18, 2025, a D.C. Circuit panel affirmed the district court's ruling. Although the Copyright Act does not explicitly define "author," the court found that multiple provisions make clear that authors must be human beings. Ownership provisions assume the author can hold property; duration provisions measure terms by the author's lifespan; joint authorship requires intent; and registration requires a signature – all capacities only humans possess. The court also noted that Congress adopted the Copyright Office's longstanding human authorship requirement when enacting the 1976 Act.

The court rejected Dr. Thaler's alternative arguments. His work-made-for-hire claim failed because that doctrine presupposes the underlying work is copyrightable – which requires human authorship. His argument that he should be recognized as author by virtue of creating the AI was waived for not being raised during the administrative proceedings. The court acknowledged, however, that Congress could revisit these questions if AI evolution renders the human authorship requirement counterproductive.

### Supreme Court Denies Review

Dr. Thaler petitioned for certiorari, arguing that the lower courts' decisions created a "chilling effect on anyone else considering using AI creatively." The Department of Justice opposed, emphasizing that this case was a poor vehicle for broader questions about AI-assisted authorship because Dr. Thaler consistently disclaimed any human creative contribution. The Court denied certiorari on March 2, 2026, concluding this particular litigation, though the broader legal landscape remains in flux.

## 01The March 2023 registration guidance

On March 16, 2023, the Office published a statement of policy in the Federal Register: Copyright Registration Guidance: Works Containing Material Generated by Artificial Intelligence (88 FR 16190). It took effect the same day and is still the Office's main rule for applicants.

### The test

The Office asks whether a work is "basically one of human authorship, with the computer merely being an assisting instrument," or whether its traditional elements of authorship were produced by a machine. It called this "necessarily a case-by-case inquiry."

### The duty to disclose

The guidance says applicants "have a duty to disclose the inclusion of AI-generated content" in a work they submit, with a brief explanation of what the human author contributed. AI material that is more than de minimis (more than trivial) should be excluded from the claim. Applicants should not list an AI tool, or the company behind it, as an author or co-author.

### Fixing older filings

The guidance also explains how to correct a pending application or an issued registration that left AI material out. It warns that applicants who do not correct the record "risk losing the benefits of the registration." Step-by-step instructions for each field are on our page about how to complete the AI disclosure on a copyright application.
