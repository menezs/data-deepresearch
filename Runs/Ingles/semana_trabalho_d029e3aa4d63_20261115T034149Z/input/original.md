# Four-day week trials deliver wellbeing, not proof

The four-day workweek, in its most-studied form, cuts working time by about a fifth while keeping pay whole and asking for unchanged output. The best available evidence shows that it reliably improves employee wellbeing at the firms that volunteer to try it. The strongest study, a 2025 *Nature Human Behaviour* paper by Wen Fan, Juliet Schor, Orla Kelly and Guolin Gu, followed 2,896 employees at 141 trial and 12 control organizations and found significant falls in burnout and gains in job satisfaction and in mental and physical health, which persisted at 12 months.[1] The business claims that dominate headlines, such as rising revenue and steep drops in sick days and resignations, come largely from self-reported data compiled by advocacy groups and their academic partners.[2] The most rigorous controlled pilot, in Germany, found no significant change in revenue, profit or sick days, which reads as "no harm" rather than "gain."[3] Governments have mostly chosen soft instruments such as rights to request, public-sector schemes, subsidies and pilots. The biggest statutory change of 2026 was Brazil's constitutional amendment cutting the maximum week from 44 to 40 hours, on a five-day schedule rather than a four-day one.[4] As of November 2026, the four-day week is a durable but narrow practice: well supported on wellbeing, unproven as an economy-wide productivity policy, and spreading mainly through variants such as the nine-day fortnight and the 4.5-day week.

## A fifth less time for the same pay, in theory

"Four-day week" names two different policies, and much confusion in the debate comes from mixing them. The model that most pilots tested is known as 100-80-100: workers keep 100% of their pay for 80% of their time in exchange for maintaining 100% of their output. Fan describes it as "not a compressed workweek," but one that "involves genuine reductions in work hours without reductions in pay."[1] The alternative, compressed hours, packs the same weekly hours into fewer and longer days. The day off is paid for with longer shifts, and work can spill into the fifth day. Fortune's review of failed schemes concluded that the most successful trials used reduced hours at the same pay rather than compression.[5]

Even "reduced-hours" pilots are less uniform than their branding suggests. In Portugal's government-backed trial, 51% of firms gave one day off a week and 49% chose a nine-day fortnight.[6] In Germany, many organizations adopted "more conservative or adapted versions" of 100-80-100, and nearly 40% piloted the scheme in selected teams only.[7] Iceland's celebrated trials were not four-day weeks at all: they moved public-sector staff from 40 to 35 or 36 hours.[8]

The five-day week is itself a 20th-century invention: Ford adopted it in 1926, and the Fair Labor Standards Act of 1938 phased in a federal 40-hour standard.[9] Supporters make a similar argument today: the 2024 Senate hearing on a 32-hour bill was titled "Workers Should Benefit from New Technology and Increased Productivity: The Need for a 32-Hour Work Week with No Loss in Pay."[10] Skeptics, such as the Lithuanian experts quoted by LRT, reply that productivity must rise first.[11]

## Advocacy-run pilots report stable revenue and sharply lower burnout

Most pilot data come from 4 Day Week Global (4DWG) and allied advocacy groups such as Autonomy and the 4 Day Week Foundation, working with university researchers. The UK pilot of 2022, the largest and best documented, ran from June to December 2022 with 61 companies and about 2,900 employees, and its results appeared in February 2023.[12] It launched with 70 companies and 3,300 workers, so some participants dropped out or were reclassified before the final analysis.[13] Revenue was broadly unchanged, rising 1.4% on average over the trial. In addition, 71% of employees reported lower burnout, sick days fell 65%, and the likelihood of an employee quitting fell 57%. At the end of the pilot, at least 56 of the 61 companies continued, and 18 called the change permanent.[12] A year later, at least 54 firms (89%) still ran a four-day week and 31 (51%) had made it permanent. Schor noted that the gains in job satisfaction and sleep had "nudged down a bit" from six months.[14]

Results elsewhere point the same way. The US and Ireland pilot covered 33 companies and more than 900 employees, over half of the firms with 10 or fewer staff. Revenue rose about 8% over the trial, and 97% of employees wanted to continue. The same report found that 20% of workers had higher burnout scores and 52% felt the pace of their work had increased.[15] A year after the North American trials, none of 41 US and Canadian companies planned to return to five days, and average weekly hours had fallen from 38 to 32.97.[13] South Africa's 2023 pilot reported burnout down 57%, average revenue up 10.5%, and 92% of companies intending to continue.[16] Portugal's trial, covering 41 companies and more than 1,000 workers, cut weekly hours including overtime from 41.6 to 36.5. The share of workers finding it hard to balance work and family fell from 50% to 16%, 80% of managers rated the trial financially neutral, and only 4 companies returned to a five-day week.[6] A 2025 UK cohort of 17 organizations and more than 1,000 employees ended with all 17 keeping reduced hours, 5 of them as a nine-day fortnight. That figure comes from a left-leaning outlet repeating the 4 Day Week Foundation's own numbers.[17]

Two widely cited cases deserve extra caution. Microsoft Japan's "Work Life Choice Challenge" closed its offices on Fridays for one month in 2019, and sales per employee rose 39.9% compared with August 2018. That result comes from a single firm and a single month with no controls.[18] Valencia's month-long 2023 city trial for 360,000 workers reported stress down 35% but a slight rise in tobacco and alcohol use, with no independent evaluator named.[19]

These numbers do not add up to a business case. The revenue figures are not comparable across pilots, are not adjusted for inflation or market trends, and come from firms that chose to share data. In the UK pilot, the widely quoted 35% year-on-year revenue increase rests on the 23 of 61 companies that reported sales data, and SHRM noted that it is unclear whether the four-day week caused it.[20]

## Controlled studies confirm the wellbeing gains but not the business case

Fan and colleagues' paper, published in July 2025, is the field's main peer-reviewed result.[21] It analyzed employees in the US, Canada, the UK, Ireland, Australia and New Zealand. Trial employees cut about five hours a week while hours at control firms stayed stable. Burnout fell significantly at trial firms with no change among controls. Larger cuts in hours produced larger gains, and the effects held at 12 months, with no differences by industry, gender, parental status or education. Better sleep, less fatigue and higher self-rated work ability explained much of the effect.[1] The dose-response pattern and year-long persistence are the strongest arguments against a mere novelty effect.

The paper's limits matter just as much. The firms volunteered, outcomes were self-reported, the 12 controls were interested firms that did not implement, the sample leaned toward Anglophone professional services and non-profits, and workers had an incentive to overstate benefits to keep the schedule. The paper reports no company-level outcomes; Fan referred only to unpublished observations on revenue, resignations and sick days, and said that "the optimal design would be a truly group-randomized trial."[1] Because it analyzes the same 4DWG pilot populations behind the advocacy reports, it is a more rigorous treatment of the same data, not independent confirmation.

Germany's 2024 pilot, run by the consultancy Intraprenör with the University of Münster, used the strongest measurement design so far. It combined three survey waves with smartwatch data, hair-cortisol samples, organizational metrics and internal control groups across 41 organizations. Measured daily stress minutes were significantly lower than among controls, and sleep rose by 38 minutes a week. Productivity was the same or moderately higher, but the small rises in revenue and profit and the slight fall in sick days were not statistically significant.[3] Two years on, 70% of the German organizations kept some form of reduced working time.[7] Portugal's evaluators also compared participants with a control group and found significant falls in exhaustion and negative mental-health symptoms. The same report contains a useful clue about durability: companies that made two or more organizational changes had an 8% chance of reverting to five days, against 38% for those that made one change or none.[6] No published randomized controlled trial of a four-day workweek was found.

The best-identified evidence on cutting days comes from a neighboring policy, the four-day school week, which has spread across the rural US West and Midwest. RAND researchers studying six states concluded that "by and large, a four-day school week causes student achievement to suffer." The yearly losses are small but accumulate to roughly pandemic-scale learning losses over eight years.[22] Later studies found the harm concentrated in non-rural schools and in schools with the least instructional time, and a University of Oregon review of 11 high-quality studies found "no evidence of large positive effects."[23] Schools are not offices, but the transferable lesson is that cutting time without redesigning work costs output.

## Governments prefer rights, pilots and subsidies over mandates

Iceland is the template advocates cite. After government-run trials in 2015–2019 covering about 2,500 workers, unions negotiated shorter hours for tens of thousands of members.[8] An estimated 86% of the workforce now works shorter hours or has the right to.[24] The change came through collective bargaining, not statute. Daiga Kamerade of the University of Salford cautioned that public-sector conditions may not transfer to the private sector.[8]

Belgium shows the limits of a legal right to compressed hours. Since November 2022, full-time private-sector employees can ask to fit their weekly hours into four days, but only about 0.52% had done so by November 2023, and a quarter of employers surveyed considered the arrangement inappropriate or harmful.[25] By 2025, uptake had reached only about 0.8% of employees and 3% of companies.[11]

Gulf and East Asian governments have moved through their own workforces and through incentives. Sharjah moved its public sector to a permanent Monday-to-Thursday week, the UAE federal government adopted a 4.5-day week in January 2022, and Dubai ran summer-only compressed schedules in 2024 and 2025.[26] A Sharjah government study reported traffic accidents and deaths down "by up to 40 per cent," though most of its other claims came without numbers.[27] Since April 2025, Tokyo's metropolitan government has let staff take one weekday off a week through flexible hours rather than a cut in total hours, a policy framed around careers and family life.[28] South Korea's government under President Lee Jae-myung adopted a 4.5-day-week roadmap: a support law with tax credits and pilots in 2025, curbs on the comprehensive wage system in 2026, and national dialogue from 2027. The Korea Employers Federation objected that hourly labor productivity was only 77.4% of the OECD average.[29] By January 2026, several major banks were testing a Friday finish one hour early.[30] A secondary expat-news site reports subsidies for smaller firms from that month; passage of the support act is unconfirmed.[31]

Legislative attempts to cut statutory hours have mostly failed. Spain's Congress rejected a bill to reduce the legal week from 40 to 37.5 hours by 178 votes to 170 on 10 September 2025.[32] By March 2026 there was no timetable to revive it.[33] The Labour Ministry turned instead to a time-recording decree, still unfinished in July 2026.[34] In the United States, Sen. Bernie Sanders and Rep. Mark Takano introduced the Thirty-Two Hour Workweek Act in March 2024, with endorsements from the AFL-CIO, UAW and SEIU.[10] They reintroduced it in September 2026 in the 119th Congress, where it would make work beyond 32 hours a week eligible for overtime.[35] At the state level, California's AB 2932, which would have required overtime after 32 hours at larger companies, stalled in 2022.[36] Maryland's 2023 bill offered tax credits only for real cuts to 32 hours, excluding four 10-hour days.[37]

The United Kingdom illustrates how politics shapes experimentation. South Cambridgeshire District Council began a 32-hour trial in 2023 and was placed under a Best Value Notice by the Conservative government, which also warned that funding could be affected. An academic analysis found performance improved or held in 22 of 24 service areas, and in November 2024 the Labour government dropped the notice. It also stated that a general move to a four-day week is "not government policy."[38] The council voted to make the arrangement permanent in July 2025.[39] Scotland's year-long 32-hour pilot at two agencies with 259 staff improved self-assessed mental health and cut sick days taken for psychological reasons.[40] Even so, the public finance minister said in August 2025 that the government "will not be moving to a 32-hour week."[41] Poland's labor ministry funded a pilot of shorter working time. Headlines report about 90 employers and more than 5,000 employees taking part, with average funding of PLN 0.5 million per employer, but the primary pages could not be checked.[42] One Polish headline said the first findings "give pause for thought," without detail that could be verified.[43]

Brazil made the largest legal change. The Senate approved the amendment ending the six-days-on, one-off schedule by 75 votes to 1 on 14 October 2026, and DIEESE estimates that about 26.8 million workers will benefit.[44] Promulgated as Constitutional Amendment No. 141/2026 on 16 October, it cuts the cap to 42 hours after 60 days and to 40 hours a year later, under a five-day schedule. Collective agreements can still allow other arrangements, including six-day schedules with compensatory rest.[4] Elsewhere the direction is reversed. Germany's coalition is drafting a weekly rather than daily cap on working hours.[45] Greece has allowed six-day, 48-hour weeks in some industries since July 2024.[5] In Lithuania, the prime minister has called a shorter week the country's future, but no bill has been reported.[11]

## Employers split along the line between reduced and compressed hours

Interest outstrips practice. SHRM's 2023 benefits survey found 9% of US organizations had implemented a four-day week.[20] A 2025 Tech.co survey of senior leaders found that openness rose from 23% to 38% in a year, while the share of businesses actually using a four-day week fell from 17% to 14%.[46] In early 2024, 30% of large-company US CEOs surveyed by KPMG said they were exploring schedules such as a four- or 4.5-day week.[47] Employer enthusiasm often means compression: 45% of senior managers in a Robert Half Canada survey favored four 10-hour days.[20]

The reversals cluster where compression meets shift work or constant client demand. Asda tried 44 hours over four days, which meant 11-hour shifts that staff called "physically demanding," with knock-on problems for public transport and childcare. It switched to a flexible 39-hour, five-day week. The insurer Domestic & General found that a condensed week left some staff psychologically exhausted and suited some teams but not others.[5] A trade magazine reports that Cooper Parry ended a three-year trial in 2026 because the model could not be applied fairly across client-facing teams, and that Magyar Telekom ended its trial after uneven gains and scheduling bottlenecks. Neither account has primary confirmation.[48]

Bolt made its policy permanent in 2022 after 94% of workers wanted it to continue, a self-reported figure from a tech firm of about 550 staff.[49] Lamborghini's December 2023 union deal gave production workers 22 to 31 extra days off a year with no pay cut, alongside 500 new jobs.[50] Together with Portugal's reversion data, these cases suggest the four-day week lasts where firms redesign work rather than squeeze the same hours into fewer days.

## Critics target self-selection, self-reports and who benefits

The sharpest critique concerns identification. The economist Daniel Hamermesh said the 4DWG survey "merely demonstrates that people are willing to participate in the experiment" and that he would give it "very little, if any, weight." Schor herself called randomized trials the "gold standard" while asking, "how do you get companies to agree to submit to RCTs?" She also conceded that many firms lack productivity data and that the results are "self-reports of companies saying this innovation is working." Dean Baker, an economist sympathetic to shorter hours, called a 25% productivity gain achieved overnight "impossibly great."[2] Firms that expect to fail do not volunteer, and workers who want to keep their day off have reason to report success. Continuation rates near 90% therefore describe self-selected firms, not employers in general.

Economic critics raise questions of distribution. A February 2026 Institute of Economic Affairs paper argued that no central authority could measure the productivity effects of four-day or compressed weeks, that the benefits would favor higher-paid professionals and public-sector workers, and that the costs would surface as slower wage growth, fewer jobs and higher prices. Its supporting figure, wages 2% to 7% lower after the pandemic in remote-capable jobs, concerns remote work, not the four-day week.[51] The sample skew in Fan's study toward white, college-educated office workers supports the narrower point that the evidence comes mostly from knowledge work.[1]

Intensification is a quieter risk. The finding that 52% of US and Irish participants felt their pace increased, and that a fifth reported more burnout, suggests the gains are not evenly spread.[15] Public-sector successes show strain where output is tied to hours. South Cambridgeshire's two lagging areas were rent collection and the time needed to re-let housing.[38] Scottish agency SOSE's three-day response rate slipped from 94% to 92%.[40] Environmental co-benefits are also unproven; Germany's pilot found none.[3]

## Outlook for late 2026: a durable niche, spreading slowly

The evidence base has shifted from headline pilots to consolidation. 4DWG's press list ends with the launch of a Swiss pilot in March 2024, and no Swiss results were found.[13] Adoption is creeping rather than surging: more than 200 UK employers, covering about 5,000 staff, had permanently adopted a four-day week by 2025.[17] The momentum is in hybrids such as nine-day fortnights, 4.5-day weeks and Friday early finishes, which are easier to staff and easier for governments to subsidize than full 100-80-100 models.

Over the next year, the main tests will be Brazil's move to 42 and then 40 hours, Korea's planned national dialogue in 2027, and any published results from Poland's pilot.[4][29] A group-randomized trial with company-level outcomes remains the missing piece. Until one is published, claims of productivity gains rest on self-reported data, while the claim of no harm to output is increasingly credible for self-selected knowledge-work firms.

## Conclusion

The debate has narrowed. Whether shorter weeks make workers happier is largely settled, since controlled data say they do for at least a year. The open question is whether firms outside the volunteer pool can capture those gains without losing output. The evidence points to work redesign, not the day off itself, as the active ingredient: firms that made several organizational changes kept the policy far more often, while those that compressed hours or applied the model unevenly dropped it.

For policymakers, the evidence implies that mandates are premature and rights to request compressed hours change little, as Belgium's near-zero uptake shows. Funded pilots and subsidies that require participants to share data, as Maryland's stalled bill proposed, are the instruments most likely to produce both adoption and the randomized evidence the field still lacks. Brazil's amendment shows where hours policy has real political traction in 2026: in cutting long statutory weeks, not in reaching four days.
