# Europe Recalibrates While Brazil's Bill Waits

As of 14 November 2026, the European Union and Brazil stand at very different points on the same regulatory path. The EU's Artificial Intelligence Act, Regulation (EU) 2024/1689, has been in force since 1 August 2024 and is now largely enforceable, but its first major amendment, the Digital Omnibus on AI (Regulation (EU) 2026/1744), pushed its core high-risk obligations back to December 2027 and August 2028.[1][2] Brazil's Bill PL 2338/2023, which closely mirrors the EU's risk-based architecture, passed the Senate in December 2024 but has sat in a special committee of the Chamber of Deputies for twenty months with no rapporteur's report, no substitute text and no vote.[3][4] The two texts share a definition of AI, banned uses, a high-risk list and a tier for powerful general-purpose models.[5] They diverge on who bears obligations, what rights individuals hold, how copyright is treated, and how hard sanctions bite.[6][7] Both have been shaped by industry lobbying, US pressure and a growing argument that rules written in 2021–2024 should be slowed before they apply.[8][9] The result is that Brazil is debating whether to copy a European model that Europe itself has just recalibrated.

## The EU built a four-tier law in three years

The Commission proposed the AI Act on 21 April 2021, the Council adopted its general approach on 6 December 2022, and the Council and Parliament reached a provisional agreement on 9 December 2023 after three days of talks.[1] Parliament approved the text on 13 March 2024 by 523 votes to 46, with 49 abstentions.[10] The December 2023 compromise had weakened Parliament's original plans for general-purpose AI, after France and other member states pushed for a carve-out to protect home-grown start-ups such as Mistral.[10] The Council gave final approval on 21 May 2024, and the Regulation, dated 13 June 2024, appeared in the Official Journal on 12 July 2024.[1][11] Under Article 113 it entered into force on the twentieth day after publication, which was 1 August 2024.[2][1]

The Act defines an AI system as "a machine-based system that is designed to operate with varying levels of autonomy."[2] On that definition it builds four tiers: unacceptable risk (banned), high risk (strict obligations), transparency risk, and minimal risk such as spam filters and AI-enabled video games, which carries no obligations.[12] Article 5 prohibits harmful manipulation, exploitation of vulnerabilities, social scoring, crime prediction based solely on profiling, untargeted facial-image scraping, emotion inference in workplaces and schools, biometric categorization to infer sensitive traits, and, with narrow exceptions, real-time remote biometric identification by police in public spaces.[2] These eight prohibitions have applied since February 2025.[12]

High-risk systems come in two kinds. Article 6(1) and Annex I cover AI that is a safety component of, or is itself, a product regulated by EU harmonization law, while Article 6(2) and Annex III list stand-alone uses in eight areas: biometrics, critical infrastructure, education, employment, access to essential services, law enforcement, migration and border control, and the administration of justice and democratic processes.[2] An Article 6(3) filter lets providers treat an Annex III system as not high-risk if it performs only a narrow procedural or preparatory task, although systems that profile natural persons are always high-risk.[2] Providers of high-risk systems must manage risk, use high-quality datasets, log activity, document the system, ensure human oversight, and meet robustness, cybersecurity and accuracy standards.[12] Conformity assessment is mostly self-assessment: only biometric systems may involve a notified body, and the other Annex III areas use internal control.[2] Public bodies, private providers of public services, and deployers of credit-scoring and life and health insurance pricing systems must also carry out a fundamental rights impact assessment before first use.[2] Article 50 adds transparency duties: people must know when they are interacting with AI, synthetic outputs must carry machine-readable marks, and deployers must disclose deepfakes.[2]

General-purpose AI (GPAI) received its own chapter. All GPAI providers must keep technical documentation, inform downstream providers, adopt a copyright policy and publish a summary of training content, and models trained with more than 10^25 floating-point operations are presumed to pose systemic risk, which triggers adversarial testing, risk mitigation, incident reporting and cybersecurity duties.[2] These obligations have applied since 2 August 2025, and models placed on the market before that date have until 2 August 2027 to comply.[2][13] The Commission published a voluntary GPAI Code of Practice on 10 July 2025, missing the 2 May 2025 statutory deadline.[13] As of October 2026 the Commission lists 22 full signatories, including Amazon, Anthropic, Google, Microsoft, Mistral AI and OpenAI, while xAI signed only the Safety and Security chapter.[14] Meta does not appear on the list, but the sources reviewed for this report do not confirm the widely reported claim that it declined to sign.[14]

Enforcement is split between the Commission's AI Office, which oversees GPAI with support from an AI Board of member-state representatives and an independent scientific panel, and national market surveillance authorities that member states had to designate by 2 August 2025.[2][13] Fines reach €35 million or 7% of worldwide annual turnover for prohibited practices, €15 million or 3% for most other breaches including Article 50, and €7.5 million or 1% for misleading information, with the lower figure applying to SMEs.[2] The Commission may fine GPAI providers up to 3% or €15 million, a power that took effect on 2 August 2026.[2][12] The research found no reports of any fine imposed under the Act as of November 2026.

## The 2026 Omnibus delayed high-risk rules but added a ban

The Act's first major amendment arrived before most of its high-risk regime had applied. In July 2025 more than 40 European companies, including ASML, Philips, Siemens and Mistral, asked Commission President von der Leyen for a "two-year clock-stop" on high-risk and GPAI obligations.[15] The Commission proposed the Digital Omnibus on AI on 19 November 2025.[16][12] The Council agreed its position on 13 March 2026, negotiators reached a provisional agreement on 7 May 2026, Parliament adopted the text on 16 June, and the Council gave final approval on 29 June.[1][13] Regulation (EU) 2026/1744 was published on 24 July 2026 and entered into force on 27 July 2026.[17][18]

The core change is a fixed delay. Stand-alone Annex III high-risk rules now apply from 2 December 2027 instead of 2 August 2026, and product-embedded Annex I rules from 2 August 2028 instead of 2 August 2027.[18][19] The GPAI provisions kept their original timetable.[17] The delay was therefore shorter and narrower than industry had requested.[15][17] The Register reported that the deal followed pressure from "both Washington and European industry" and called it "a notable rollback."[8] The Commission insisted the delay concerned timing, because standards and guidance were not ready, rather than a watering down of the rules.[8]

The Omnibus also added protections. A new prohibition covers AI that generates non-consensual intimate images of identifiable people, including "nudification," and child sexual abuse material; it applies from 2 December 2026.[2][18] The Register links this ban to the backlash over xAI's Grok.[8] The other transparency duties, including disclosure of AI interaction and deepfake labeling, were not delayed and have applied since 2 August 2026.[17] The machine-readable marking duty of Article 50(2) for generative systems already on the market received a grace period ending on 2 December 2026, but sources disagree on its length: the Council says it was cut from six months to three, while K&L Gates describes a four-month grace period.[18][17] The end date is consistent; the length remains unresolved.

Other changes rebalance the Act. Small mid-caps with fewer than 750 employees and turnover up to €150 million now receive SME-style benefits such as simplified documentation and priority sandbox access, and a new Article 4a allows processing of special-category data where strictly necessary to detect and correct bias.[13] The AI Office gained exclusive supervision of AI systems built on GPAI models by the same provider, and it now acts as market surveillance authority for AI in very large online platforms and search engines, with periodic penalty payments of up to 5% of average daily turnover.[17][13] The deadline for national regulatory sandboxes moved to 2 August 2027.[2] According to The Register, providers claiming exemption from high-risk classification must still register in the EU database, but this report could not verify that point against the final text.[8]

EDRi said the proposal "tears the AI Act apart," delays high-risk requirements "without a clear timeline," and gives providers "carte blanche" to exempt themselves.[20] The Council framed the reform as making the EU more competitive while protecting fundamental values.[18] As of 14 November 2026, then, the prohibitions, AI literacy, GPAI duties, governance, penalties and most of Article 50 apply, while the new ban and the end of the Article 50(2) grace period arrive in December 2026 and the high-risk regime remains a year or more away.[2]

## Brazil's bill moved from principles to risk tiers

Brazil began with a much lighter text. On 29 September 2021 the Chamber of Deputies approved PL 21/2020 by 413 votes to 15, a bill of OECD-inspired principles that critics said set ethical guidelines rather than binding rules.[21] In February 2022 Senate President Rodrigo Pacheco formed a commission of jurists to draft an alternative.[22] On 6 December 2022 the commission presented a report of more than 900 pages with substitute texts for PL 5051/2019, PL 21/2020 and PL 872/2021, proposing a risk-based approach combined with a rights-based model and a federal authority to oversee enforcement.[23] Pacheco introduced PL 2338/2023 on 3 May 2023.[24] Its official summary states that it "provides for the development, promotion, and ethical and responsible use of artificial intelligence based on the centrality of the human person."[4]

The Senate process was long and contested. A December 2023 substitute from Senator Marcos Pontes reportedly stripped the chapters on affected persons' rights, governance and algorithmic impact assessments, which Mendes and Kira called a "setback."[5] A temporary Senate commission (CTIA) then worked on the bill for 19 months, holding 24 meetings and 14 public hearings.[22] It approved rapporteur Eduardo Gomes's (PL-TO) substitute on 5 December 2024, and the plenary approved the same text by symbolic vote on 10 December 2024, after more than 500 days of debate.[3] More than 40 amendments, mostly from Senator Esperidião Amin, were rejected.[3] The key concession was removing AI used by digital platforms to produce, recommend and distribute content from the high-risk list, which Gomes described as a deal among party caucuses.[25] Tech Policy Press called the result "watered-down."[22]

The Senate text remains ambitious. Article 13 bans "excessive-risk" systems, including those that induce harmful behavior or exploit vulnerabilities, predict crime from personality traits, produce child sexual abuse material, enable illegitimate or disproportionate social scoring by public authorities, or function as autonomous weapons; it also bans real-time remote biometric identification in public spaces, with exceptions for criminal investigation, locating missing persons, flagrante delicto and arrest warrants.[26] Article 14 lists high-risk uses from hiring and essential services to autonomous vehicles, health diagnostics and border control, and Article 15 lets the regulator add more.[26] The text also adds a "systemic risk" tier for general-purpose and generative AI, defined broadly and without an exhaustive list.[27]

Rights and assessments form the bill's distinctive core. Articles 5 to 12 give everyone the right to know they are interacting with AI and to non-discrimination, and give people affected by high-risk systems rights to explanation, contestation and human review, enforceable individually or collectively in administrative or judicial proceedings.[26] Developers and deployers of high-risk systems must complete an algorithmic impact assessment before market placement and publish its conclusions, while a preliminary risk assessment is mandatory only for developers of general-purpose and generative systems and is otherwise a voluntary good practice.[26][27] Developers must publish a summary of protected works used in training, rights holders may opt out, and developers must pay remuneration for protected content used in training, with a non-commercial exception for research, journalism, cultural and educational institutions.[26][28] Governance runs through the National AI Regulation and Governance System (SIA), coordinated by the data protection authority (ANPD), which also acts as residual regulator where no sectoral agency exists.[26] Sanctions run from warnings to fines of up to R$50 million per infraction or 2% of a private group's gross revenue in Brazil, plus suspension and bans from sandboxes.[26]

## Twenty months in the Chamber without a report

The Chamber registered the bill on 17 March 2025, and because it was distributed to six committees, the presidency created a special committee under the Chamber's internal rules.[4] That committee was installed on 20 May 2025, with Luisa Canziani as chair and Aguinaldo Ribeiro (PP-PB) as rapporteur.[29] Canziani had been the rapporteur of PL 21/2020 in 2021, a continuity that links today's process to Brazil's earlier, lighter approach.[21]

Aos Fatos counted at least 83 visits by lobbyists for Amazon, Google, IBM, Meta, Microsoft and OpenAI to Congress between May and October 2025, including visits to the offices of 15 committee members, in a country with no lobbying regulation.[9] Ribeiro, Canziani and vice-chair Adriana Ventura also traveled to the United States on a trip Canziani said was paid for by a free-market parliamentary caucus.[9] A vote expected at the end of 2025 did not happen.[9]

In 2026 the calendar slipped repeatedly. By May the committee had not met that year, and targets of 19 May for the report and 27 May for a plenary vote were set and missed.[30] CNN Brasil reported two obstacles: deputies wanted assurance that the Senate would vote quickly after the Chamber, amid tension between the government and Senate President Davi Alcolumbre, and there was a dispute over whether to fold Redata, a special tax regime for data centers, into the bill.[31] On 9–10 June, Chamber President Hugo Motta ruled out a vote that week, and Canziani said that "alignment with the Senate is fundamental."[32] Ribeiro admitted that "there is no solution today that satisfies everyone" on copyright and said he wanted a "timeless" law.[33] In late May he floated removing the statutory risk classification and leaving it to regulators, which the Coalizão Direitos na Rede opposed.[34] On 24 August he said the vote would come after the October 2026 elections, with work returning "at full force" toward the end of the year, and that he was negotiating copyright with Gomes because the bill must return to the Senate.[35]

The official record as of 13–14 November 2026 shows the bill under the priority regime and "Awaiting the Rapporteur's Opinion in the Special Committee," with no report, substitute or vote recorded and about 45 attached bills.[4] An independent tracker counted 37 attached bills and noted conflicting regime entries on 12 and 13 November.[29] This report found no evidence of committee activity, a filed report or a new vote date after the elections. PL 2338/2023 is not law, and approval within 2026 rests only on the rapporteur's prediction, not on any scheduled vote.

## Shared architecture, divergent burdens

The EU's influence on PL 2338 is clearest in its architecture. Mendes and Kira write that the bill "mirrors the EU AI Act in many aspects," sharing the OECD's broad AI definition, a risk-based structure, similar high-risk lists and bans on practices such as social scoring.[5] They nonetheless reject the label of "legal transplant," arguing that the added rights-based layer responds to Brazil's own inequalities.[5] Because that argument predates the Senate's 2024 dilutions, the final Senate text arguably sits closer to the EU baseline than the jurists' draft did.

The differences lie mainly in who carries obligations. ITS Rio's preliminary June 2025 comparison counted 68 obligations in PL 2338 against 43 in the EU Act; 41 of the EU's 43 fall on providers, whereas 34 of Brazil's 68 apply jointly to providers, deployers and distributors, and 14 target the public sector specifically.[7] An earlier ITS Rio count of the November 2024 text, reported by Tech Policy Press, found 56 obligations, so the exact figures depend on the version and method and should not be treated as definitive.[22] The rights chapter has no direct EU counterpart, since the EU addresses explanation and contestation largely through the GDPR.[6] Brazil's bill also includes civil liability provisions, although one law firm calls the Senate text's choice between strict and fault-based liability "ambiguous."[6][5]

Copyright and sanctions mark the sharpest divergence. The EU Act requires GPAI providers to adopt a copyright policy and publish training summaries, but it creates no remuneration right; Brazil's Article 65 does.[2][26] That provision is now the main obstacle in the Chamber.[30][35] On sanctions, Brazil's ceiling of R$50 million or 2% of Brazilian revenue is far below the EU's €35 million or 7% of global turnover, which gives the EU much greater leverage over multinational firms.[6] On governance, Brazil relies on a hub-and-spoke system of sectoral agencies coordinated by the ANPD, while the EU combines national authorities with an increasingly powerful AI Office.[30][17] One law firm reports that the ANPD's coordinating role may require constitutional amendment, but no second source confirms this.[6]

On recommender systems, the two laws converge in form but not in effect. The Senate dropped platform curation and recommendation systems from the high-risk list after industry lobbying, and it cut most references to information integrity after critics claimed they could enable censorship.[27][22] The EU Act does not classify recommender systems as high-risk either, but it leaves them to the Digital Services Act, whereas Brazil's platform bill, PL 2630/2020, has stalled.[5] The same formal choice leaves Brazil with a gap rather than a parallel regime.

ITIF's Ayesha Bhatti argues that EU digital rules force costly alignment on emerging economies and were "designed without emerging economies in mind."[36] Kati Cseres argues that the Brussels effect is "now being tested and contested" in Brazil, where data protection converged with the GDPR but platform and competition bills diverged, and she asks how countries that copied EU frameworks should respond to the Omnibus.[37] United States pressure adds another layer. The Trump administration revoked Biden's 2023 AI executive order, and Mark Zuckerberg said Meta would work with it to push back on foreign regulation.[22] Washington's formal actions against Brazil, including a Section 301 finding in June 2026 that proposed a 25% tariff and a July 2026 letter from 20 Republican House members, targeted content moderation, payments and the digital-markets bill PL 4675 rather than PL 2338.[38][39] No reviewed source records a direct US government statement against the AI bill.

## Conclusion

The comparison reverses a common assumption. Brazil is not lagging behind a fixed European standard; it is chasing a moving one. By deferring high-risk obligations while keeping GPAI rules and adding a ban on sexual deepfakes, the EU has signaled that the timing and intensity of AI regulation are negotiable even after adoption. That gives Brazilian legislators cover to delay or delegate, as Ribeiro's proposal to move risk classification to regulators shows, but Europe's prohibitions, transparency rules and GPAI regime are already operating, so Brazil cannot claim the model is untested.

The most consequential open question is whether Brazil keeps the features that made its bill more than a transplant: the rights chapter, horizontal obligations that reach deployers and the public sector, and copyright remuneration. Those are exactly the provisions industry has targeted and the Chamber is renegotiating. If they survive, Brazil will offer a distinct Global South model with weaker fines but stronger individual rights. If they fall, Brazil will converge on the lighter, delayed version of the EU model that emerged in 2026.
