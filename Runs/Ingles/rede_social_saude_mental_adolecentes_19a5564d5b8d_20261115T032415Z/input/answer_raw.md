# Lawmakers Outpace the Science on Teen Social Media

Social media use is linked to poorer adolescent mental health, but the average link is small, and whether it causes harm across the whole population remains unsettled as of November 2026. Umbrella reviews put typical correlations at around r = .10. Compulsive or "problematic" use, by contrast, shows a much more consistent tie to depression, anxiety and stress.[1] The strongest quasi-experimental and within-person studies point to real but modest harm.[2][3] Even so, the National Academies of Sciences, Engineering, and Medicine concluded that there is not enough evidence to say social media changes adolescent health at the population level.[4] Harms concentrate in specific patterns of use, such as late-night use, compulsive checking and girls' appearance-focused use, while benefits flow disproportionately to LGBTQ+ and Black teens.[5][6][7] Policy has nonetheless raced ahead. Australia has barred under-16s from major platforms since December 2025, and the United Kingdom and European Union are building similar regimes.[8][9] In the United States, a Los Angeles jury found Meta and YouTube negligent, and state attorneys general reportedly secured a settlement worth up to $17 billion that imposes default time limits and night blocks on Meta's teen users.[10][11] Early evaluations suggest that simple access bans leak badly, shifting attention to design rules.[12][13]

## Teen distress climbed for a decade while measured effects stayed small

Among US high school students, the share reporting **persistent sadness or hopelessness rose from 30% in 2013 to 42% in 2021**, then eased to 40% in 2023. Over the same period, the share who seriously considered suicide rose from 17% to 22% before falling back to 20%.[14] Girls reported every outcome more often than boys, and 77% of students used social media several times a day.[14] The National Academies noted that youth mental health declined over roughly the same 15 years in which smartphones transformed young people's internet use, but it framed this as a parallel trend, not proof of cause.[4] That timing is the backbone of Jonathan Haidt's argument that a "phone-based childhood" caused the teen mental health crisis.[15] The partial improvement after 2021, while use stayed near-universal, fits awkwardly with any simple account based on time spent on platforms.

When researchers measure the association directly, it shrinks. Amy Orben and Andrew Przybylski's 2019 specification-curve analysis drew on more than 300,000 adolescents in US and UK datasets. It found that **digital technology use was associated with at most 0.4% of the variance in wellbeing**.[16] Regularly eating potatoes had nearly as large a negative association, and being bullied had about 4.3 times the negative association of screen use.[16] A 2026 umbrella review of 72 reviews published between 2021 and April 2025 found that 13 of 20 meta-analyses reported small but significant links between social media use and higher ill-being. Earlier umbrella reviews reported associations of around r = .10, which one described as "on average negative but very small."[1]

The same review explains why findings on general use look so inconsistent. Its power analysis covered 1,070 effect estimates from 452 studies. For problematic use and ill-being, every meta-analysis had median study power above 99%. For general use, only 4 of 15 meta-analyses exceeded 80% power, and 8 fell below 50%.[1] A meta-meta-analysis posted in November 2026, **not yet peer reviewed**, reached a similar overall estimate. It reported a cross-sectional standardized mean difference of 0.21 and a longitudinal effect of d = 0.24, with only 3 of 7 underlying meta-analyses preregistered.[17] The "0.4% of variance" and "d of 0.2" figures do not conflict; they reflect different metrics and measures of use. The size of the association is now fairly settled as small. What remains disputed is whether it is causal and whether a small average effect matters at population scale.

## Natural experiments and cohort data tilt toward modest real harm

The best causal evidence comes from designs that exploit timing. Luca Braghieri, Ro'ee Levy and Alexey Makarin used Facebook's staggered 2004–2006 rollout across 775 US colleges, together with more than 430,000 student health survey responses. They found that **access raised an index of poor mental health by 0.085 standard deviations**, about 22% of the effect of losing a job.[2] The share of students likely to have depression rose about 2 percentage points from a 25% baseline. The most susceptible students showed more depression diagnoses and antidepressant use, and effects among women were suggestively larger.[2] The authors point to unfavorable social comparison as the mechanism.[2] But the subjects were college students, not adolescents, and 2004-era Facebook bears little resemblance to today's algorithmic feeds.

Cohort data on younger children point in the same direction. In the Adolescent Brain Cognitive Development (ABCD) study, Jason Nagata and colleagues followed 11,876 children from ages 9–10 for three years. Average daily use rose from 7 to 73 minutes.[3] **Within-person increases in use predicted higher depressive symptoms the following year** (β = 0.07, then 0.09), while earlier symptoms did not predict later use.[3] The effects are small and observational, and the finding conflicts with the preprint synthesis, which found effects in both directions: use predicted later poorer mental health (d = 0.25), and poorer mental health predicted later use (d = 0.18).[17] Which way the relationship mainly runs is still unresolved.

Randomized trials have not settled the question. The trials in this literature typically last only one to four weeks. One planned meta-analysis of them took down its preliminary results "due to some initial concerns about data quality."[18] Przybylski argues that the evidence linking *reduced* screen time to *better* wellbeing is even weaker than the correlational evidence. He cites his own 2023 cross-national study, which found no measurable effect of Facebook on global wellbeing.[15] Brief individual abstinence trials also cannot capture network effects, where a whole peer group lives online, and those effects are central to Haidt's account. The National Academies concluded that most research cannot determine the direction of effects or account for individual differences.[4]

## Compulsive use, lost sleep and appearance comparison carry the clearest risks

Meta-analyses support claims of unique vulnerability among girls and younger teens less consistently than popular accounts suggest. Gender was a significant moderator in only 3 of 15 meta-analyses that tested it, and age in 4 of 17.[1] The preprint meta-regression found no systematic moderation by age or gender.[17] The most robust differentiator is the *quality* of use. Problematic use is defined by compulsion, loss of control and interference with daily life, and the time someone spends online is not necessarily connected to it.[1] A 2024 meta-analysis of 98 studies by Ahmed and colleagues found small but significant associations between general use and depression and anxiety. **Problematic use was associated with depression, anxiety and sleep problems**, and with lower wellbeing.[19] Active use shows neutral to positive associations, while passive scrolling is more likely to provoke harmful comparison.[1][20] Compulsive use may itself partly be a symptom of distress, so this robust finding is also the one most exposed to reverse causation.

Sleep displacement is the most plausible mechanism and the one teens themselves report most often. The American Psychological Association (APA) advises at least eight hours of sleep for adolescents. It links technology use within an hour of bedtime to sleep disruption, and insufficient sleep to altered brain development, emotional difficulties and suicide risk.[5] In Pew's 2024 survey, **45% of US teens said social media hurts their sleep** (50% of girls, 40% of boys). Only 19% said it hurts their mental health.[6] Appearance-based comparison comes next. The APA links it to poorer body image, disordered eating and depressive symptoms, particularly among girls.[5] Meta's own research, leaked in 2021, found that 32% of teen girls said Instagram made them feel worse when they already felt bad about their bodies.[21]

Several other harms are serious but harder to measure across populations. Exposure to online discrimination predicts increases in anxiety and depressive symptoms even after controlling for offline exposure, and both perpetrators and victims of cyberbullying face elevated risk.[5] Algorithmic exposure to self-harm content received an official finding in Britain in 2022. A coroner ruled that 14-year-old Molly Russell died "from an act of self-harm while suffering depression and the negative effects of online content." She had saved, shared or liked 16,300 Instagram posts in six months, 2,100 of them related to depression, self-harm or suicide.[22] Meta's internal data showed that among teens reporting suicidal thoughts, 13% of British users and 6% of American users traced the issue to Instagram.[21] Financially motivated sextortion typically targets boys aged 14 to 17. The National Center for Missing & Exploited Children reports that at least 36 teenagers have taken their own lives since 2021 after threats to leak explicit images.[23]

Neuroscience explains why early adolescence may be a vulnerable window, though it does not show that damage occurs. The APA notes that brain regions tied to peer feedback become more sensitive around ages 10 to 14, while regions for self-control mature into adulthood.[5] A University of North Carolina study followed 169 middle schoolers with annual brain scans. Students who checked social media more than 15 times a day, about a third of the sample, showed *rising* neural sensitivity to anticipated peer feedback over three years. Non-checkers showed *declining* sensitivity.[24][25] But the groups differed at baseline, and the authors claim only an association.[24]

## Benefits flow most to teens who lack support offline

Any account of harm must weigh real benefits, and these are concentrated among the youth most exposed to offline adversity. In Pew's 2024 survey, 74% of teens said social media makes them feel more connected to friends' lives, and 63% said it gives them a creative outlet.[6] Black teens were far more likely than White teens to say it makes them feel "a lot" more accepted (25% versus 10%). They were also more likely to get mental health information there (49% versus 30%).[6] The National Academies adds support for youth facing bereavement or illness and community for LGBTQ+ teens.[4]

The Trevor Project's 2024 survey of 18,663 LGBTQ+ young people found that 74% go online because connection is hard to find in daily life. Respondents who felt safe nowhere online, just 7% of the sample, reported higher recent anxiety and more past-year suicide attempts (14% versus 11%).[7] The same data hold a warning, though. Respondents who more strongly agreed that they went online *because* offline connection was hard had higher odds of depression (adjusted odds ratio 1.29) and suicide attempts. The survey is cross-sectional, so cause and effect cannot be separated.[7] Online refuges and offline distress are therefore intertwined, and blanket restrictions risk removing support from the groups that rely on it most. Perceived support is also eroding, falling from 67% of teens in 2022 to 52% in 2024. Meanwhile, 48% now say it has a mostly negative effect on people their age, though only 14% say the same about themselves.[6]

## The scientific fight is now about how to weigh evidence, not effect size

The scientific dispute is no longer mainly about numbers. Haidt's 2024 book *The Anxious Generation* proposes four norms: no smartphones before high school, no social media before 16, phone-free schools, and more independent play. He defends them on precautionary grounds: if the alarm ringers turn out to be wrong, he argues, "the costs are minimal and reversible."[15] Candice Odgers, reviewing the book in *Nature*, countered that the claim of an epidemic caused by technology "is not supported by science." She noted that researchers had found "a mix of no, small and mixed associations," and warned that the focus on social media could distract from the real causes.[15] Przybylski likens Haidt's synthesis to "vote counting" and warns that age rules could backfire. Christopher Ferguson notes that US middle-aged white men are three to five times more likely to die by suicide than teen girls. Pete Etchells warns of a "moral panic."[15]

Both camps have legitimate points. The critics are right about the evidence base. Studies mix outcomes, define use loosely, rely on unvalidated measures and cross-sectional designs, and show publication bias in 4 of 18 meta-analyses that checked.[1] Large datasets allow more than 600 million analytic specifications, leaving ample room to find any desired pattern.[16] The alarm ringers, in turn, are right that the best-designed recent studies (the Facebook rollout and the ABCD within-person analyses) lean toward harm rather than toward nothing. A fair reading of the record as of 2026 is that social media is probably a partial cause of distress for some adolescents and some kinds of use, and that it is insufficient on its own to explain the full post-2012 trend. The real disagreement is whether that level of evidence justifies acting on precaution.

## Juries and attorneys general turned internal research into liability

Litigation is resolving in practice what science has not, using the companies' own research. Internal Meta researchers wrote in documents leaked in 2021 that "aspects of Instagram exacerbate each other to create a perfect storm," and 14% of US boys said the app made them feel worse about themselves.[21] A 2023 complaint by a bipartisan coalition of 33 attorneys general alleged that Meta knew millions of under-13s used its platforms, at one point about 30% of 10- to 12-year-olds. It also alleged that Mark Zuckerberg vetoed a proposed ban on filters that simulate plastic surgery.[26]

On March 25, 2026, a Los Angeles jury delivered the first verdict in the youth-harm cases. It **found Meta and Google's YouTube negligent and responsible for a 20-year-old plaintiff's depression and anxiety**, and awarded $6 million, 70% of it owed by Meta.[10] Jurors saw an internal document stating, "If we wanna win big with teens, we must bring them in as tweens." The plaintiffs sidestepped Section 230 immunity by targeting design features such as infinite scroll, notifications, autoplay and beauty filters, rather than content. The defense argued there is no scientific proof of harm.[10] Post-trial motions were reportedly denied and appeals are pending. A second bellwether trial in the same state-court proceeding was underway in November 2026.[27] A day before the Los Angeles verdict, a New Mexico jury ordered Meta to pay $375 million for misleading consumers about child safety.[10] A court later brought the total to a reported $942 million. A separate data-practices case reportedly produced a $25 billion civil-penalty order, a figure that rests on a single aggregator source and that Meta says it will appeal.[27]

The largest outcome came without a verdict. The multistate federal trial against Meta settled on August 26, 2026, eight days after it opened.[27] Reported terms run to **up to $17 billion over 10 years**, with a substantial portion contingent on rival platforms adopting equivalent obligations. Reports put the size of the coalition at anywhere from 47 to 51 attorneys general, and at least one outlet reported the value as up to $18 billion.[11][28][29] The design terms matter more than the money. By default, teen accounts face a midnight-to-6 a.m. block, a two-hour daily limit across Meta's apps, a weekday "School Mode" that silences notifications during school hours, hidden like counts and disabled cosmetic-surgery filters. A 10-year independent auditor oversees compliance, and Meta admits no liability.[11] Federal multidistrict cases reportedly number about 4,309, and the first school-district bellwether settled before a jury was seated.[27]

## Governments converge on age 16 and safe-by-design mandates

The policy turn began with public health officials. In June 2024, US Surgeon General Vivek Murthy called for a warning label stating that social media is associated with significant mental health harms for adolescents, and urged limits on push notifications, autoplay and infinite scroll.[30] The APA applauded the call.[31] The National Academies instead favored industry design standards, harassment reporting, media literacy and researcher data access over broad bans.[4]

Other governments went further. Australia's under-16 minimum age has applied since December 10, 2025.[8] The United Kingdom took legislative powers in April 2026 to impose a ban, and announced in June that protections for under-16s would take effect in spring 2027. Its model follows Australia's but excludes messaging services.[32] The European Commission proposed an "EU Kids Act" on September 17, 2026, which Parliament and Council must still adopt. It would bar personal accounts under 13 and give 13- to 15-year-olds "mini accounts" limited to one hour a day. It would also ban infinite scroll, endless autoplay and profiling-based recommendations for minors, with fines up to 6% of global turnover.[9] France's Constitutional Council struck down a blanket under-15 ban in August 2026.[9] Malaysia, Indonesia and Turkey have reportedly adopted age limits, and Brazil's statute requiring parental consent for under-16s took effect in March 2026.[8]

The US Congress has still not enacted the Kids Online Safety Act (KOSA) or COPPA 2.0, its companion bill to extend children's privacy protections to teens. The House passed a narrower KIDS Act 267–117 in June 2026. It drops KOSA's "duty of care," and Senate sponsors called it dead on arrival.[33] State laws remain tangled in First Amendment litigation: Virginia's one-hour default limit for under-16s is blocked, while Mississippi's and Ohio's laws survived recent rulings.[34] In October 2026 the Eleventh Circuit allowed Florida's under-14 ban to be enforced.[35]

## Early evaluations find leaky bans and compensating behavior

The first evidence on whether these measures work is sobering. Australia's regulator reported that platforms restricted about 4.7 million under-16 accounts.[36] Three months in, the share of under-16s with accounts on restricted platforms had fallen from 52.4% to 42.1%, yet **81.5% still reported using an age-restricted platform**.[12] Just over half of those with active accounts said they had never been asked to prove their age. Workarounds included spoofed age checks (12%) and parental help (10.9%), and daily messaging use rose 11.8%.[12] Fear of missing out dipped, but there was no discernible change in offline activities or problematic online behaviors.[12] Mental health outcomes will come from a two-year study of more than 4,000 children.[37]

School phone bans show a similar pattern. The SMART Schools study surveyed 1,227 adolescents in 30 English secondary schools. Restrictive policies were **not associated with better wellbeing, sleep or attainment**. Students in restrictive schools used their phones about 30 minutes less during the school day, but their total phone time showed no difference.[13][38] Commentators concluded that school bans alone are unlikely to suffice without regulation of the most harmful platform features.[13] Voluntary platform tools have fared no better. Independent testing of 47 Instagram Teen Accounts safety tools rated 30 as ineffective or non-existent and only 8 as fully working. Teen accounts could still see suicide, self-harm and eating-disorder content.[39]

## Conclusion

The center of gravity has shifted from *whether* to restrict teens' access to *how* platforms are built. Measures that limit access alone, including age bans, school phone rules and opt-in parental tools, keep running into the same problem: adolescents compensate, migrate to messaging and gaming, or get around the rules, sometimes with their parents' help. The interventions most likely to matter target the mechanisms with the strongest evidence, namely nighttime use, compulsive feedback loops and appearance-driven recommendation. The Meta settlement's curfews and default limits make it the first large-scale test of that approach, run by consent decree rather than statute.

Precautionary policy is now producing the natural experiments the science lacked. Australia's cohort study, the UK's spring 2027 start and Meta's court-enforced defaults will generate outcome data within two to three years. Researchers and regulators should judge these policies by changes in depression, anxiety, self-harm and sleep, not by counts of closed accounts. They should also watch whether the LGBTQ+ and marginalized youth who rely most on online support end up bearing the costs.
