## Working in Iceland

### Working hours

The average employee works 37.4 hours a week (OECD, 2025), counting part-timers. According to the Autonomy and Alda report, the 2019-2021 contracts brought standard hours in the public sector to 36 a week (36, in some cases 32, for shift workers), while private-sector cuts varied by contract and workplace. Employers may not schedule a workday longer than 13 hours, and workers get at least 11 hours of continuous rest in 24.

### Paid leave and holidays

The minimum is 24 weekdays of paid leave a year, under the Holiday Allowance Act No. 30/1987. There are 16 public holidays a year.

### Part-time and flexible work

In 2025, 21.6% of employed people aged 15-64 worked part time: 31.0% of women and 13.2% of men (Eurostat).

### Remote work

In 2025, 37.9% of employees aged 15-64 worked from home at least sometimes: 5.3% usually and 32.6% sometimes (Eurostat).

## Is there a 4-day week in Iceland?

Partly. Iceland has not adopted a four-day week, but it has moved to a shorter one. After two public-sector trials, unions won shorter hours in contracts signed between 2019 and 2021. According to the Autonomy and Alda report, about 86% of Iceland's working population had by June 2021 either moved to shorter hours or gained a way to negotiate them.

The trials ran in Reykjavík City Council (from March 2015) and the national government (from 2017). They cut hours from 40 to 35 or 36 a week with no cut in pay. More than 2,500 workers took part, over 1% of the working population. The report found that productivity and service provision stayed the same or improved in most workplaces, and that wellbeing rose.

Three caveats. The change is in weekly hours, not days: in the public sector the contracts cut 13 minutes a day, effective January 2021. The 86% includes people whose contracts only let them negotiate shorter hours at their workplace, and unlike the trials, the later cuts were not all cost-free. And a 2024 Alda and Autonomy follow-up, using a 2021-22 government survey, found 59% of workers had been offered reduced hours in the previous two years, so not everyone had been.
