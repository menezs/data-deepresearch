Is it really possible to give people an extra day off every week without sacrificing performance?

According to a new study in *Nature Human Behaviour*, yes – and it could also be one of the most effective ways to support employee wellbeing. The international trial, led by sociologist Wen Fan of Boston College and co-authored by Juliet Schor, Orla Kelly and Guolin Gu, found that moving to a four-day week with no reduction in pay  led to widespread improvements in workers’ mental and physical health, reduced burnout and increased job satisfaction.

The results span six countries – Australia, Canada, Ireland, New Zealand, the UK and the US – and involve 2,896 employees across 141 companies. Crucially, these organisations didn’t just shave a day off the calendar. Before the six-month trial began each company had around eight weeks to reorganise its operations. Meetings were streamlined and workflows were redesigned. The goal was to maintain productivity while cutting scheduled hours by 20%.

Twelve control companies that didn’t implement the change failed to see any of the positive outcomes.

So what can HR leaders take from this? Time, when thoughtfully managed, can be a strategic lever. But there’s more to this story than just adding ‘four-day week’ to your benefits package.

## The efficiency dividend

At the heart of the trial’s success is a principle long understood but rarely enacted, that much of what fills the modern workweek is waste.

Researchers observed that companies in the trial achieved the same or similar levels of output by removing unproductive tasks – particularly unnecessary meetings – and by improving collaboration. These operational changes, not just the shorter week itself, helped preserve performance.

And it’s a timely insight. Many organisations still struggle to define what “good productivity” looks like in knowledge work, especially in hybrid settings.

Pedro Gomes, an economist at Birkbeck University of London, notes in a write up in *Nature* magazine that rest and performance are not at odds. “When people are more well rested, they make fewer mistakes and work more intensely,” he tells the magazine. 

Indeed, participants in the study reported higher job satisfaction and fewer sleep problems – two metrics that rarely appear together in traditional workforce reporting but speak volumes about the lived experience of work.

## Redesigning work, not just reducing hours

The research authors note: “Both company-level and individual-level reductions in hours are correlated with wellbeing gains, with larger individual-level (but not company-level) reductions associated with greater improvements in wellbeing.” In other words, how much a person’s own workweek changed had more impact than how much the organisation changed overall.

That matters for HR. It suggests that personal workload and control over time are key levers and not just headline policies. Giving someone a four-day week won’t have the intended effect if they still have to cram five days’ worth of meetings and outputs into four.

Wen Fan herself had anticipated this potential downside. “When workers want to deliver the same productivity, they might work very rapidly to get the job done and their wellbeing might actually worsen,” she told *Nature*. “But that’s not what we found.”

Instead, the opposite was true. The researchers identified three key factors mediating the wellbeing gains:

- Improved self-reported work ability
- Reduced fatigue
- Fewer sleep issues

These are all early warning signals of burnout and reversing them could be one of the most cost-effective forms of preventive health available to employers.

## But what about productivity?

The trial didn’t formally assess company-wide productivity, though participating organisations were asked to maintain output levels. It’s a notable omission and one that critics will latch onto.

However, more than 90% of companies chose to continue with the four-day week after the trial ended, suggesting that perceived or actual productivity didn’t decline enough to warrant a return to the five-day model.

It’s also worth noting that the benefits extended beyond the trial period. Data collected a full year after the initial intervention showed that employee wellbeing remained high, undercutting the idea that this was a novelty effect.

## What Did the UK Four-Day Week Trial Actually Find?

From June to December 2022, 61 UK organizations and about 2,900 employees participated in a six-month pilot studied by researchers at the University of Cambridge and Boston College with the think tank Autonomy. Workers took a 20% reduction in hours with no reduction in pay.

The results, by the numbers:

- **71%** of employees self-reported lower burnout
- **39%** said they were less stressed compared to the start of the trial
- **65%** reduction in sick days vs. the same period the prior year
- **57%** fall in staff turnover vs. the same period the prior year
- **Revenue** rose 1.4% on average, but only**23 of 61 organizations** provided revenue data. That denominator matters.
- **56 of 61** companies (92%) intended to continue; 18 confirmed the policy as permanent

One year later, the follow-up was strong. Autonomy's "Making It Stick" report found at least 54 of 61 firms (89%) were still operating a four-day week, and at least 31 (51%) had made it permanent.

What this trial did not have: a control group. Every firm entered voluntarily through an advocacy organization. The 92% continuation figure tells you something about these specific firms, not about all firms.

## The Biggest Study Yet: 141 Companies in Six Countries

Fan, Schor, Kelly, and Gu (2025), published in *Nature Human Behaviour*, followed 2,896 employees across 141 organizations in six countries through a six-month trial. Twelve companies served as controls.

Average company hours fell from **39.21 to 34.01 per week**, a cut of about 13%, not the full 20% the label implies. Work burnout fell from **2.83 to 2.38** on a 1-to-5 scale. Job satisfaction, mental health, and physical health all improved. The control companies showed no comparable changes.

The mediators: self-reported work ability improved, sleep problems fell, fatigue decreased. The gains were not about more output per hour. They were about less damage per week.

The authors are explicit about the limits. Firms were recruited through 4 Day Week Global, an advocacy organization. The sample is mostly white-collar. The authors themselves write that their findings likely overestimate the true effect.

## 4 Day Work Week: What the Trials Actually Measured

The best evidence on the four-day work week says it improves how workers feel: lower burnout, better sleep, less fatigue. In the firms that reported revenue (23 out of 61 in the largest UK pilot), output mostly held steady. But no trial was randomized, every participating company volunteered, and most outcomes were self-reported. The version of "four-day week" matters too: 32 hours at full pay is a different intervention than 40 hours crammed into four 10-hour days. **Key takeaways:**

- The two biggest trials (UK 2022 and Fan et al. 2025) measured well-being, not productivity. Revenue data came from fewer than half the UK firms.

## What Is a 4 Day Work Week? Two Models, Not One

The phrase "4 day work week" describes two structurally different schedules.

**The 100-80-100 model** means 100% pay, 80% time, 100% expected output. Workers drop to about 32 hours across four days. This is what the UK pilot and Fan et al. tested.

**The compressed 4x10 schedule** keeps total hours at 40 but packs them into four 10-hour days. Tokyo's Metropolitan Government adopted this model in April 2025, allowing staff to take three days off per week under a flexible system with total monthly hours of 155. Same hours, rearranged.

In the US, the Thirty-Two-Hour Workweek Act (S. 3947) was introduced in March 2024 by Senators Sanders and Butler. It would lower the FLSA overtime threshold from 40 to 32 hours in phases. It has been introduced. It is not law.

The health trade-offs diverge between models. Bambra's 2008 review of compressed schedules found that work-life balance improved, but the studies were low quality and outcomes mostly self-reported. A 10-hour day can bring fatigue that a 32-hour week avoids.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Is a 4 day work week 32 hours?

Usually, but not always. The 100-80-100 model tested in the UK pilot and Fan et al. means about 32 hours at full pay. A compressed 4x10 schedule keeps total hours at 40. Tokyo's public-sector model uses 155 hours per month. Same label, different hours.

### Which companies have a 4 day work week?

Microsoft Japan trialed it for one month in August 2019. The Tokyo Metropolitan Government began offering a compressed option in April 2025. Beyond those, the best-documented examples are the 61 UK pilot firms (56 intended to continue) and the 141 volunteer organizations in Fan et al., mostly white-collar and recruited through 4 Day Week Global.

### Do you get paid the same on a 4 day work week?

In the 100-80-100 model, yes. Workers take a 20% reduction in hours with no cut in pay. The compressed 4x10 model also preserves full pay since total hours remain at 40. Both the UK pilot and Fan et al. held wages constant.

### Is a 4x10 schedule better than five 8-hour days?

Bambra's 2008 review found that compressed weeks improved work-life balance, but the studies were low quality and outcomes were mostly self-reported. A 10-hour day can bring more fatigue, and childcare logistics can be harder. Whether it is "better" depends on your role, your commute, and how well you sustain focus past hour eight.

### Did productivity drop in the four-day week trials?

Revenue in the 23 UK firms that reported it rose 1.4% on average. Fan et al. measured well-being, not revenue. Microsoft Japan's 39.9% jump was one company, one month, with concurrent changes. Output mostly held steady where measured, but it was not measured in most trials.

### Is the US moving to a 32-hour work week?

The Thirty-Two-Hour Workweek Act (S. 3947) was introduced in the Senate in March 2024. It would phase the FLSA overtime threshold down from 40 to 32 hours. It has not advanced beyond introduction and is not law. No federal policy change is imminent as of 2026.

## What the Trials Changed Before They Cut the Day

The companies that made four-day weeks work did not just cross Friday off the calendar. They redesigned how work happened first.

Microsoft Japan capped meetings at 30 minutes. Fan et al. describe "pre-trial work reorganization" in which companies improved efficiency and collaboration before the six-month clock started. The common thread: fewer meetings, async communication, and protected focus blocks. These are the changes Parkinson's law predicts: work expands to fill the time, so when you cut the time, you cut the filler.

Whether your company runs four days or five, the practical lesson is the same. Your output tracks your focused hours, not your logged hours. Protecting two or three deep work blocks per day from meetings and interruptions is the part of these trials any schedule can copy, even if the headline was about the missing Friday. ## So, Does the 4 Day Work Week Work?

For well-being in volunteer, mostly white-collar firms: yes, consistently. Burnout drops, sleep improves, fatigue falls, people stay longer.

For output and revenue: probably neutral in the firms that tried it, but the data is thin (23 firms), self-reported, and drawn from companies already optimistic about the idea.

For everyone: unproven. Hourly workers, shift workers, and industries with hard coverage requirements were mostly absent. And the compressed 4x10, which is what many employers actually mean by "four-day week," is a different intervention with different trade-offs. The version matters more than the label.

## Iceland's "Four-Day Week" Was Actually a 35-to-36-Hour Week

The Iceland trials, run by Reykjavík City Council and the national government from 2015 to 2019, involved more than 2,500 workers, about 1% of the Icelandic workforce. Hours dropped from 40 to 35 or 36 at the same pay.

For many participants, that meant five shorter days, not four. Calling it a "four-day week" is media shorthand.

Service provision was maintained or improved, and well-being rose. The political impact was large: according to Autonomy and the Association for Sustainable Democracy (Alda), about 86% of Iceland's workforce has since moved to shorter hours or gained the right to negotiate them. A policy success, but one about reducing weekly hours, not about a specific day off.

## Does a 4 Day Work Week Increase Productivity?

This is the question everyone asks, and the answer is less clean than the headlines suggest.

**Microsoft Japan** gave employees five Fridays off in August 2019, capped meetings at 30 minutes, and pushed communication online. Sales per employee rose 39.9% versus August 2018. But this was one company, one month, with concurrent process changes. A case study, not a controlled experiment.

The broader evidence on hours and output is older but stronger. Economist John Pencavel, analyzing data on British munitions workers from World War I, found that below about 49 hours per week, output was roughly proportional to hours. Above that, returns diminished sharply. Cutting from 55 to 40 costs less output than you would expect. Cutting from 40 to 32 is a different question, and Pencavel's data does not answer it.

A 2021 WHO/ILO study estimated that working 55+ hours per week led to 745,000 deaths from stroke and ischemic heart disease in 2016: 35% higher stroke risk, 17% higher heart disease mortality versus 35-to-40-hour weeks. That is the case against long weeks, not proof that shorter weeks improve output.

## Mind the bias

As with any large-scale stud, context matters. All 141 companies volunteered to take part – meaning the sample may be skewed towards more progressive, change-ready organisations. The outcomes, therefore, might not translate directly to businesses with different cultures or operational constraints.

Moreover, all results were self-reported. That doesn’t mean they are invalid but it does open the door to social desirability bias – the possibility that participants exaggerated the benefits because they wanted to keep their extra day off.

The authors themselves call for further randomised studies to firm up the evidence base. But even with those caveats the direction of travel is clear. A growing body of research, from Iceland’s four-year public sector trial to UK pilots led by 4 Day Week Global, points to similar conclusions: reducing hours without cutting pay can work but only with intentional redesign.

### Implications for HR

For people leaders the big takeaway isn’t that you should immediately shift to a four-day week but that wellbeing and performance are not in opposition and that time is a variable you can actively manage.

Here are four practical questions HR leaders should be asking:

1. **Where are we wasting time?**
Audit meetings, workflows and reporting to identify what can be streamlined. Don’t reduce days without first reducing noise.
2. **Do teams have autonomy over how they manage their time?**
Blanket policies rarely account for functional variation. Focus on outcomes and give teams permission to flex how they deliver them.
3. **Are we measuring wellbeing in a way that informs action?**
Go beyond pulse surveys. Track indicators like sleep quality, fatigue and work ability – the early signs of burnout.
4. **How could we pilot change in a low-risk way?**
Not every business can flip the switch overnight. Start with one team, one function or one month and iterate from there.
