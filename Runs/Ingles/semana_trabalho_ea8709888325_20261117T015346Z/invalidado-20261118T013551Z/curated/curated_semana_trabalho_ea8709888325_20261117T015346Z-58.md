# 141 Organizations Tried a Four-Day Week. Burnout Fell Most for People Who Cut 8 Hours or More.

## The study, published in Nature Human Behaviour on July 21, 2025, followed 2,896 employees, mostly in six countries, against 12 control companies where hours and well-being stayed flat. I read the full paper and its peer review file: here is what it shows, what its design cannot show, and what I would set up before trying it in a small team.

On July 21, 2025, Nature Human Behaviour published a study of what happens to workers when their whole organization moves to a four-day week without cutting pay. The authors, from Boston College and University College Dublin, followed 2,896 employees in 141 organizations, mostly in the United States, Canada, the United Kingdom, Ireland, Australia and New Zealand, across six trial cohorts that ran between February 2022 and June 2024. Burnout, job satisfaction, mental health and physical health improved. In 12 control companies, they did not.

If a founder asked me whether to try a four-day week, this is the paper I would put on the table. So I read it closely: the full text (Nature sells it, but the NSF public access repository has a free copy of the accepted manuscript) and the peer review file that Nature publishes alongside. The headline holds up. The fine print changes what you should do with it.

## What Did the UK Four-Day Week Trial Actually Find?

From June to December 2022, 61 UK organizations and about 2,900 employees participated in a six-month pilot studied by researchers at the University of Cambridge and Boston College with the think tank Autonomy. Workers took a 20% reduction in hours with no reduction in pay.

The results, by the numbers:

- **71%** of employees self-reported lower burnout
- **39%** said they were less stressed compared to the start of the trial
- **65%** reduction in sick days vs. the same period the prior year
- **57%** fall in staff turnover vs. the same period the prior year
- **Revenue** rose 1.4% on average, but only**23 of 61 organizations** provided revenue data. That denominator matters.
- **56 of 61** companies (92%) intended to continue; 18 confirmed the policy as permanent

One year later, the follow-up was strong. Autonomy's "Making It Stick" report found at least 54 of 61 firms (89%) were still operating a four-day week, and at least 31 (51%) had made it permanent.

What this trial did not have: a control group. Every firm entered voluntarily through an advocacy organization. The 92% continuation figure tells you something about these specific firms, not about all firms.

## The Biggest Study Yet: 141 Companies in Six Countries

Fan, Schor, Kelly, and Gu (2025), published in *Nature Human Behaviour*, followed 2,896 employees across 141 organizations in six countries through a six-month trial. Twelve companies served as controls.

Average company hours fell from **39.21 to 34.01 per week**, a cut of about 13%, not the full 20% the label implies. Work burnout fell from **2.83 to 2.38** on a 1-to-5 scale. Job satisfaction, mental health, and physical health all improved. The control companies showed no comparable changes.

The mediators: self-reported work ability improved, sleep problems fell, fatigue decreased. The gains were not about more output per hour. They were about less damage per week.

The authors are explicit about the limits. Firms were recruited through 4 Day Week Global, an advocacy organization. The sample is mostly white-collar. The authors themselves write that their findings likely overestimate the true effect.

## What was actually tested

The organizations were recruited by 4 Day Week Global, a non-governmental organization, on a simple rule: 80% of the hours for 100% of the pay. Companies did not have to adopt a literal four-day schedule, but they had to keep pay constant and offer a "meaningful" reduction in time. 82% offered a standard four-day week. Before the six-month trial, 4 Day Week Global ran about eight weeks of webinars to help them redesign work. The paper's example of what goes: "low- and zero-value activities, such as unnecessary meetings."

The hours did move. The company average fell from 39.21 to 34.01 a week, and the most common week went from 40 hours to 32. In the control companies, it went from 38.88 to 39.16.

The outcomes are the employees' own ratings. Burnout fell from 2.83 to 2.38 on a scale of 1 to 5. Job satisfaction rose from 7.07 to 7.59 out of 10. Mental health went from 2.93 to 3.32 and physical health from 3.01 to 3.29, both on 1 to 5. The authors call these small-to-medium effects. In the control companies, burnout went from 2.90 to 2.94 and job satisfaction slipped from 6.66 to 6.47. The gap between the two groups is statistically significant on all four measures.

A 12-month survey found hours still lower (33.91 a week at company level, against 38.97 at baseline) and the gains still there. Only job satisfaction showed signs of drifting back, while staying above where it started.

# 4 Day Work Week: What the Trials Actually Measured

## 4 Day Work Week: What the Trials Actually Measured

The best evidence on the four-day work week says it improves how workers feel: lower burnout, better sleep, less fatigue. In the firms that reported revenue (23 out of 61 in the largest UK pilot), output mostly held steady. But no trial was randomized, every participating company volunteered, and most outcomes were self-reported. The version of "four-day week" matters too: 32 hours at full pay is a different intervention than 40 hours crammed into four 10-hour days. At the far end of the scale, see what working 80 hours a week costs.

**Key takeaways:**

- The two biggest trials (UK 2022 and Fan et al. 2025) measured well-being, not productivity. Revenue data came from fewer than half the UK firms.
- Every major trial used self-selected companies. The authors of Fan et al. say their findings likely overestimate the true effect.
- A "4 day work week" can mean 32 hours at full pay (the 100-80-100 model) or a compressed 4x10 schedule. The health and focus implications are different.
- The firms that succeeded did not just delete Friday. They cut meetings, moved communication async, and protected focus time first.

## What the design cannot show

It is not randomized, and the companies chose to join. The paper says its findings "probably overestimate the true effect of a 4-day workweek." It argues that senior management made the choice, which should limit the bias in employee outcomes. One reviewer pointed out that in smaller companies, senior managers often consult staff before such decisions.

The control group is small and late. It is 12 US companies that attended an information session and did not join, 285 employees in the analysis, less than 10% of the sample, with an overrepresentation of non-profits and professional or social services. They were surveyed from November 2023 to August 2024, while five of the six trial cohorts started in 2022 (the authors report that results hold when they keep only trial companies surveyed in the same period), and fewer of their employees answered: 58% at baseline and 59% at endpoint, against 80% and 73% in trial companies. The peer review file shows how central that group was. The first version, which did not include it, was rejected in March 2024, and one reviewer pointed to a "glaring absence of a control group." The authors replied: "Since submitting our initial manuscript, we have collected data from 12 control companies." The rejection was reconsidered after their appeal, and the paper was accepted in June 2025.

Everything is self-reported, hours included. Trial employees knew they were in a trial, while control employees were not told the surveys were linked to one. Analysis was not blind and the study was not preregistered. The authors answer the obvious objection, that people might overstate their gains to keep the trial, in two ways. The two surveys are six months apart, which makes earlier answers hard to remember. And there is a telling detail: satisfaction with time rose 2.10 points out of 10, against 0.5 to 0.9 for job, life, household finances and relationships. Their argument: people inflating everything would not draw such clear lines between areas. They also read the 12-month results as a sign that the gains are not merely a Hawthorne effect.

There is no output measure. The paper reports no revenue, sales or delivery data. Work ability is the closest thing, and it is a perception.

## The finding most summaries skip

For a manager, the useful result is not the average. It is the dose.

Compared with control employees and with people whose hours did not drop, and after adjusting for background factors, those who cut 8 hours or more saw burnout improve by 0.271 points. Those who cut 5 to 7 hours: 0.203. Those who cut 1 to 4 hours: 0.098. That is a clear dose-response, and the top group gained almost three times as much as the bottom one.

At company level, there is no such gradient. Organizations that cut a lot and organizations that cut a little improved about equally against the controls. The authors' reading is that organizational cuts work largely by changing each person's actual hours.

And not everyone's hours moved. In the analysis sample, 31.44% of respondents reported the same or more hours, and 8.96 points of that are control-group employees. That leaves about 715 of the 2,896 trial employees, roughly one in four, whose hours did not go down. The paper lists who was less likely to cut: women, non-white employees, parents of school-age children, respondents in Australia and New Zealand, people in larger organizations and in civil, social and other services. Supervisors, meanwhile, appear to gain more from reduced hours.

So the policy is not the treatment. The hours are.

## The mechanism, and one warning sign

Three channels contribute most to the effect, though they do not explain all of it: self-rated work ability, fewer sleep problems, and less fatigue. Work ability, which asks people to rate their current ability against their lifetime best on 0 to 10, rose from 7.04 to 7.83. Fatigue fell from 2.68 to 2.26.

The warning sign is pace. Perceived job demands, meaning tight deadlines and high speed, rose slightly in trial companies, from 3.51 to 3.59 on 1 to 5, although that rise is not statistically different from the control group's. In the regressions, company-wide cuts predicted higher perceived demands for individuals, while a person's own cut of 8 hours or more predicted lower ones. The authors suggest broader restructuring, "probably introducing new responsibilities and tighter deadlines." Fewer hours can also mean denser hours.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Is a 4 day work week 32 hours?

Usually, but not always. The 100-80-100 model tested in the UK pilot and Fan et al. means about 32 hours at full pay. A compressed 4x10 schedule keeps total hours at 40. Tokyo's public-sector model uses 155 hours per month. Same label, different hours.

### Which companies have a 4 day work week?

Microsoft Japan trialed it for one month in August 2019. The Tokyo Metropolitan Government began offering a compressed option in April 2025. Beyond those, the best-documented examples are the 61 UK pilot firms (56 intended to continue) and the 141 volunteer organizations in Fan et al., mostly white-collar and recruited through 4 Day Week Global.

### Do you get paid the same on a 4 day work week?

In the 100-80-100 model, yes. Workers take a 20% reduction in hours with no cut in pay. The compressed 4x10 model also preserves full pay since total hours remain at 40. Both the UK pilot and Fan et al. held wages constant.

### Is a 4x10 schedule better than five 8-hour days?

Bambra's 2008 review found that compressed weeks improved work-life balance, but the studies were low quality and outcomes were mostly self-reported. A 10-hour day can bring more fatigue, and childcare logistics can be harder. Whether it is "better" depends on your role, your commute, and how well you sustain focus past hour eight.

### Did productivity drop in the four-day week trials?

Revenue in the 23 UK firms that reported it rose 1.4% on average. Fan et al. measured well-being, not revenue. Microsoft Japan's 39.9% jump was one company, one month, with concurrent changes. Output mostly held steady where measured, but it was not measured in most trials.

### Is the US moving to a 32-hour work week?

The Thirty-Two-Hour Workweek Act (S. 3947) was introduced in the Senate in March 2024. It would phase the FLSA overtime threshold down from 40 to 32 hours. It has not advanced beyond introduction and is not law. No federal policy change is imminent as of 2026.

## Iceland's "Four-Day Week" Was Actually a 35-to-36-Hour Week

The Iceland trials, run by Reykjavík City Council and the national government from 2015 to 2019, involved more than 2,500 workers, about 1% of the Icelandic workforce. Hours dropped from 40 to 35 or 36 at the same pay.

For many participants, that meant five shorter days, not four. Calling it a "four-day week" is media shorthand.

Service provision was maintained or improved, and well-being rose. The political impact was large: according to Autonomy and the Association for Sustainable Democracy (Alda), about 86% of Iceland's workforce has since moved to shorter hours or gained the right to negotiate them. A policy success, but one about reducing weekly hours, not about a specific day off.

## Does a 4 Day Work Week Increase Productivity?

This is the question everyone asks, and the answer is less clean than the headlines suggest.

**Microsoft Japan** gave employees five Fridays off in August 2019, capped meetings at 30 minutes, and pushed communication online. Sales per employee rose 39.9% versus August 2018. But this was one company, one month, with concurrent process changes. A case study, not a controlled experiment.

The broader evidence on hours and output is older but stronger. Economist John Pencavel, analyzing data on British munitions workers from World War I, found that below about 49 hours per week, output was roughly proportional to hours. Above that, returns diminished sharply. Cutting from 55 to 40 costs less output than you would expect. Cutting from 40 to 32 is a different question, and Pencavel's data does not answer it.

A 2021 WHO/ILO study estimated that working 55+ hours per week led to 745,000 deaths from stroke and ischemic heart disease in 2016: 35% higher stroke risk, 17% higher heart disease mortality versus 35-to-40-hour weeks. That is the case against long weeks, not proof that shorter weeks improve output.

## So, Does the 4 Day Work Week Work?

For well-being in volunteer, mostly white-collar firms: yes, consistently. Burnout drops, sleep improves, fatigue falls, people stay longer.

For output and revenue: probably neutral in the firms that tried it, but the data is thin (23 firms), self-reported, and drawn from companies already optimistic about the idea.

For everyone: unproven. Hourly workers, shift workers, and industries with hard coverage requirements were mostly absent. And the compressed 4x10, which is what many employers actually mean by "four-day week," is a different intervention with different trade-offs. The version matters more than the label.

## What the Trials Changed Before They Cut the Day

The companies that made four-day weeks work did not just cross Friday off the calendar. They redesigned how work happened first.

Microsoft Japan capped meetings at 30 minutes. Fan et al. describe "pre-trial work reorganization" in which companies improved efficiency and collaboration before the six-month clock started. The common thread: fewer meetings, async communication, and protected focus blocks. These are the changes Parkinson's law predicts: work expands to fill the time, so when you cut the time, you cut the filler.

Whether your company runs four days or five, the practical lesson is the same. Your output tracks your focused hours, not your logged hours. Protecting two or three deep work blocks per day from meetings and interruptions is the part of these trials any schedule can copy, even if the headline was about the missing Friday. ## What Is a 4 Day Work Week? Two Models, Not One

The phrase "4 day work week" describes two structurally different schedules.

**The 100-80-100 model** means 100% pay, 80% time, 100% expected output. Workers drop to about 32 hours across four days. This is what the UK pilot and Fan et al. tested.

**The compressed 4x10 schedule** keeps total hours at 40 but packs them into four 10-hour days. Tokyo's Metropolitan Government adopted this model in April 2025, allowing staff to take three days off per week under a flexible system with total monthly hours of 155. Same hours, rearranged.

In the US, the Thirty-Two-Hour Workweek Act (S. 3947) was introduced in March 2024 by Senators Sanders and Butler. It would lower the FLSA overtime threshold from 40 to 32 hours in phases. It has been introduced. It is not law.

The health trade-offs diverge between models. Bambra's 2008 review of compressed schedules found that work-life balance improved, but the studies were low quality and outcomes mostly self-reported. A 10-hour day can bring fatigue that a 32-hour week avoids.

## What I would do with it

I am a CTO and an indie developer, and I built BidForge for small French businesses that answer public tenders, so I read this paper with very small teams in mind. Here is how I would use it rather than quote it.

Reorganize first, and write it down. Trial companies generally redesigned work before the trial, so the paper cannot fully separate its effect from the day off. Treat the list of meetings, reports and approvals you remove as part of the treatment, not as preparation.

Measure hours per person, not the policy. The paper asks two simple questions: "How many hours per week do you usually work in your job?" before, and the same question over the last four weeks after. Look at the distribution, not the mean, and look for who stays at five days.

Choose your output measures before day one, because the study did not have any. Two or three numbers you already track, and a decision rule written in advance.

Watch the pace. Add a question on deadlines and speed. If demands rise while hours fall, you compressed the week instead of reducing it.

In France, check what you are copying. The legal full-time week is 35 hours, and the daily maximum is 10 hours, barring exemptions. A four-day week at 35 hours means 8 hours 45 minutes a day: that is a compressed week, which this study did not test. The same 20% cut applied to 35 hours gives 28.

And if you work alone, this paper says nothing directly about you. It studied employees inside organizations.

The four-day week in this study is not a calendar change. It is a cut in low-value work, then a cut in hours, measured person by person. Copy all three, or do not cite the paper.
