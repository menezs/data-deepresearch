Historical approaches to working hours, particularly the resistance by employers to reduce working hours, have shaped current economic models by embedding assumptions that longer hours equate to greater output. This has influenced the development of production functions which assume continuous positive marginal returns, disregarding empirical findings that indicate diminishing returns beyond specific working hour thresholds .

Between 1960 and 2000, the demographic composition of long-hour workers in the US shifted with increases in the probability of long hours for both men and women as education levels rose. Age and self-employment remained significant factors, with older age groups and self-employed individuals more likely to work long hours .

Historical data, such as observations collected during the World Wars and analysis of contemporary labor markets, shows that while production initially increases with longer hours, beyond a certain point productivity falls due to worker fatigue and errors. This challenges the assumption that longer hours always equate to higher output .

The principle challenged is the assumption that longer working hours directly correlate with increased productivity, which contradicts the law of diminishing returns. Empirical research indicates that after a certain point, additional hours of work result in reduced productivity due to diminishing returns .

The employer's understanding of the production function influences work schedules by assuming complete knowledge of how inputs, including labor, affect output. This often leads them to set longer working hours under the belief that it maximizes productivity. However, historical and empirical evidence suggests that this understanding is incomplete, as often the resulting production does not account for diminishing returns .

According to OECD data, American labor patterns exhibit higher average annual hours than other high-income countries, with a significant portion of the workforce working 40 or more hours per week. This contrasts with countries like Germany and France, which have considerably lower average annual hours .

The 'lump of labor' hypothesis suggests that there is a fixed amount of work to be done, implying that reducing hours could lead to increased employment to maintain output levels. However, research suggests this notion is flawed; reductions in working hours often require proportionally larger increases in employment in high-hour contexts compared to low-hour settings to achieve the same output .

Verifying the law of diminishing returns for working hours is critical for economic models as it underpins many theorems about labor supply and productivity. Without confirmation, predictions made using these models could be flawed, particularly in optimizing firms' labor inputs and responses to policy changes about working hours .

US Census data indicates that the probability of working long hours increases with higher education levels. In 2000, college graduates had a significantly higher probability of working long hours compared to high school graduates, with a difference of 7.6 percentage points for men and 6.9 for women .

The trend from 1979 to 2015 shows that average annual working hours have decreased in both the US and Europe, but the reduction has been less pronounced in the US. This suggests persistent cultural differences regarding work, with the US maintaining longer working hours possibly due to fewer statutory holidays and a higher fraction of the workforce engaged in lengthy workweeks .
