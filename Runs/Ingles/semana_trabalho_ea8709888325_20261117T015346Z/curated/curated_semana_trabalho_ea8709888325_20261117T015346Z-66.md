**45 German companies tested the 4-day week for six months. The headlines afterwards: “Success!”, “Future of Work!”, “Breakthrough!”. The reality: 70 percent continue, 20 percent return to the 5-day week, and 10 percent are still undecided. That’s not a failure. But it’s not a no-brainer either. The honest assessment lies between the extremes – and that’s exactly where it becomes relevant for decision-makers.**

From February to August 2024, 45 companies from across Germany tested the shortened workweek. The University of Münster, led by Prof. Julia Backmann, provided scientific support. The range spanned from a 12-person startup to a medium-sized production company. Most participants reduced their hours to 32-36 hours with full pay.

**What is the 4-day week?** The 4-day week refers to a working time model where the weekly working hours are distributed over four days – either with the same number of hours (compressed) or with reduced hours (typically 32 instead of 40) with full salary compensation. Most pilot projects test the reduced variant: fewer hours, same pay, same or higher productivity as a goal.

The results are nuanced. Revenue and profit remained at the previous year’s level. That sounds unspectacular, but it’s the crucial number: the same economic output with 20 percent less working hours implies a productivity increase of around 25 percent. Whether this is sustainable or a pilot effect (higher motivation, selection of engaged companies) can only be shown by long-term observation.

Sources: Uni Münster/intraprenör (2025), 4 Day Week Global UK Pilot (2023)

The self-reported productivity was significantly higher among participants than in the control group (7.69 vs. 6.83 on a 10-point scale). The Bertelsmann Foundation’s conclusion is sobering: the 4-day week is an opportunity for more flexible working time models, but it’s not a panacea and not a replacement for good leadership.

The trial reported a positive impact on employee recruitment and retention, with participating organizations observing a significant increase in employee numbers during the pilot period. The 4DW was perceived as an attractive benefit, making employers more appealing and competitive in the job market, thus facilitating recruitment. Employee retention was likely supported by increased job attraction and satisfaction due to the additional time off, as reflected in qualitative reports of enhanced work-life balance and life satisfaction .

The key selection criteria for organizations to participate in the 4-day workweek trial in Germany included a willingness to test reduced working hours without a proportional salary reduction, diversity in industry representation, company size, and active involvement of key decision-makers. Organizations intending to reduce pay proportional to work hour reductions or execute compressed workweeks were excluded. This ensured a homogenized sample focused on truly evaluating the feasibility and impact of a 4DW under constant salary conditions, essential for assessing its benefits without confounding variables related to pay cuts .

The integration of qualitative and quantitative data provided robust, multi-dimensional insights into the 4DW's effects. Quantitative data offered measurable changes in productivity, satisfaction, and health metrics, supported by large-scale survey results. Meanwhile, qualitative interviews provided contextual depth, capturing individual perceptions and organizational dynamics that were not fully reflected in numerical data. This dual approach allowed the research team to validate findings across methodologies, understand nuanced impacts on employee and organizational behavior, and address potential biases inherent in self-reported data .

The main findings indicated a significant increase in overall life satisfaction among participants of the 4DW trial, primarily driven by more free time, as confirmed by both quantitative survey data and qualitative interview responses. Over 90% of participants reported increased well-being, with 50% indicating substantial improvement. In contrast, job satisfaction showed no strong quantitative evidence of a positive change, likely due to high pre-existing levels. This paradox was attributed to already flexible working hours and a broader perspective on life improvements rather than just work satisfaction .

The 4DW trial findings revealed a positive impact on health-related behaviors, with participants exhibiting increased physical activity and longer sleep durations compared to the control group. Participants logged, on average, 24.45 more minutes of physical activity weekly and slept approximately 38 minutes longer per week. The increase in physical activity was attributed to the additional free time, allowing for more engagement in sports and recreation. The longer sleep duration, particularly from Thursday to Sunday, suggested enhanced restfulness due to reduced work demands .

The 2-year follow-up (published February 2026) shows: 70 percent of participants continue to work in some reduced form. 22 percent have adapted their model – instead of sticking to the pure 4-day week, they’ve developed hybrid variants: 4.5 days, flexible Fridays, or seasonal models. This paints a more realistic picture than “4 days for everyone forever.”

The 20 percent who dropped out are the more instructive part of the study. Their reasons can be condensed into three patterns:

**Customer Contact Issues:** Companies with a high proportion of external communication – agencies, consultancies, service providers – struggled to maintain availability. Customers expect five-day availability. An internal Friday off doesn’t work if the customer calls on Fridays.

**Compression Stress:** Squeezing 40 hours of work into 32 hours isn’t a reduction in working hours – it’s a compression of working hours. Some teams reported higher stress levels on the four working days because buffers and informal communication were lost. Life satisfaction increased on the free day, but pressure on working days also rose.

**Inequality Within Teams:** In companies with production-related and knowledge-based roles, a two-class society emerged. Office employees got the free Friday. Production, warehouse, and customer service had to remain staffed for five days. This inequality generated frustration that consumed the cultural benefits of the 4-day week.

The British pilot project (61 companies, 2,900 employees, 2022-2023) provides the most robust data to date. The results are significantly more positive than the German ones: 92 percent of companies permanently adopted the 4-day week after the pilot. 62 percent of employees reported less burnout. 41 percent reported improved mental well-being.

Why the difference from Germany? Two factors: Firstly, the UK study was more focused on knowledge workers – industries where reducing working hours is easier. Secondly, the British pilot group was self-selected from companies that already had a high willingness to change. The German study had a broader mix of industries and thus more realistic results.

Iceland provides the longest dataset: Between 2015 and 2019, 2,500 employees (over 1 percent of the working population) tested the reduced week. The result: productivity remained stable or increased, well-being measurably improved. Today, 86 percent of Icelandic employees either work reduced hours or have the right to do so. Iceland is proof of concept at the national level – and at the same time proof that the transition doesn’t have to be disruptive but can be gradual.

The honest assessment: The 4-day week works for knowledge workers in organizations willing to overhaul their processes. It doesn’t work as a simple “one day less” for everyone. It requires active process optimization, meeting reduction, and clear prioritization. Without this preparation, it’s just work intensification – and that makes people sick instead of productive.

What’s missing: Long-term data beyond two years. Hard output metrics instead of self-assessment. And an honest answer to the fairness question in mixed organizations. The 4-day week isn’t a gift to employees. It’s an organizational development project that only works if treated as such.

The counterposition: Critics argue that pilot projects take place under ideal conditions – highly motivated teams, media attention, temporary Hawthorne effect. The real test comes when the 4-day week becomes normal and initial enthusiasm wears off. The 2-year follow-up data show initial adjustments: 22 percent have already modified their model.

**1. What proportion of value creation is location-independent?** The higher the proportion of knowledge-based work, the better the 4-day week works. In organizations with more than 70 percent knowledge work, pilot data consistently show positive results. Below 50 percent, it becomes difficult without creating fairness problems.

**2. Are meetings ready to be reduced?** The most successful pilot participants reformed their meeting culture before reducing working hours: meetings from 60 to 30 minutes, asynchronous voting instead of meetings, “No-Meeting-Days”. Without this step, work just becomes more intense.

**3. What’s Plan B?** 20 percent of German pilot participants have returned to the old system. That’s not a failure – that’s an expected outcome. Anyone testing the 4-day week needs a defined evaluation period, clear metrics, and an exit plan that doesn’t trigger a debate about loss of face.

## Is there a 4-day week in Germany?

No. Germany has no national 4-day week and no law that shortens the standard week. The first pilot study in Germany was a six-month trial in 2024, run by Intraprenör, the University of Münster and 4 Day Week Global. In early 2024, 45 organisations aimed to trial a shorter week and 44 started; two large ones dropped out after two months and one that cut pay in proportion was excluded, so the report analysed 41. About 900 employees switched, and weekly hours fell by 3.95 on average. Revenue and profit showed no significant change from the previous year, and participants slept 38 minutes a week longer than a control group, but the report found no strong evidence of fewer sick days.

A follow-up report found that in February 2026, 70% of the 40 responding organisations still operated some form of reduced working time, 30% did not, and 22% had adapted their model.

Policy points the other way. Chancellor Friedrich Merz said in May 2025 that with a four-day week and work-life balance Germany would not keep its prosperity. The 2025 coalition agreement wants to make a weekly instead of a daily maximum working time possible. In the Saarland steel industry, an IG Metall agreement of December 2023 lets company-level parties cut the 35-hour week to 32 hours, with 33 hours paid, where jobs are under pressure.

Caveat: the pilot firms chose to take part, and the organisers excluded firms that planned to cut pay or only compress hours.

Participant organizations assessed productivity changes primarily through self-reported survey data and qualitative interviews. Employees often associated productivity with output, stating they completed the same workload in less time, indicating an increase in productivity. However, this understanding conflated output with productivity, creating interpretational challenges. The lack of objective performance data limited the ability to draw definitive conclusions. Only two organizations provided such data, which showed no significant differences compared to the previous year. The challenges in interpretation arise from the colloquial understanding of productivity and potential self-reporting bias .

Organizational compliance and engagement were pivotal in the trial's design and execution, requiring robust commitment from top management and decision-makers. The design emphasized the voluntary nature of participation and set strict criteria, excluding organizations wanting to reduce salaries or compress existing schedules. Active engagement was crucial for reliable data collection and implementation fidelity, enabling assessment of genuine 4DW impacts. Additionally, ongoing communication through webinar consultations and events ensured sustained organizational engagement throughout the trial phase .

The 4-day workweek trial employed a mixed-methods approach, incorporating both qualitative and quantitative data collection techniques. Quantitative methods included questionnaire-based surveys targeting all participants, while qualitative methods focused on interviews with select individuals across various roles within the organizations. The surveys were translated and adapted for the local context by the University of Münster, ensuring the meaning remained consistent. Additionally, objective measurements, such as smartwatch tracking data and hair samples, were integrated to provide broader insights, combining subjective and objective assessments. This methodological diversity was significant as it allowed for a comprehensive understanding of the impact of the 4DW on organizations and employees, capturing both perceived changes and measurable outcomes .

The trial managed potential biases through a combination of survey design, rewording of questions, and mixed-method data collection. Participants were inadvertently reporting on output rather than productivity, leading to potential biases. The researchers addressed this by asking follow-up questions to clarify definitions, pointing out discrepancies between subjective reports and objective metrics. The mixed-methods approach, involving both qualitative insights from interviews and quantitative surveys, provided a more nuanced understanding and cross-validation of participant responses, somewhat mitigating self-report bias .

The 4-day week is neither revolution nor fad. It’s a working time model that works under certain conditions – and not under others. The 70/20/10 distribution of the German pilot is the most realistic result there is: a gain for the majority, not feasible for a fifth, and still unclear for the rest. For those considering it: six months of piloting with clear KPIs, honest evaluation, and a defined Plan B. Those who can’t set this up aren’t ready for the 4-day week – regardless of whether the idea works.

The data is nuanced. In the German pilot, sales and profits remained stable despite a 20 percent reduction in working hours – implying a productivity increase of around 25 percent. Self-reported productivity was 7.69 vs. 6.83 in the control group. Hard output data (unit numbers, code lines, contract closures) is lacking in most studies. The honest answer: productivity per hour demonstrably increases. Whether absolute output remains the same depends on the company.

Significantly more challenging than in knowledge work. Production lines require physical presence and cannot be compensated by more efficient meetings. Some models work with shift systems (alternating Monday-Thursday and Tuesday-Friday), others with a 36-hour week instead of 32. The fairness question between office and production is the biggest obstacle in mixed organizations.

Direct costs are manageable: process consulting, workshop moderation, pilot guidance. Indirect costs are higher: reorganization of processes, adjustment of IT systems, training of managers. Most pilot participants report a 3 to 6-month lead time. Revenue side: fewer absences, higher employer attractiveness, and potentially lower recruiting costs.

Not in Germany. The Part-Time and Fixed-Term Employment Act (TzBfG) gives employees the right to reduce working hours, but not to a specific distribution of days. The 4-day week at full pay is a voluntary employer decision. In Iceland, the majority of the working population already works reduced hours or has the right to do so. There is currently no EU legislation mandating a 4-day week.

## Working in Germany

### Working hours

Employed people work 34.5 hours a week on average (OECD, usual hours in the main job, 2025). The Working Hours Act caps the working day at eight hours, extendable to ten if the average does not exceed eight hours over six months or 24 weeks. Under the Federal Leave Act, working days (Werktage) are all days except Sundays and public holidays, so Saturday counts.

### Paid leave and holidays

The Federal Leave Act gives at least 24 working days a year (Werktage, which include Saturdays), the equivalent of four six-day weeks. For someone working five days a week that is 20 days, and public holidays are not counted as leave.

### Part-time and flexible work

In 2024, 28.9% of employed people aged 20-64 worked part-time, against 17.1% in the EU. Employees with more than six months' service at a firm that normally has more than 15 employees have a legal right to reduce their hours, with three months' notice. The employer must agree unless operational reasons stand in the way, and a further request is possible at the earliest two years after the employer agreed to a reduction or justifiably refused one.

### Remote work

In 2025, 22.9% of employees aged 15-64 worked from home usually (11.6%) or sometimes (11.3%), according to Eurostat.
