# Early HPV vaccination is erasing cervical cancer

HPV vaccination prevents cervical cancer, and the proof now comes from cancer registries as well as from trials that measured precancer. HPV causes more than 95% of cervical cancers, and about 90% of cervical cancer deaths occur in low- and middle-income countries.[1] In 2022 the disease caused an estimated 662,044 new cases and 348,709 deaths.[2] A 2025 Cochrane review of 225 real-world studies found that girls vaccinated at or before age 16 had about **80% lower risk of cervical cancer**, and no increased risk of the syndromes behind past safety scares.[3] In Scotland, **no invasive cancers** had occurred among women vaccinated at 12 or 13, whether they received one, two or three doses.[4] In December 2025 a randomized trial in 20,330 Costa Rican girls showed that **one dose protected as well as two** against persistent HPV16/18 infection.[5][6] The main constraint is now delivery: only **33% of eligible girls** worldwide received even a first dose in 2025, against WHO's target of 90% fully vaccinated by age 15.[7][8] Vaccinated cohorts will take decades to reach the ages of peak risk, so screening and treatment remain essential to WHO's goal of incidence below 4 per 100,000 women in every country.[8]

## Persistent infection with a dozen HPV types drives almost every case

HPV is a non-enveloped, double-stranded DNA virus. Its capsid is built from the L1 (major) and L2 (minor) proteins, and more than 200 types have been identified.[9] WHO classifies 12 types as high-risk (cancer-causing): 16, 18, 31, 33, 35, 39, 45, 51, 52, 56, 58 and 59. HPV16 is the most oncogenic.[9] Most infections are harmless. Between 70% and 90% cause no symptoms and clear within one to two years.[9] Only about 5–10% of infected women develop a persistent infection, which WHO calls a necessary precursor of cervical cancer. Progression to invasive cancer usually takes 15–20 years or longer.[9]

The virus reaches the basal layer of the epithelium through micro-abrasions. Two viral oncoproteins then drive cancer. E6 promotes the breakdown of the p53 tumor suppressor, which disables cell-cycle control and programmed cell death. E7 binds the retinoblastoma protein (pRb) and pushes the cell into DNA synthesis.[10] Women living with HIV have an estimated sixfold higher risk, and they account for about 20% of cervical cancers in sub-Saharan Africa.[9] Natural infection gives weak immunity: only 70–80% of women make antibodies after infection, and the response is slow with low titers.[9] Vaccination fills that gap.

The mix of HPV types sets the limit on what each vaccine can prevent. HPV16 and HPV18 cause **71% of cervical cancers** worldwide. The next most common types are HPV45 (6%), HPV31 (4%), HPV33 (4%), HPV52 (3%) and HPV58 (2%). The seven cancer-causing types in the nonavalent (9-type) vaccine account for about **90% of HPV-positive squamous cell carcinomas**.[9] HPV also causes nearly all anal squamous cell carcinomas, and the low-risk types 6 and 11 cause up to 90% of anogenital warts.[9]

The burden follows poverty. GLOBOCAN 2022 put age-standardized incidence at 14.1 per 100,000 women and mortality at 7.1, making cervical cancer the fourth most common cancer and the fourth leading cause of cancer death in women.[2] China and India account for 42% of cases. Eswatini has the highest incidence in the world, at 95.9 per 100,000. Mortality in countries with a low Human Development Index (HDI) is 16.3 per 100,000, nearly five times the rate of 3.3 in very-high-HDI countries.[2] WHO reports that 19 of the 20 hardest-hit countries are in Africa.[1] If 2022 rates stay the same, cases will rise 56.8% to about 1 million and deaths 80.7% to about 630,000 by 2050.[2] Because cancer takes so long to develop, vaccine effects show up first in infection and precancer rates and reach invasive cancer only one to two decades later.

## Virus-like particles produce antibody levels far above natural infection

Every HPV vaccine is made from recombinant L1 protein that self-assembles into virus-like particles (VLPs). These particles contain no viral DNA and cannot cause infection. They are given as an intramuscular dose with an adjuvant, an ingredient that strengthens the immune response.[9] The resulting antibody levels are **1–4 log units higher than after natural infection**, or roughly 10 to 10,000 times higher. They peak about four weeks after the last dose, fall over 12–18 months, then plateau.[9] The antibodies also bind more tightly (higher avidity). WHO suggests that VLP antigen persisting in lymph nodes may explain both the tight binding and the protection seen after a single dose.[9] Neutralizing IgG reaches the cervix by crossing the genital epithelium and by leaking out at sites of micro-trauma. There it is thought to block the virus before it reaches basal cells.[9][10] One caveat: no minimum protective antibody level has been established.[9]

The FDA licensed Gardasil, a quadrivalent vaccine against types 6, 11, 16 and 18, in 2006. Australia and the EU licensed Cervarix, a bivalent 16/18 vaccine with the AS04 adjuvant, in 2007. The FDA licensed Gardasil 9, which adds types 31, 33, 45, 52 and 58, in 2014.[10] Asian manufacturers have since widened supply. Innovax's Cecolin, a bivalent vaccine made in *E. coli*, was licensed in China in December 2019 and in 2021 became the fourth HPV vaccine prequalified by WHO.[11] Walrinvax, from Walvax's Zerun subsidiary, was licensed in China in March 2022.[12] WHO prequalified it in August 2024 as the fifth HPV vaccine, and in October 2024 WHO cleared Cecolin for single-dose use.[1] The Serum Institute of India's quadrivalent Cervavac, licensed for girls and boys aged 9–26, was prequalified effective 25 September 2026.[13][14] China approved Cecolin 9, its first domestic nonavalent vaccine, in mid-2025 (reports differ on whether in late May or June). It showed over 98% efficacy against persistent infection in trials with more than 11,000 volunteers.[15][16] We found no evidence that Cecolin 9 has been prequalified. WHO judges the bivalent, quadrivalent and nonavalent vaccines to give comparable protection against cervical precancer and cancer.[9]

## Protection is near-total when girls are vaccinated at 12 or 13

The licensing trials used high-grade precancer as the endpoint, measured as cervical intraepithelial neoplasia grade 2 or 3 or worse (CIN2+/CIN3+). An invasive-cancer endpoint was neither ethical nor feasible. These trials showed that infection status at the time of vaccination largely determines benefit. In FUTURE II (12,167 women aged 15–26, quadrivalent vaccine), efficacy against HPV16/18-related CIN2/3 or adenocarcinoma in situ was 98% in women not previously infected, but 44% among all randomized women, including those already infected.[17] FUTURE I found 100% per-protocol efficacy against vaccine-type cervical lesions.[18] In PATRICIA (18,644 women, Cervarix), efficacy against CIN3+ from any HPV type was **93.2% in women HPV-naive at baseline** but 45.6% in the full cohort.[19] In the nonavalent trial (14,215 women), efficacy against high-grade disease caused by the five added types was 96.7%, rising to 97.4% with longer follow-up.[20][21] A 2025 Cochrane review of 60 trials with 157,414 participants confirmed that Cervarix, Gardasil and Gardasil 9 reduce precancer in people aged 15–25.[22] As one commentator noted, though, no randomized evidence exists for the under-15 age group that programs target.[23]

National registries filled that gap and confirmed what the trials predicted. In Sweden, 1,672,983 girls and women were followed from 2006 to 2017. The adjusted incidence rate ratio (IRR) for invasive cancer was **0.12 for vaccination before age 17**, an 88% reduction, and 0.47 for vaccination at 17–30.[24] In Denmark, the IRR was 0.14 for vaccination at age 16 or younger. It was 0.32 at ages 17–19, which was not statistically significant, and there was no reduction for vaccination at 20–30.[25] England used the bivalent vaccine. By mid-2019, cervical cancer was 87% lower in the cohort offered vaccination at 12–13, 62% lower at 14–16 and 34% lower at 16–18.[26] A 2024 update, with data to mid-2020, estimated that vaccination had prevented 687 cancers and 23,192 cases of CIN3. The largest absolute gains were in the most deprived fifth of the population: 192 cancers prevented, against 61 in the least deprived fifth.[27] Scotland reported zero cancers among women vaccinated at 12–13. Women vaccinated at 14–22 with three doses had 3.2 cancers per 100,000 person-years, against 8.4 in unvaccinated women.[4] In the United States, cervical cancer deaths among women under 25 fell 62% over a decade. The authors attribute this "likely" to vaccination, but the link is ecological rather than measured in individuals.[28]

The Cochrane review of real-world programs pooled these data from more than 132 million people in 46 countries. Across all ages, vaccination cut cervical cancer risk by 63% (RR 0.37). For vaccination at or before age 16, the reduction was about 80% (RR 0.20, 95% CI 0.09–0.44), and CIN3+ fell 74%. These estimates were rated moderate certainty, but most included studies carried a serious or critical risk of bias from residual confounding.[3] Screening behavior is the obvious candidate for such confounding. Even so, a causal reading is better supported. The same steep age gradient appears in four countries and with two vaccines, it mirrors the gap between infected and uninfected women seen in the trials, and it has a clear biological mechanism. Herd effects add to the benefit. A meta-analysis of 65 studies from 14 high-income countries examined programs 5–8 years after introduction. HPV16/18 prevalence had fallen 83% in girls aged 13–19, and anogenital warts had declined even in unvaccinated boys and older women.[29]

Protection has shown no waning so far. In an IARC cohort of about 17,000 Indian girls vaccinated in 2009–2010, single-dose efficacy against persistent HPV16/18 infection was 92.0% after an average of 12 years. No vaccinated girl developed HPV16/18-related high-grade precancer.[30] Protection beyond about 15 years is plausible but has not yet been measured directly.

## A randomized trial confirms that one dose matches two

The ESCUDDO trial, published in the *New England Journal of Medicine* in December 2025, gave the single-dose question a randomized answer.[5] Costa Rican girls aged 12–16 received one dose of bivalent or nonavalent vaccine. Six months later, half received a second dose and half received a control vaccine. Those aged 15 and older were tested every six months for five years.[31] Efficacy against persistent HPV16/18 infection was 98.2% with one bivalent dose and 97.8% with two. For the nonavalent vaccine it was 97.0% with one dose and 98.5% with two. One dose was noninferior to two for both vaccines.[6][31]

ESCUDDO measured persistent HPV16/18 infection, not precancer or cancer, although an AP report described its result as about 97% protection "against cervical cancer."[32] Other studies agree with it. Kenya's KEN SHE trial, in 2,275 women aged 15–20, found 97.5% single-dose efficacy against persistent HPV16/18 infection at 18 months for both bivalent and nonavalent vaccines.[33] The Indian cohort and Scotland's zero cancers after one or two doses at age 12–13 point the same way.[30][4] Evidence that one dose prevents cancer itself is therefore still observational. Single-dose evidence is also thinner for boys and for HPV-related cancers other than cervical cancer.[32]

## Hundreds of millions of doses show no serious harm, but scares cost whole cohorts

By 2017, WHO's Global Advisory Committee on Vaccine Safety (GACVS) had reviewed data on more than 270 million doses and judged HPV vaccines "extremely safe." It found anaphylaxis at about 1.7 per million doses. UK and US surveillance ruled out a Guillain-Barré syndrome (GBS) risk above 1 per million doses. GACVS found no causal link with postural orthostatic tachycardia syndrome (POTS), complex regional pain syndrome (CRPS) or premature ovarian insufficiency.[34] The 2025 Cochrane review supports this. Pooled risk ratios were 0.99 for POTS, 0.96 for chronic fatigue syndrome, 0.76 for CRPS and 0.62 for paralysis, all at moderate certainty, and there was no increased risk of infertility. GBS remains the least certain outcome: case-series studies gave a risk ratio of 1.53 with a wide confidence interval (0.78–2.98), rated low certainty.[3] In ESCUDDO, 7 of more than 20,000 participants had serious adverse events possibly related to vaccination, though the trial was not designed to assess safety.[6]

Safety scares, not safety signals, have done the real damage. Japan suspended its proactive recommendation in June 2013, two months after its program began. It resumed recommendations only in April 2022.[35] Coverage fell from 79.46% for girls born in fiscal year 1997 to 0.83% for the fiscal 2002 cohort, and researchers say the cohorts born in fiscal years 2000–2005 now need much better screening.[35] GACVS had warned in 2015 that policy decisions based on weak evidence cause "real harm."[34] In Denmark, uptake fell to 49.6% of baseline during heavy negative media coverage in 2015. A national campaign brought it back to 109.2% of baseline, but about 26,000 fewer girls started vaccination than would have otherwise.[36] In Ireland, uptake fell from 87% to about 50% by early 2017 after an online anti-vaccine campaign. A health-service response with an alliance of 40 non-governmental organizations brought provisional first-dose uptake back to 61% in 2017/18.[37] A 2014 episode in Colombia is often cited, but our sources did not document it, so it is not assessed here.

## Policy has moved to single doses, but only one girl in three is reached

WHO's December 2022 recommendations followed an April 2022 conclusion by its advisory group SAGE that one dose offers solid protection. They allow one or two doses at ages 9–20 and two doses from age 21. People who are immunocompromised should receive at least two doses and ideally three. Girls aged 9–14 are the primary target, and boys are a secondary target "where feasible and affordable."[38] The number of countries using a single dose rose from 37 in 2023 to 57 in September 2024.[1] By the end of 2024 it was 65 of the 147 countries with HPV vaccine in their schedules, and 79 of those countries also vaccinated boys.[39] Gavi, the vaccine alliance, estimated that switching to one dose freed enough vaccine for more than 24 million additional girls in 2023–2024.[40]

Coverage has improved but remains far below target. In 2025, about 22.5 million of 66.1 million eligible girls received a first dose. Of the 43.6 million girls missed, 22.8 million lived in countries without an HPV program.[7] Fifteen countries introduced the vaccine in 2025, including China and Pakistan. Those introductions added about 2.5 million girls, but a fall of about 1.3 million in Bangladesh nearly cancelled the gain.[7] Gavi reported in November 2025 that it had reached its cumulative target of 86 million girls, based on preliminary data. It projects that this will avert about 1.4 million future deaths. Gavi countries pay US$2.90–5.18 per dose, against US$100 or more elsewhere, and first-dose coverage in Africa reached 44% by the end of 2024, above Europe's 38%.[41]

The United States has become a procedural outlier. About 63% of US teenagers have completed both doses, a figure unchanged for four years.[32] On 5 January 2026, a memo from the Department of Health and Human Services (HHS) cut the HPV recommendation to one dose without a vote by the Advisory Committee on Immunization Practices (ACIP). It also stopped requiring insurers to cover additional doses.[42] In mid-March 2026, a federal judge in Boston granted a preliminary injunction in a suit brought by the American Academy of Pediatrics. The injunction blocked the schedule changes and the reconstituted ACIP, and an appeal was expected.[43][44] In September 2026, AP reported that the order still blocked the HPV change and that experts still advised two doses for children.[32] We could not confirm the case's status after that report. The one-dose schedule itself matches WHO policy and ESCUDDO; the dispute is over how the change was made. An April 2026 *JAMA Oncology* study also found that vaccinated males were nearly 50% less likely to develop certain head and neck, esophageal, anal or penile cancers.[32]

## Elimination below 4 per 100,000 needs both vaccination and screening

In August 2020 the World Health Assembly adopted WHO's elimination strategy (resolution WHA73.2). It defines elimination as incidence below 4 per 100,000 women in every country. Its 2030 targets, known as 90-70-90, are 90% of girls fully vaccinated by 15, 70% of women screened with a high-performance test by 35 and again by 45, and 90% of women with precancer or cancer treated.[8][45] WHO's 2020 modeling projected about 62 million deaths averted by 2120, while the 2022 position paper estimated 45 million deaths over 100 years.[8][9] With global incidence at 14.1 per 100,000, most of the world remains several times above the threshold.[2]

Vaccination alone cannot reach the threshold in time, because women who are already adults gain nothing from vaccinating adolescents. Australia shows how the two tools combine. A modeling study projected that its incidence would fall below 4 per 100,000 around 2028 (range 2021–2035), but only if high coverage is maintained for both vaccination and five-yearly HPV screening.[46] That date is a projection, and we found no observed data on whether Australia has reached it.

Screening is adapting to vaccinated populations. The American Cancer Society's December 2025 guideline recommends primary HPV testing every five years from age 25 and accepts self-collected samples.[47] A December 2024 draft from the US Preventive Services Task Force (USPSTF) adds self-collected HPV testing for ages 30–65. We could not verify whether that draft has been finalized, and neither guideline yet sets separate rules for vaccinated women.[47][48] As vaccination lowers disease prevalence, a smaller share of abnormal cytology results reflect true lesions. That favors HPV-based testing and longer screening intervals. Japan is the opposite case: there, screening must protect the cohorts the suspension left exposed.[35]

## Conclusion

The evidence shows that age at vaccination matters more than the product or the number of doses. Every vaccine type gives near-total protection when given before exposure, and protection declines steadily the later vaccination starts. That makes one dose at ages 9–14 the most efficient policy, because each dose saved can go to a girl who would otherwise go unvaccinated, and unvaccinated girls are now the binding constraint. The weak points are delivery and trust, not the science. Bangladesh's 2025 decline erased most of a year's global gains, and Japan's nine-year suspension left birth cohorts that only screening can now protect.

Three uncertainties remain: cancer-endpoint evidence for single doses is observational, durability beyond about 15 years has not been measured, and evidence for boys and for non-cervical cancers lags. None of these justifies delay. A 12-year-old vaccinated today reaches peak cervical cancer risk around 2050, the year when, at current rates, global deaths are projected to approach 630,000. Coverage this decade will decide which side of that projection she lands on.
