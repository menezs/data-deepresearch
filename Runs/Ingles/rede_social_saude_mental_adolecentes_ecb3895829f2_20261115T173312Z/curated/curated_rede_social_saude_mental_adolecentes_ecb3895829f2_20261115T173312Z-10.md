**November 10, 2026** 

From 2023-2025, U.S. high school students reported broad mental health improvements, with some indicators of distress showing historic declines in the 2025 Youth Risk Behavior Survey (YRBS) and Youth Health in Focus report from the CDC. The American Foundation for Suicide Prevention (AFSP), the largest private funder of suicide prevention research, recognizes these improvements as encouraging but cautions that distress rates remain high: 33% of high school students report experiencing persistent feelings of sadness or hopelessness and amongst these students, 29% reported an unmet need for counseling or therapy, with greater impacts for some groups by sex, race, and ethnicity. These findings underscore the need to continue prioritizing youth mental health research, awareness, and advocacy and to strengthen public health, community, and interpersonal supports for all youth, particularly those disproportionately affected. In 2025, the question about transgender identity was eliminated from the survey despite evidence that transgender youth experience disproportionately greater suicide risk associated with stigma, discrimination, and other adverse experiences.

The 2025 YRBS survey examined two- and 10-year trends. This nationally representative survey of U.S. high school students has run since 1991. The student sample decreased by nearly half (47%) from 20,103 students in 2023 to 10,710 in 2025 raising the question of whether these findings fully reflect the experiences of all U.S. high school students. Other federally funded studies support the findings.

**What areas of youth mental health and suicide behavior are improving?**

From 2023 to 2025, all six mental health and suicide-related indicators were reported improved:

- 
**Persistent sadness or hopelessness:** Declined from 40% to 33%, the largest two-year decrease since 1999

- 
**Poor mental health:** Declined from 29% to 23%

- 
**Seriously considered attempting suicide:** Declined from 20% to 14%

- 
**Made a suicide plan:** Declined from 16% to 12%, the largest two-year decrease since the survey’s inception

- 
**Suicide attempts:** Declined from 9% to 6%, the largest two-year decrease since the survey’s inception

- 
**Suicide attempts that required medical treatment:** Declined from 2% to 1%

**What helps protect youth's mental health and prevent suicide?**

Protective factors support mental health and reduce suicide risk. The 2025 YRBS data reports improvements over 2023 with students feeling greater school connectedness and support from caring adults.

- 
**Feel close to people at their school** : increased from 55% to nearly two-thirds (63%).

- 
**An adult in their household tried hard to make sure their basic needs are met** : increased from 86% to 89%.

- 
**Parents or other adults in their family know where they are going or with whom they will be with** : increased from 84% to 87%.

Findings from the 2023 YRBS data showed that students reporting greater school connectedness were less likely to report suicidal thoughts, plans, and attempts, consistent with prior research.

**American Indian/Alaska Native (AI/AN) Students** 

While still high, rates of persistent feelings of sadness or hopelessness, seriously considering suicide, and attempted suicide decreased slightly as reported by AI/AN students. However, more than one in four (27%) AI/AN students reported making a suicide plan in 2025, consistent with 2023 findings, whereas this indicator decreased for White students, with 17% reporting making a plan in 2023 compared to 12% in 2025.

**Native Hawaiian/Other Pacific Island (NA/OPI) Students**  

NH/OPI saw the largest increases of adverse mental health and suicide related outcomes between 2023 and 2025. Persistent feelings of sadness or hopelessness rose from 26% to 41%, serious consideration of suicide rose from 16% to 31%, suicide planning rose from 11% to 28%, and suicide attempts rose from 15% to 22%.

**Black and Hispanic or Latino Students** 

All four mental health and suicide-related indicators decreased for Black and Hispanic or Latino students from 2023 to 2025, consistent with high school students overall. Some indicators stand out. Black students who said they considered making a suicide plan decreased from 16% in 2023 to 11% in 2025. Hispanic or Latino students who reported making a suicide attempt decreased from 11% in 2023 to 8% in 2025. Still, both groups reported higher rates of sadness or hopelessness and suicide attempts than White students.

**Grade**  

Ninth and 10th graders were more likely than 11th or 12th graders to report making a suicide plan and report a suicide attempt in 2025, consistent with previous years’ findings.

**Do all schools participate in the YRBS survey?**

The YBRS overall response rate declined from 60.3% in 2019 to 30.6% in 2025. The 2025 student sample included 10,710 students, compared with 20,103 in 2023. In 2025, 40 states participated; Alabama, Colorado, Florida, Georgia, Idaho, Iowa, Minnesota, Oregon, Washington, and Wyoming did not. State participation declined from 47 states in 2015 to 40 in 2025, reaching a low of 39 in 2023.

**What advocacy and policies are helping youth mental health and suicide prevention?**

This data informs AFSP’s research, advocacy, and public education efforts. As revealed in the YRBS, 29% of students who reported persistent feelings of sadness or hopelessness also reported an unmet need for mental health services (indicating they wanted help but were unable to access it due to cost, uncertainty about where to go or how to seek help, or other barriers), and 42% reported no perceived need for mental health services. In 2023, 29% of high school students reported they have ever lived with a parent or guardian who had severe depression, anxiety, or another mental illness, or was suicidal. This data was not available in 2025. When parental mental health is supported, so is youth mental health.

The YRBS also found that 31% of high school students had access to a loaded gun that is ready for them to fire without parental or adult permission or supervision. Promoting secure firearm storage through federal and state policies is a key AFSP advocacy priority, recognizing its potential to save lives by reducing access to firearms among youth experiencing a suicidal crisis.

Through efforts such as AFSP’s Advocacy Action Days, AFSP Volunteer Advocates across the nation will meet with state lawmakers and their staff from January through May to highlight the critical need for suicide prevention programs in K-12 schools, advocate for comprehensive crisis services, including the 988 Suicide & Crisis Lifeline, and promote lethal means safety policies as lifesaving measures. Strategies to Promote Mental Health and Wellness

To support the mental health and well-being of their adolescents, parents and families should take care of their own mental health and seek help for themselves or their adolescent, when needed. They can also consider:

Talking with their teen about mental health. Having frequent conversations can help build trust and make open and honest conversations easier.

Spending time engaging in shared activities to foster connection and help them build safe and stable relationships with you and other caring adults.

Providing developmentally appropriate supervision to facilitate healthy decision-making.

Schools can also play an important role in promoting students' mental health and well-being through education, prevention, and early intervention by creating safe and supportive environments, connecting students to caring adults, supporting positive peer relationships, linking students to mental health services, and helping them cope with emergencies.

Schools can consider implementing evidence-based strategies and practices that focus on:

Strengthening students' mental health literacy.

Increasing connectedness with peers and teachers.

Building skills for managing relationships, emotions, and challenges.

Teaching coping and problem-solving skills.

Training school staff to identify students exhibiting suicide-risk behaviors and make appropriate referrals.
