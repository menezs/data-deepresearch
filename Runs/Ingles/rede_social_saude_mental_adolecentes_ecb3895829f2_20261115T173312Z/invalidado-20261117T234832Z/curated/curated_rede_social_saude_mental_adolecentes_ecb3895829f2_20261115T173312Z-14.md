NEW YORK, Nov. 10, 2026 /PRNewswire/ -- From 2023-2025, U.S. high school students reported broad mental health improvements, with some indicators of distress showing historic declines in the 2025 Youth Risk Behavior Survey (YRBS) and Youth Health in Focus report from the CDC. The American Foundation for Suicide Prevention (AFSP), the largest private funder of suicide prevention research, recognizes these improvements as encouraging but cautions that distress rates remain high: 33% of high school students report experiencing persistent feelings of sadness or hopelessness and amongst these students, 29% reported an unmet need for counseling or therapy, with greater impacts for some groups by sex, race, and ethnicity. Additionally, the decrease in the student sample by nearly half (47%) from 20,103 students in 2023 to 10,710 in 2025 raises the question of whether these findings fully reflect the experiences of all U.S. high school students. In 2025, the question about transgender identity was eliminated from the survey despite evidence that transgender youth experience disproportionately greater suicide risk associated with stigma, discrimination, and other adverse experiences.

This data underscores the need to continue prioritizing youth mental health research, awareness, and advocacy and to strengthen public health, community, and interpersonal supports for all youth, particularly those disproportionately affected.

*What areas of youth mental health and suicide behavior are improving?*

From 2023 to 2025, all six mental health and suicide-related indicators were reported improved, some substantially. For example, students who reported persistent sadness or hopelessness declined from 40% to 33%, the largest two-year decrease since 1999. Those who reported making a suicide plan declined from 16% to 12% and those reporting suicide attempts declined from 9% to 6%, both are the largest two-year decreases since the survey's inception.

*Which youth stand out in their mental health and suicide-related outcomes?*

**Female Students**

In this 2025 survey, female students report higher levels of poor mental health, suicidal thoughts and behaviors than males, consistent with prior surveys. Notably, the greatest differences were seen in persistent feelings of sadness or hopelessness (43% females vs. 24% males) and poor mental health (31% females vs 16% males).

**November 10, 2026** 

From 2023-2025, U.S. high school students reported broad mental health improvements, with some indicators of distress showing historic declines in the 2025 Youth Risk Behavior Survey (YRBS) and Youth Health in Focus report from the CDC. The American Foundation for Suicide Prevention (AFSP), the largest private funder of suicide prevention research, recognizes these improvements as encouraging but cautions that distress rates remain high: 33% of high school students report experiencing persistent feelings of sadness or hopelessness and amongst these students, 29% reported an unmet need for counseling or therapy, with greater impacts for some groups by sex, race, and ethnicity. These findings underscore the need to continue prioritizing youth mental health research, awareness, and advocacy and to strengthen public health, community, and interpersonal supports for all youth, particularly those disproportionately affected. In 2025, the question about transgender identity was eliminated from the survey despite evidence that transgender youth experience disproportionately greater suicide risk associated with stigma, discrimination, and other adverse experiences.

The 2025 YRBS survey examined two- and 10-year trends. This nationally representative survey of U.S. high school students has run since 1991. The student sample decreased by nearly half (47%) from 20,103 students in 2023 to 10,710 in 2025 raising the question of whether these findings fully reflect the experiences of all U.S. high school students. Other federally funded studies support the findings.

**What areas of youth mental health and suicide behavior are improving?**

From 2023 to 2025, all six mental health and suicide-related indicators were reported improved:

- 
**Persistent sadness or hopelessness:** Declined from 40% to 33%, the largest two-year decrease since 1999

- 
**Poor mental health:** Declined from 29% to 23%

- 
**Seriously considered attempting suicide:** Declined from 20% to 14%

- 
**Made a suicide plan:** Declined from 16% to 12%, the largest two-year decrease since the survey’s inception

- 
**Suicide attempts:** Declined from 9% to 6%, the largest two-year decrease since the survey’s inception

- 
**Suicide attempts that required medical treatment:** Declined from 2% to 1%

**Native Hawaiian/Other Pacific Islander (NH/OPI) Students**

NH/OPI students saw the largest increases of adverse mental health and suicide related outcomes between 2023 and 2025. Persistent feelings of sadness or hopelessness rose from 26% to 41%, serious consideration of suicide rose from 16% to 31%, suicide planning rose from 11% to 28%, and suicide attempts rose from 15% to 22%.

**Black and Hispanic or Latino Students**

All four mental health and suicide-related indicators were reported improved for Black and Hispanic or Latino students from 2023 to 2025, consistent with high school students overall. Some indicators stand out. Black students who said they had made a suicide plan decreased from 16% in 2023 to 11% in 2025. Hispanic or Latino students who reported making a suicide attempt decreased from 11% in 2023 to 8% in 2025. Still, both groups reported higher rates of sadness or hopelessness and suicide attempts than White students.

*What helps protect youth mental health and prevent suicide?*

Protective factors support mental health and reduce suicide risk. The 2025 YRBS data reports improvements over 2023 with students feeling greater school connectedness and support from caring adults. For example, students reporting feeling closer to people at their school increased from 55% to 63%. Students reporting that an adult in their household tried hard to make sure their basic needs are met increased from 86% to 89%. Findings from the 2023 YRBS data showed that students reporting greater school connectedness were less likely to report suicidal thoughts, plans, and attempts, consistent with prior research.

*Did all schools participate in the YRBS survey?*

The YBRS overall response rate decreased from 60.3% in 2019 to 30.6% in 2025. The 2025 student sample included 10,710 students, compared with 20,103 in 2023. In 2025, 40 states participated; Alabama, Colorado, Florida, Georgia, Idaho, Iowa, Minnesota, Oregon, Washington, and Wyoming did not.

**Lesbian, Gay, Bisexual (LGB) Students** 

Lesbian, gay, and bisexual (LGB) youth continued to report higher rates of sadness and hopelessness, considering suicide, making a plan, and attempting suicide than their heterosexual counterparts. However, rates for these indicators also decreased among LGB high school students. In 2025, self-reported rates for LGB students considering suicide decreased from 48% in 2021to 32% in 2025, yet this rate remains high with one in three (32%) considering suicide compared to more than one in 10 (11%) heterosexual students. Sexual identity data has only been available since 2021. Data for transgender and non-binary youth, collected since 2019, is unavailable for 2025.

Most racial and ethnic groups saw substantial decreases in suicidal thoughts, plans, and attempts between 2023 and 2025. However, many disparities persist.

Reasons for nonparticipation vary and may include reliance on separate statewide youth surveys and misinformation about youth surveys and data use. The CDC’s Morbidity and Mortality Weekly Report supplements note that: “Nonpublic schools and schools below the median poverty level were less likely to participate, whereas schools in cities were more likely to participate.” Although the YRBS has been an important source of nationally representative data on youth mental health, its findings may not fully reflect the experiences of all U.S. high school students.

**What advocacy and policies are helping youth mental health and suicide prevention?**

This data informs AFSP’s research, advocacy, and public education efforts. As revealed in the YRBS, 29% of students who reported persistent feelings of sadness or hopelessness also reported an unmet need for mental health services (indicating they wanted help but were unable to access it due to cost, uncertainty about where to go or how to seek help, or other barriers), and 42% reported no perceived need for mental health services. In 2023, 29% of high school students reported they have ever lived with a parent or guardian who had severe depression, anxiety, or another mental illness, or was suicidal. This data was not available in 2025. When parental mental health is supported, so is youth mental health.

The YRBS also found that 31% of high school students had access to a loaded gun that is ready for them to fire without parental or adult permission or supervision. Promoting secure firearm storage through federal and state policies is a key AFSP advocacy priority, recognizing its potential to save lives by reducing access to firearms among youth experiencing a suicidal crisis.

Through efforts such as AFSP’s Advocacy Action Days, AFSP Volunteer Advocates across the nation will meet with state lawmakers and their staff from January through May to highlight the critical need for suicide prevention programs in K-12 schools, advocate for comprehensive crisis services, including the 988 Suicide & Crisis Lifeline, and promote lethal means safety policies as lifesaving measures. *What can I do to help support youth mental health and prevent suicide?*

AFSP and The Jed Foundation intend to merge in January 2027, and youth mental health and suicide prevention will continue to be a high priority.  While this report points to encouraging improvements across certain groups, progress is still needed so that youth mental health support is experienced equally across all groups. Take action! Sign up to become an AFSP Volunteer Advocate, join your local AFSP chapter, bring AFSP's *It's Real* or JED's high school programs to a school near you, learn about AFSP funded research and findings related to youth, and learn the suicide warning signs, risk factors, and protective factors.

The American Foundation for Suicide Prevention is dedicated to saving lives and bringing hope to those affected by suicide, including those who have experienced a loss. AFSP creates a culture that's smart about mental health through public education and community programs, develops suicide prevention through research and advocacy, and provides support for those affected by suicide. Led by CEO Robert Gebbia and headquartered in New York, with its Policy and Advocacy Office in Washington, D.C., AFSP has local chapters in all 50 states, D.C., and Puerto Rico, with programs and events nationwide. Learn more about AFSP in its latest Annual Report and join the conversation on suicide prevention by following AFSP on Facebook, X, Instagram, YouTube, LinkedIn and TikTok.

The American Foundation for Suicide Prevention and The Jed Foundation (JED) intend to merge as equals to form AFSP/JED, the nation's largest nonprofit dedicated to preventing suicide across the lifespan, and bringing hope to those affected by suicide loss or risk. The AFSP/JED mission would reflect a commitment to bring together AFSP's research, advocacy, chapter network, and loss-support expertise with JED's comprehensive approach to suicide prevention and evidence-informed youth mental health programs, creating greater scale, reach, and impact at a time of urgent national need.
