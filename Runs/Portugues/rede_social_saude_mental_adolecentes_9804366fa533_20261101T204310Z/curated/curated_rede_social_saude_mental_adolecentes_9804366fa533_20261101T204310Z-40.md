A 2025 Pew Research Center study of 13- to 17-year-olds and their parents reveals increasingly complex views about social media and mental health. Although 74% of teens report that social media helps them stay connected to friends, nearly half (48%) now say these platforms negatively affect people their age—up from 32% in 2022. Yet, only 14% believe it harms their mental health. This growing divergence highlights the urgent need for clinicians to begin assessing social media harm in routine adolescent mental health evaluations.

## Key Findings for Behavioral Health Clinicians:

- Rising numbers of parents (55%) and teens (35%) report serious concerns about adolescent mental health. Girls, in particular, are more likely to report social media harm—25% say it negatively affects their mental health versus 14% of boys.
- While many teens perceive their peers as being harmed by social media, most perceive themselves as unaffected—underscoring a potential barrier to accurate self-assessment and highlighting the importance of dual-perspective social media harm evaluation.
- Teen girls report more social media-induced harm (e.g., to sleep, confidence, mood). Black teens are more likely than White and Hispanic peers to say social media gives them a strong sense of support and acceptance.
- 34% of teens get mental health information from social media. Among these, 63% consider it an essential source. TikTok and other platforms often blur the line between peer advice and professional guidance, raising both opportunities and ethical concerns when evaluating the harm caused by social media.
- 80% of parents say they’re very comfortable discussing mental health with their children, but only 52% of teens feel the same. Teens are more likely to open up to friends than to therapists or teachers.
- Despite concerns, 63% of teens say social media supports their creativity and 74% report that it helps them feel connected to their friends. However, 39% feel overwhelmed by online drama, and 27% say social media makes them feel worse about their lives.
- 45% of teens now say they spend too much time on social media—up from 36% in 2022. Nearly half say they’ve attempted to cut back. Girls are more likely than boys to report trying to reduce usage.

### Expand Confidential Dialogue Options

Recognize that teens may be more willing to speak to a therapist than a parent or teacher if the right trust is established. Building this trust is crucial for understanding the full extent of social media harm.

#### Sample Questions:

1. Who do you feel safest talking to about emotional struggles?
2. Are there things you don’t feel comfortable telling your parents about your online experiences?
3. What would make it easier for you to open up to someone about your mental health?
4. Have you ever talked with a therapist before?
5. Would you prefer texting or chatting online with a therapist?
6. How would you feel if your social media use came up in a conversation?
7. What would help you trust a mental health provider more easily?

### Monitor Screen Time Without Shame

Support behavior change in tech use without moralizing. Emphasize function (e.g., sleep, productivity, grades) over abstract notions of “addiction.” Focus on identifying and managing early signs of social media harm.

#### Sample Questions:

1. How does screen time affect your sleep or ability to get things done?
2. Do you feel distracted or tired after spending time online?
3. Have you noticed any mood changes after using social media?
4. Do you feel in control of your time online?
5. What would you do more of if you spent less time online?
6. Have you tried setting limits on app usage?
7. How do you feel when you’re not on social media for a while?

## Implications for Clinical Practice: Assessing Social Media Harm

### Conduct Dual-Level Assessments

Ask about both personal experiences and perceptions of peers to get a fuller picture of the teen’s social media environment. Focus on identifying signs of social media harm both in the individual and their peer group.

#### Sample Questions:

1. How much time do you spend on social media each day, on average?
2. What kind of posts or content do you usually engage with?
3. Have you ever felt worse about yourself after being on social media?
4. Do you think social media helps or hurts your friends’ mental health?
5. What do your friends say about their experiences online?
6. Have you ever seen posts that made you worry about a friend’s safety or mental state?
7. Do you feel pressure to keep up with what your friends post?

### Use Gender-Sensitive Protocols

Be alert to the higher rates of social media harm reported by girls and tailor intervention strategies accordingly.

#### Sample Questions:

1. Have you ever felt excluded because of something you saw online?
2. How often do you feel pressure to post something others will like?
3. Do you feel judged based on appearance or lifestyle in posts?
4. Have you experienced online bullying or subtle exclusion?
5. Does social media affect your sleep?
6. What kinds of content make you feel uncomfortable?
7. Do you think social media is more stressful for girls or boys?

### Integrate Digital Literacy Tools

Equip teens with strategies to critically evaluate online mental health content and mitigate social media harm.

#### Sample Questions:

1. Have you seen mental health advice on platforms like TikTok or Instagram?
2. How do you decide if the content you see online is trustworthy?
3. Do you follow any therapists or influencers who talk about mental health?
4. Have you tried something you saw online to feel better?
5. Can you tell the difference between a professional opinion and a personal story online?
6. Do you know how to find credible mental health resources online?
7. Would you be interested in learning how to evaluate sources more effectively?
